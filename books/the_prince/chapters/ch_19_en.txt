Chapter 19
That One Should Avoid Being Despised And Hated

I have already discussed the most important characteristics a prince should have. Now I want to briefly cover the rest under one general rule. As I mentioned before, a prince must carefully avoid anything that makes him hated or despised. If he succeeds in this, he has done his part. He will not face any danger from other criticisms.

Above all, a prince makes himself hated by being greedy and by taking the property and women of his subjects. He must keep his hands off both. As long as you do not touch the property or honor of the majority of men, they will live happily. Then you only have to deal with the ambition of a few, which you can easily crush in many ways.

A prince makes himself despised if he is seen as unpredictable, frivolous, weak, cowardly, or indecisive. He must guard against these flaws as if they were a dangerous reef. Instead, he must ensure his actions show greatness, courage, seriousness, and strength. When settling disputes among his subjects, his decisions must be final. He must build such a strong reputation that no one even dreams of tricking or manipulating him.

A prince who creates this impression is highly respected, and it is very hard to conspire against a man who is highly respected. As long as he is known as an excellent leader who is admired by his people, enemies will find it difficult to attack him. A prince only has two real fears. One comes from inside, from his own subjects. The other comes from outside, from foreign powers. He can defend himself against foreign powers by having a strong army and good allies. If his army is strong, he will always have good allies. And as long as external affairs remain peaceful, internal affairs will remain peaceful, unless a conspiracy has already started. Even if a foreign power does attack, a well-prepared prince who follows my advice and never gives up will easily fight off every assault, just like Nabis the Spartan did.

But when it comes to his subjects, if external affairs are peaceful, his only fear is a secret conspiracy. A prince can easily protect himself from this by making sure he is neither hated nor despised, and by keeping the people satisfied. As I explained at length earlier, keeping the people satisfied is absolutely necessary. In fact, one of the best defenses against conspiracies is simply not being hated or despised by the public. A conspirator always believes that killing the prince will please the people. But if the conspirator thinks the assassination will anger the people, he will never have the courage to go through with it. The obstacles facing a conspirator are already massive. Experience shows that there have been many conspiracies, but very few have succeeded. A conspirator cannot act alone, and he can only recruit people he thinks are unhappy. But the moment you share your plot with an unhappy man, you give him exactly what he needs to be happy: the chance to betray you for a reward. When he sees guaranteed profit in betraying you, and nothing but deadly risk in joining you, he would have to be an incredibly loyal friend, or an insanely stubborn enemy of the prince, to keep your secret.

To put it simply, the conspirator lives with nothing but fear, paranoia, and the terrifying prospect of execution. The prince, on the other hand, has the majesty of his office, the laws, his allies, and the entire state protecting him. Add the goodwill of the people to all that, and it becomes almost impossible for anyone to be foolish enough to conspire against him. Usually, a conspirator only has to fear the danger of carrying out the plot. But when he is up against the people's goodwill, he must also fear what happens after the crime. The people will become his enemy, and he will have absolutely no hope of escape.

I could give endless examples of this, but I will stick to one that happened during our fathers' time. Lord Annibale Bentivogli, the prince of Bologna and grandfather of the current Annibale, was murdered by the Canneschi family, who had conspired against him. The only member of the Bentivogli family left was Giovanni, who was just a baby. But immediately after the assassination, the people rose up and slaughtered every single member of the Canneschi family. This happened because the house of Bentivogli enjoyed massive popular goodwill in Bologna at the time. The goodwill was so strong that even though there was no one left in Bologna to rule the state after Annibale's death, the citizens took drastic action. They heard that there was a distant relative of the Bentivogli family in Florence, a man who had lived his whole life believing he was the son of a blacksmith. The people of Bologna went all the way to Florence, brought him back, and handed him control of their city. He ruled until Giovanni was old enough to take over.

Therefore, a prince should not worry much about conspiracies if his people respect him. But if the people are hostile and hate him, he must fear everything and everyone. Well-organized states and wise princes always make sure not to push the nobles to desperation, while also keeping the common people satisfied and happy. This is one of the most important jobs a prince has.

France is one of the best organized and governed kingdoms of our time. It has many excellent institutions that protect the king's freedom and security. The most important of these is the parliament and its authority. The founder of the French kingdom knew how ambitious and arrogant the nobility could be, and he realized they needed a bit in their mouths to restrain them. At the same time, he knew the common people hated the nobles out of fear, and he wanted to protect the people. But he did not want the king to carry the burden of this conflict. If the king favored the people, the nobles would blame him. If he favored the nobles, the people would blame him. So, the founder created an independent judge: the parliament. This judge could crush the powerful nobles and protect the common people, all without the king taking any blame. There could not be a better, wiser, or safer system for the king and his kingdom. From this, we can draw a crucial rule: princes should delegate unpopular duties to others and keep the popular, gracious duties for themselves. Again, a prince must respect his nobles, but he must never let the common people hate him.

Some people who have studied the lives and deaths of the Roman emperors might think they prove my theory wrong. Many of these emperors lived noble lives and showed great strength of character, yet they still lost their empire or were murdered by their own subjects. To answer these arguments, I will examine the characters of a few emperors and show that their ruins were caused by exactly the things I have described. I will focus on the most important details for anyone studying that era.

It is enough to look at the emperors who took the throne from the philosopher Marcus Aurelius down to Maximinus. These men were Marcus, his son Commodus, Pertinax, Julian, Severus, his son Antoninus Caracalla, Macrinus, Heliogabalus, Alexander, and Maximinus.

First, you must understand a unique problem these Roman emperors faced. In normal principalities, a ruler only has to manage the ambition of the nobles and the arrogance of the people. But the Roman emperors had a third, incredibly difficult problem: they had to put up with the cruelty and greed of their own soldiers. This balancing act was so hard that it destroyed many emperors. It was nearly impossible to satisfy both the soldiers and the people at the same time. The people loved peace, so they preferred a quiet, modest prince. But the soldiers loved a warlike prince who was bold, cruel, and greedy. The soldiers wanted the prince to unleash those brutal qualities on the people so they could double their pay and satisfy their own greed and cruelty. 

Because of this, any emperor who lacked the sheer authority to control both groups—whether by birth or by reputation—was always overthrown. Most of them, especially the newly crowned emperors who saw the impossible conflict between these two factions, chose to satisfy the soldiers and ignored the suffering of the people. This choice was necessary. Since a prince cannot avoid being hated by someone, he must first try to avoid being hated by everyone. If that is impossible, he must do whatever it takes to avoid the hatred of the most powerful group. Therefore, inexperienced emperors who desperately needed support naturally sided with the powerful soldiers over the people. Whether this strategy worked depended entirely on how well the emperor maintained his authority over the army.

This impossible dynamic explains why Marcus, Pertinax, and Alexander all came to tragic ends, even though they lived modest lives, loved justice, hated cruelty, and treated people with kindness. Marcus was the only exception. He lived and died highly honored because he inherited the empire by birthright. He owed his power to neither the soldiers nor the people. Furthermore, he possessed many brilliant virtues that commanded immense respect. Because of this, he successfully kept both factions in line throughout his entire life, and he was never hated or despised.

Pertinax, on the other hand, was made emperor against the soldiers' wishes. The army had grown used to living wildly under Commodus, and they refused to accept the honest, disciplined life Pertinax tried to force on them. This made the soldiers hate him. Adding to their hatred was contempt for his old age. As a result, Pertinax was overthrown at the very beginning of his rule. You must note here that a prince can earn hatred just as easily by doing good deeds as by doing bad ones. Therefore, as I said before, a prince who wants to keep his state is very often forced to do evil. If the group you rely on to stay in power—whether it is the people, the soldiers, or the nobles—is corrupt, you have to stoop to their level and indulge them. When that happens, doing good deeds will only destroy you.

Now consider Alexander, a man of such incredible goodness that during his fourteen years in power, he never put a single person to death without a trial. Yet he was seen as weak and feminine, a man controlled by his mother. He became utterly despised. The army conspired against him and slaughtered him.

Now look at the opposite extremes: Commodus, Severus, Antoninus Caracalla, and Maximinus. You will find them all to be cruel and greedy men. To satisfy their soldiers, they did not hesitate to commit every horrifying crime imaginable against the people. All of them, except Severus, came to brutal ends. Severus had so much Virtù and ability that, by keeping the soldiers loyal, he ruled successfully even while crushing the people. His sheer ability made both the soldiers and the people admire him. The people were left stunned and terrified, while the soldiers remained respectful and satisfied. Because Severus achieved such massive success as a new prince, I want to briefly show how brilliantly he played both the fox and the lion—the two natures every prince must imitate.

Severus knew the Emperor Julian was lazy and weak. So Severus convinced his army in Sclavonia, which he commanded, that they needed to march on Rome to avenge the death of Pertinax, who had been killed by the Praetorian guards. Using this excuse, without ever revealing his ambition for the throne, he marched his army toward Rome. He reached Italy before anyone even knew he had left. When he arrived in Rome, the terrified Senate elected him emperor and executed Julian. After this, Severus wanted to take total control of the empire, but he faced two major threats. In Asia, a commander named Niger had declared himself emperor. In the west, Albinus was also planning to take the throne. Severus knew it was too dangerous to fight both of them at once. He decided to openly attack Niger while tricking Albinus. He wrote a friendly letter to Albinus, claiming the Senate had elected him emperor and that he wanted to share the power. Severus even sent Albinus the title of Caesar and named him co-ruler. Albinus completely fell for it. But after Severus defeated and killed Niger and secured the east, he returned to Rome and complained to the Senate. He claimed Albinus was ungrateful, treacherous, and had tried to murder him. Severus used this as an excuse to hunt Albinus down in France, where he stripped him of his power and his life. If you study Severus carefully, you will see he was a fiercely violent lion and a brilliantly cunning fox. He was feared and respected by everyone, and his army never hated him. It is no wonder this new man held his massive empire so effectively. His legendary reputation protected him from the intense hatred the people normally would have felt toward his violence.

His son Antoninus Caracalla was also a prominent man with excellent qualities. The people admired him, and the soldiers accepted him. He was a ruthless warrior who could endure endless hardship and despised luxury, which made the armies love him. However, his savagery and cruelty were completely unheard of. After committing countless individual murders, he slaughtered a massive portion of the population of Rome and all the people of Alexandria. The entire world hated him, and even those in his inner circle feared him. Eventually, he was murdered by a centurion right in the middle of his own army. You must note that a prince cannot avoid assassinations carried out by a desperate, fearless individual who does not care if he dies. Anyone who has no fear of death can strike the fatal blow. But a prince should not worry about these attacks too much because they are incredibly rare. He simply must be careful not to deeply offend anyone in his inner circle or state service. Antoninus failed to take this basic precaution. He had violently humiliated and murdered the centurion's brother, and he threatened the centurion every single day. Yet, insanely, he kept this very man in his personal bodyguard. It was a reckless move, and it destroyed him.

Now let us look at Commodus. He had the easiest path to keeping the empire because he inherited it from his father, Marcus. All he had to do was follow his father's footsteps to keep both the people and the soldiers happy. But Commodus was naturally vicious and brutal. To unleash his greed on the people, he pampered and corrupted the army. At the same time, he completely destroyed his own dignity. He constantly went down into the arenas to fight gladiators and engaged in vile, disgusting behavior completely beneath an emperor. The soldiers quickly grew to despise him. Hated by the people and despised by the army, a conspiracy formed, and he was assassinated.

Finally, we have Maximinus. He was a brutal, warlike man. When the armies got sick of Alexander's weakness, they murdered him and elected Maximinus to the throne. But Maximinus did not last long. Two things made him totally hated and despised. First, he came from a pathetic background—he used to be a sheep herder in Thrace. Everyone knew this, and they considered it a massive humiliation. Second, when he was elected, he delayed going to Rome to take the imperial throne. Instead, he gained a terrifying reputation for extreme cruelty by letting his deputies terrorize Rome and the rest of the empire. The entire world raged against his low birth and trembled at his savagery. Africa rebelled first, followed by the Senate, the people of Rome, and all of Italy. Even his own army turned against him. While struggling to besiege the city of Aquileia, his soldiers grew disgusted by his cruelty. Realizing he had too many enemies to fight back, they lost their fear of him and murdered him.

I will not bother discussing Heliogabalus, Macrinus, or Julian. They were utterly pathetic and quickly wiped out. I will conclude by saying that princes today do not face this extreme problem of perfectly satisfying their armies. Modern princes have to indulge their soldiers a little, but the problem is easily managed. None of today's princes have standing armies deeply entrenched in the government and administration of the provinces, like the Roman armies were. Back then, it was more important to satisfy the soldiers than the people. Today, except for the Turkish Sultan and the Egyptian Sultan, it is much more important for every prince to satisfy the people, simply because the people are now more powerful.

I exclude the Turkish Sultan because he always keeps twelve thousand infantrymen and fifteen thousand cavalrymen at his side. The total security and strength of his kingdom depend on them. He must prioritize keeping their loyalty above any concern for the people. The Egyptian Sultan is similar. His kingdom is completely controlled by the military, so he must also secure their loyalty before worrying about the people. It is important to note that the Egyptian Sultan's state is totally unique. It operates a lot like the Christian Papacy. It cannot be called a hereditary principality, nor is it a newly formed one. When the old ruler dies, his sons do not inherit the throne. Instead, the new ruler is elected by a council of authorities, and the previous ruler's sons remain mere noblemen. Because this is an ancient tradition, it does not suffer from the same problems as a brand-new principality. Even though the ruler is new, the foundation of the state is old and perfectly designed to welcome him exactly as if he were a hereditary king.

Returning to our main subject, anyone who thinks about this will realize that the Roman emperors were destroyed by either hatred or contempt. You will also understand how, even though these rulers took totally different approaches, only one in each camp found a happy end, while the rest were ruined. It would have been useless and dangerous for Pertinax and Alexander, as new princes, to copy Marcus, who was born into power. Likewise, it was fatal for Caracalla, Commodus, and Maximinus to copy Severus, because they simply lacked the absolute Virtù and talent to pull off his brutal methods. Therefore, a new prince cannot act like Marcus, nor does he have to act like Severus. Instead, he must take from Severus the ruthless tactics necessary to establish a new state, and take from Marcus the glorious, just methods needed to maintain a state that is already stable and secure.
