Chapter 8
Concerning Those Who Have Obtained a Principality by Wickedness

A man can rise from private life to become a prince in two other ways that cannot be entirely credited to fortune or ability. I must discuss them here, though the second way could be explored more fully in a discussion about republics. The first method is rising to power through wicked and ruthless actions. The second is when a private citizen becomes prince through the favor of his fellow citizens. I will explain the first method using two examples, one ancient and one modern. Without diving deeper into the theory, these two examples will be enough for anyone forced to follow their path.

Agathocles the Sicilian became King of Syracuse despite starting from a shockingly low and poor background. He was the son of a potter and lived a brutal, criminal life through every stage of his career. However, he combined his wickedness with such immense physical and mental ability that he joined the military and climbed the ranks to become the Praetor of Syracuse. Once securely in that position, he decided to make himself prince. He resolved to use absolute violence to take what the people had willingly granted him, owing nothing to anyone. To do this, he conspired with Hamilcar the Carthaginian, who was fighting in Sicily with his army. One morning, Agathocles called the people and the senate of Syracuse together, pretending he needed to discuss matters of the republic. At a given signal, his soldiers murdered all the senators and the wealthiest citizens. With them dead, he seized control of the city and ruled without any resistance from the public. Even though the Carthaginians defeated him twice and eventually placed Syracuse under siege, Agathocles did not just defend his city. He left some of his troops to hold the walls, crossed the sea with the rest, and attacked Africa. This bold move quickly broke the siege of Syracuse. The Carthaginians were pushed to the brink of ruin. They were forced to make peace with Agathocles, settling for control of Africa and leaving Sicily entirely to him.

If you examine this man's actions and abilities, you will find very little that can be credited to fortune or luck. As shown above, he did not rely on anyone's favor to gain power. Instead, he rose through the military ranks step by step, enduring a thousand hardships and deadly risks. Once in power, he held it through bold and dangerous actions. Yet, we cannot call it true virtue or talent to slaughter fellow citizens, betray friends, and live without loyalty, mercy, or religion. These brutal methods can win a man an empire, but they will never win him glory. Still, if you consider Agathocles' courage in facing and escaping danger, along with his mental toughness in enduring and overcoming severe hardships, he stands as tall as any great military commander. Nevertheless, his savage cruelty, his complete lack of humanity, and his endless crimes prevent him from being celebrated among the truly excellent men. In the end, his massive achievements cannot be attributed to either fortune or true virtue.

In our own times, during the reign of Pope Alexander the Sixth, there was Oliverotto da Fermo. He was orphaned at a young age and raised by his uncle, Giovanni Fogliani. In his early youth, he was sent to fight under the commander Paolo Vitelli. The goal was for Oliverotto to learn military discipline and achieve a high rank in the army. After Paolo died, Oliverotto fought under Paolo's brother, Vitellozzo. Blessed with intelligence and a strong body and mind, he quickly became the top soldier in his unit. However, he felt it was beneath him to take orders from others. He decided to seize control of his home city, Fermo. He enlisted the help of the Vitelli family and a few local citizens who preferred the enslavement of their city over its freedom. Oliverotto wrote to his uncle Giovanni, saying he had been away for many years and wanted to visit him and see his family estate. He claimed he had fought only for honor. To prove to the citizens of Fermo that his time had not been wasted, he wanted to arrive in a grand fashion. He announced he would bring one hundred armed horsemen, consisting of his friends and loyal followers. He begged his uncle to ensure the city welcomed him with great respect. He argued this grand reception would honor not only him, but also Giovanni, the man who had raised him.

Giovanni did everything he could for his nephew. He organized a grand welcome from the citizens of Fermo and hosted Oliverotto in his own home. After a few days of setting his sinister trap, Oliverotto threw a lavish banquet. He invited his uncle Giovanni and all the leading men of Fermo. Once the food and entertainment were finished, Oliverotto deliberately steered the conversation toward serious political topics. He spoke about the greatness of Pope Alexander and his ruthless son, Cesare Borgia. When Giovanni and the others began to reply, Oliverotto abruptly stood up. He declared that these sensitive matters needed to be discussed in a more private room. He walked into another chamber, and Giovanni and the city leaders followed him. As soon as they sat down, armed soldiers burst from their hiding spots and slaughtered Giovanni and every last one of the guests. Following this massacre, Oliverotto leaped onto his horse, rode through the town, and besieged the chief magistrate in the palace. Terrified, the people were forced to obey him and establish a new government with Oliverotto as their prince. He systematically murdered anyone who opposed him and could threaten his rule. He enforced strict new civil and military laws. Within a single year in power, he made himself absolutely secure in Fermo and became a terrifying threat to all his neighbors. Taking him down would have been just as difficult as destroying Agathocles. However, Oliverotto allowed himself to be tricked by Cesare Borgia. As I mentioned earlier, Borgia captured him along with the Orsini and Vitelli leaders at Senigallia. Exactly one year after murdering his own uncle, Oliverotto was strangled to death alongside his mentor in courage and crime, Vitellozzo.

You might wonder how someone like Agathocles could commit endless acts of treachery and unspeakable cruelty, yet live securely in his own country for years. He defended himself against foreign enemies and never faced a rebellion from his own citizens. By contrast, many other rulers have tried to use cruelty but failed to hold onto power even in peacetime, let alone during war. I believe this difference comes down to whether cruelty is used poorly or properly. Cruelty is used properly, if we can ever speak well of evil, when it is executed entirely in a single, swift stroke to secure your power. After that initial blow, a smart ruler stops the bloodshed and focuses on doing what benefits his subjects. Cruelty is used poorly when it starts small but grows and multiplies over time instead of stopping. A prince who uses the first method can eventually stabilize his rule with the help of God and men, just like Agathocles did. But it is absolutely impossible for a prince who uses the second method to stay in power.

Therefore, when seizing a state, the conqueror must carefully calculate all the harsh violence he needs to inflict. He must execute all of these cruelties at once so he does not have to repeat them every day. By ending the violence quickly, he can calm the people and eventually win them over with generous benefits. A ruler who fails to do this, whether out of cowardice or bad advice, will be forced to keep a bloody knife in his hand forever. He will never be able to trust his subjects, and they will never feel safe with him because of the constant, repeated abuses. All injuries must be inflicted in one brutal strike, so they are felt less and resented less over time. On the other hand, benefits should be handed out slowly, drop by drop, so the people can savor them longer.

Above all, a prince must live among his people in a steady, consistent way. No unexpected turn of events, whether good or bad, should force him to change his behavior. If hard times strike and force your hand, it will be too late to use harsh measures. At the same time, any sudden acts of kindness will not help you either. The people will see your generosity as desperate and forced, and you will earn exactly zero gratitude for it.
