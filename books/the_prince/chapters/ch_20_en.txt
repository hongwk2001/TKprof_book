Chapter 20
Are Fortresses, And Many Other Things To Which Princes Often Resort, Advantageous Or Hurtful?

1. To secure their hold on power, some princes have disarmed their subjects. Others have kept their subject towns divided by factions. Some have deliberately provoked enemies against themselves, while others have worked to win over those they initially distrusted. Some princes have built fortresses; others have torn them down and destroyed them. You cannot make a final judgment on all these methods without knowing the specific details of the states where these decisions were made. Even so, I will speak as broadly about this as the subject allows.

2. No new prince has ever disarmed his subjects. In fact, when he finds them unarmed, he always arms them. By arming them, those weapons become yours. The men you previously distrusted become loyal, while those who were already loyal stay that way, and your subjects become your active supporters. You cannot possibly arm everyone. However, by providing benefits to those you do arm, you can handle the rest of the population more easily. The armed men become indebted to you because of their special treatment. The unarmed men will understand and excuse this difference, recognizing that those who face the most danger and provide the greatest service deserve the greatest rewards. But the moment you disarm your subjects, you offend them. You show that you distrust them, suspecting them of either cowardice or disloyalty, and both of these opinions will breed hatred against you. Since you cannot remain unarmed, you will be forced to rely on mercenaries. I have already discussed the poor character of mercenary troops. Even if they were good, they would never be enough to defend you against powerful enemies and a distrustful population. Therefore, a new prince in a new principality has always distributed weapons. History is full of such examples. However, things change when a prince acquires a new state and adds it as a province to his existing territory. In that case, you must disarm the men of that new state, except for those who supported you in taking it. Even those supporters, over time and when the opportunity arises, should be made soft and weak. You must manage things so that all the armed men in the entire state are your own soldiers, the ones who lived near you and served you in your original territory.

3. Our ancestors, and those considered wise, used to say it was necessary to hold Pistoia through factions and Pisa through fortresses. With this in mind, they encouraged quarrels in some of their subject towns to control them more easily. This might have worked well enough in those times when Italy was relatively stable and balanced. I do not believe, however, that this can be used as a rule today. I do not think factions are ever useful. In fact, it is certain that when an enemy attacks divided cities, you will quickly lose them. The weaker faction will always side with the invading forces, and the remaining faction will not be strong enough to resist. The Venetians likely followed this logic when they encouraged the Guelph and Ghibelline factions in their subject cities. Although they never let the disputes escalate to bloodshed, they actively fed these conflicts so the citizens would stay distracted by their differences and never unite against Venice. As we saw, this did not turn out as expected. After the Venetian defeat at Vaila, one of those factions immediately gathered courage and seized control of the state. These methods only reveal a prince's weakness. A strong, vigorous principality will never allow such factions to exist. Dividing your subjects might make them easier to manage during times of peace, but the moment war breaks out, this strategy is proven completely useless.

4. Without a doubt, princes achieve greatness when they overcome the difficulties and obstacles placed in their way. Therefore, when Fortuna wants to make a new prince great, she forces enemies to rise up and plot against him. A new prince needs to build his reputation much more than a hereditary one. By giving him enemies, Fortuna gives him the chance to defeat them. He can then use the very ladder his enemies built to climb to even greater heights. For this reason, many believe that a wise prince should carefully and secretly encourage some hostility against himself when the opportunity arises. By crushing this manufactured opposition, his fame and power will only grow stronger.

5. Princes, especially new ones, have often found more loyalty and real help from the men they initially distrusted than from the men they trusted from the start. Pandolfo Petrucci, the Prince of Siena, ruled his state largely by relying on those he had once suspected. However, you cannot make a broad generalization about this, because it varies depending on the specific people involved. I will only say this: if the men who were hostile at the beginning of your reign need your support to maintain their own positions, you can win them over with incredible ease. They will be forced to serve you with absolute loyalty because they know they must work hard to erase your bad impression of them through their actions. As a result, a prince always gets more value out of these former enemies than from those who serve him with too much security and end up neglecting his affairs. Since the subject requires it, I must warn any prince who has acquired a new state through the secret help of its citizens. You must carefully consider why those people helped you. If they were not motivated by a natural affection for you, but simply by hatred for their previous government, you will have a very difficult time keeping them friendly. It will be completely impossible to satisfy them. If you carefully weigh the reasons for this, looking at examples from both ancient and modern history, you will find a clear truth. It is much easier to make friends with the men who were perfectly happy under the old government, and who are therefore your natural enemies, than it is to keep the friendship of the malcontents who helped you seize power.

6. Princes have often built fortresses to hold their states more securely. These fortresses act as a bit and bridle to control anyone who might plot against them, while also providing a safe refuge from a sudden attack. I praise this method because it has been used since ancient times. Even so, in our own time, Niccolò Vitelli demolished two fortresses in Città di Castello just so he could keep control of that state. When Guido Ubaldo, the Duke of Urbino, returned to his territory after being driven out by Cesare Borgia, he completely leveled all the fortresses in that province. He decided that without them, it would be much harder to lose his state again. The Bentivogli family came to the exact same conclusion when they returned to Bologna. Therefore, fortresses are only useful depending on the circumstances. If they help you in one way, they will hurt you in another. You can break the issue down like this: a prince who fears his own people more than he fears foreigners should build fortresses. However, a prince who fears foreigners more than his own people should leave them alone. The castle of Milan, built by Francesco Sforza, has caused and will continue to cause more trouble for the Sforza family than any other source of disorder in the state. For this reason, the best possible fortress you can have is simply not being hated by your people. Even if you have the strongest fortresses, they will not save you if the people hate you. Once the people take up arms against you, there will never be a shortage of foreigners ready to step in and help them. In our times, we have not seen fortresses benefit a single prince, with the one exception of the Countess of Forlì after her husband, Count Girolamo, was killed. Her fortress allowed her to survive the attack of the people and wait safely for backup from Milan to recover her state. At that specific time, the political situation meant no foreigners were available to assist the rebelling people. But those same fortresses were entirely useless to her later on when Cesare Borgia attacked her, and her people, who hated her, joined forces with the foreign invaders. Therefore, both then and before, it would have been much safer for her to avoid being hated by the people than to rely on fortresses. Considering all of this, I will praise the prince who builds fortresses just as much as the one who does not. But I will fiercely blame any prince who places his trust entirely in fortresses and does not care at all if his people hate him.

This historical note refers to Catherine Sforza, the daughter of Galeazzo Sforza and Lucrezia Landriani. It was to the Countess of Forlì that Machiavelli himself was sent as an envoy in 1499. A letter from Fortunati to the countess announced the appointment, stating that he had met with the lords to find out who would be sent. They told him that Niccolò Machiavelli, a learned young Florentine nobleman and secretary to the Lords of the Ten, was to leave with him immediately.
