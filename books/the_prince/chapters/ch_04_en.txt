Chapter 4
Why The Kingdom Of Darius, Conquered By Alexander, Did Not Rebel Against The Successors Of Alexander At His Death

Considering how hard it is to hold onto a newly acquired state, some might wonder about Alexander the Great. He became the master of Asia in just a few years and died before the empire was fully settled. You would expect the entire empire to rebel. Yet, his successors kept control of it. They faced no difficulties other than those caused by their own clashing ambitions.

I answer this by pointing out that all recorded principalities are governed in one of two ways. The first is by a single prince, aided by ministers who help him govern solely through his favor and permission. The second is by a prince and his barons. These barons hold their noble rank not by the ruler's grace, but through their ancient bloodlines. Such barons have their own territories and subjects. These subjects recognize the barons as their lords and feel a natural affection for them. In states governed by a prince and his appointed ministers, the prince holds much more authority. Throughout the entire country, no one is recognized as his superior. If the people obey anyone else, they do so only because that person is a government official, not because they feel any particular love for him.

Good modern examples of these two systems are the Turkish ruler and the King of France. The entire Turkish monarchy is governed by one single lord, and everyone else is his servant. He divides his kingdom into districts and sends different administrators to run them, shifting and replacing these officials exactly as he pleases. The King of France, on the other hand, is surrounded by an ancient group of lords. These lords are recognized and loved by their own subjects. They have their own historical privileges, and the king cannot take those privileges away without putting himself in danger. If you look at both states, you will see that it is extremely difficult to conquer the Turkish state. However, once you have conquered it, it is very easy to hold. It is hard to seize the Turkish kingdom because an invader cannot be invited in by local princes. You also cannot rely on the ruler's inner circle to rebel and make things easier for you. This is because all his ministers are essentially slaves bound to him. They are very difficult to corrupt. Even if you do bribe them, they are useless because they cannot drag the common people into a rebellion with them. Therefore, anyone who attacks the Turkish empire must expect to find it completely united. You will have to rely purely on your own military strength rather than hoping for internal uprisings. But once the ruler is conquered and his armies are decisively crushed on the battlefield, the only remaining threat is the prince's own bloodline. Once you exterminate his family, there is no one left to fear. The remaining officials have no influence over the people. Just as the conqueror could not rely on them before the victory, he has absolutely no reason to fear them afterward.

The exact opposite happens in kingdoms governed like France. You can easily invade by winning over some ambitious baron within the kingdom, since you will always find locals who are unhappy and want a change of government. These men can open the gates to the state and make your initial victory incredibly easy. But if you want to hold onto the country afterward, you will face endless difficulties. These problems will come both from the people who helped you and from the people you crushed. It is not enough to just exterminate the family of the prince. The remaining lords will simply make themselves the leaders of fresh rebellions against you. Because you can neither keep them satisfied nor completely wipe them out, you will lose the state the moment they get an opportunity.

Now, if you consider the nature of Darius's government, you will find it was very similar to the Turkish kingdom. Because of this, Alexander the Great only needed to crush Darius on the battlefield and strip the country from him. After this total victory and the death of Darius, the state remained secure for Alexander, for all the reasons I just explained. If his successors had stayed united, they could have enjoyed this vast empire securely and in total peace. In fact, there were no uprisings in the kingdom other than the ones they carelessly provoked themselves.

But it is impossible to hold a state structured like France with that same level of peace. This is why there were frequent rebellions against the Romans in Spain, France, and Greece. Those regions were filled with numerous smaller principalities. As long as the local memory of those former rulers survived, the Roman grip on these territories was highly insecure. It was only through the overwhelming power and long duration of the Roman Empire that the memory of those old rulers finally faded away, allowing the Romans to become secure possessors of the land. Later, when the Romans began fighting civil wars among themselves, each Roman general was easily able to rally different parts of the country to his side, based on the local authority he had established there. Since the families of the original ancient lords had been completely wiped out, the locals recognized no authority other than the Romans.

When you remember all of these factors, no one should be surprised at how easily Alexander held the Empire of Asia. Likewise, no one should wonder why leaders like Pyrrhus and many others struggled so much to keep the lands they conquered. This difference does not come from the conqueror having too much or too little ability. It comes entirely from the different political structures of the conquered states.
