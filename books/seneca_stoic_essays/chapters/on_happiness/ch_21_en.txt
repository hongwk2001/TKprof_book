[Chapter 21]
You ask why this person lives a rich life when he says he studies philosophy. Why does he own wealth when he says wealth must be despised? Why does he live when he says life must be despised? Why does he protect his health with his best effort and wish to be as healthy as possible when he says health must be despised? He considers exile just an empty word and asks what the evil of changing one country for another is. But why does he grow old in his hometown if he is allowed to stay? He declares there is no difference between a longer and shorter time. But why does he extend his life and flourish in a green old age if he is not stopped? His answer is that despising these things does not mean he should not own them. It means he should not own them in fear and trembling. He does not chase them away from himself. But when they leave him, he calmly follows behind them. Where on earth could fortune invest wealth more safely than in a place where she can always get it back without any argument with the trustee? Cato praised Curius and Coruncanius. He lived in a time when a judge punished the ownership of just a few small silver coins as a crime. But Cato himself owned four million sesterces. It was definitely smaller than the wealth of Crassus. But it was larger than the wealth of Cato the Censor. If you compare the amounts, Cato beat his great-grandfather much more than Crassus beat Cato. If even greater wealth had fallen to his share, he would not have kicked it away. The wise person does not think he is unworthy of receiving any accidental gift. He does not love wealth, but he prefers to have wealth. He does not accept wealth into his spirit, but only into his house. Also, he does not throw away what he already owns. He keeps it and gladly wishes to gain a wider stage where his Virtue can be practiced.
