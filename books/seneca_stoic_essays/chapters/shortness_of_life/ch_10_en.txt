Chapter 10: The Three Times of Life

I could easily divide this subject into distinct sections and offer countless proofs to demonstrate that busy people find life too short. But Fabianus—not one of today's academic philosophers, but a genuine philosopher of the old school—used to say, "We must fight against the passions with overwhelming force, not with clever tricks." He believed the battle line must be broken by a bold, head-on attack, not by inflicting tiny pinpricks. Sophistry is useless; "The passions must be crushed, not merely nipped." Still, so that these victims can be held accountable for their specific faults, they must be taught, not merely pitied.

"Life is divided into three periods: that which was, that which is, and that which will be." Of these, the present is short, the future is doubtful, and the past is certain. The past is the only period over which Fortune has lost her power, and it can never be brought back under anyone's control. But the preoccupied lose this entirely. They have no time to reflect on the past, and even if they did, they find no joy in recalling things they deeply regret. They refuse to look back on wasted hours. Those whose vices would become glaringly obvious upon reflection—even vices once disguised by momentary pleasures—lack the courage to revisit those moments. "No one willingly turns their thoughts to the past unless all their actions have passed the scrutiny of their own unerring conscience." Those who have ambitiously coveted, proudly scorned, recklessly conquered, treacherously betrayed, greedily seized, or lavishly squandered are terrified of their own memories. 

Yet this past is the sacred, untouchable part of our time. It is completely beyond the reach of human misfortune and removed from the dominion of Fortune. It cannot be disturbed by poverty, fear, or disease; it can neither be troubled nor stolen away. "It is an everlasting and unanxious possession." The present gives us only one day at a time, and only minute by minute. But all the days of the past will appear whenever you summon them; they will allow you to hold them and examine them at your leisure—a practice the preoccupied simply have no time for. 

"A tranquil, untroubled mind has the power to roam freely through all the parts of its life." But the minds of the preoccupied are weighed down by a heavy yoke; they cannot turn around and look behind them. Consequently, their lives vanish into an abyss. Just as it does no good to pour water into a vessel with no bottom, it makes no difference how much time you are given if there is nowhere for it to settle—it simply leaks through the cracks and holes of an unfocused mind. 

The present is incredibly brief—so brief, in fact, that to some it seems not to exist at all. It is always in motion; it constantly flows and hurries on. "It ceases to be before it has fully arrived." It can no more stop than the cosmos or the stars, whose restless movement never allows them to linger in the same place. The preoccupied, therefore, are concerned only with the present—a time so fleeting it cannot be grasped, and even this tiny fragment is stolen from them because they are distracted by so many trivial things.
