Chapter VII: The Distracted Mind

Among the worst offenders are those who devote their time solely to alcohol and lust, for no obsessions are more shameful. Others, even if consumed by the hollow illusion of glory, at least lose their way with some dignity. You can point to the greedy, or the wrathful—those consumed by unjust hatreds or unnecessary wars—but their sins possess a certain rugged resolve. In contrast, those who drown themselves in gluttony and lust carry a dishonorable stain.

Examine the schedules of all these distracted people. Look at how much time they spend crunching numbers, laying traps, or worrying about the traps of others. See how much time they waste flattering others or basking in flattery, managing legal bail, or attending banquets—which have themselves become just another business obligation. You will realize that their preoccupations, whether you consider them good or bad, leave them without even a moment to breathe.

Ultimately, everyone agrees that a distracted person cannot successfully pursue any single endeavor—not eloquence, nor higher learning. When the mind is scattered, it absorbs nothing deeply; it simply rejects whatever is crammed into it.

"There is nothing the busy person is less busy with than living; there is nothing harder to learn."

You can find teachers for every other subject anywhere you look. We have even seen mere boys master some skills so thoroughly that they could teach them. But, "It takes an entire lifetime to learn how to live, and—perhaps even more surprising—it takes an entire lifetime to learn how to die."

Countless great men have cast aside their burdens, renouncing wealth, business, and pleasure, making it their singular goal until their final breath simply to know how to live. Yet most of them have passed away confessing they still did not know—so how could those distracted others possibly know?

Believe me, it takes a truly great person, one who has risen above human weakness, to refuse to let a single second of their time be stolen. "The life of such a person is incredibly long because they have devoted every moment entirely to themselves." None of their time lay idle or neglected; none was surrendered to the control of others. Guarding it fiercely, they found nothing in this world worthy of exchanging for their time. That person had time enough. But those who have let the public rob them of their lives have inevitably run out of time.

Do not assume these people are entirely blind to their loss. Indeed, you will sometimes hear those burdened by massive success cry out from the middle of a crowd of clients, a heated court case, or some other glorious misery: "I have no chance to live."

Of course you have no chance! Everyone who demands your attention steals you away from yourself. How many days did that defendant rob from you? How many did that political candidate take? How many were lost to that exhausting old woman burying her heirs, or to that man faking illness to manipulate greedy legacy-hunters? How many were claimed by that powerful associate who keeps you around not as a friend, but as an accessory?

Audit the days of your life. You will see that only a handful—and the scraps, at that—have actually been left for you.

The man who clawed his way to high political office suddenly wants to step down, repeating constantly: "When will this year finally be over?" Another man hosts grand public games, treating the opportunity as the prize of a lifetime, only to ask: "When will I be done with this?" A famous lawyer draws massive crowds in the forum, filling the space far beyond where his voice can even be heard, yet he sighs: "When is vacation?"

Everyone rushes through their life, suffering from a desperate yearning for the future and a deep exhaustion with the present.

But the person who dedicates every moment to their own growth, who organizes every day as if it were their last, neither longs for nor fears tomorrow. After all, what new pleasure could any hour possibly bring? They have experienced it all; they have enjoyed everything to the fullest. Fortune can deal out the rest of their days however she pleases—their life is already secure. Something might be added to it, but nothing can be taken away. They will accept any extra time the way a full, satisfied person accepts an extra dessert: they do not crave it, but they have room for it.

"There is no reason to think a person has lived long just because they have gray hair or wrinkles; they have not lived long—they have merely existed long."

Imagine a sailor who was caught in a brutal storm the moment he left the harbor. Swept back and forth by violent winds from every direction, he was driven in endless circles over the same patch of sea. You would not say he had a long voyage. He did not do much sailing; he just did a lot of tossing about.
