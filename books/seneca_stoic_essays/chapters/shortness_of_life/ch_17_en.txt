Chapter 17: The Sudden End of Power

Even the pleasures of such people are uneasy and haunted by various alarms. At the very moment of rejoicing, an anxious thought strikes them: "How long will this last?" This dread has caused kings to weep over their own power; instead of delighting in their massive fortune, they are terrified by its inevitable end.

When the King of Persia, in all his arrogant pride, deployed his army across the vast plains—an army so massive he could only measure it, not count it—he wept profusely because he realized that within a hundred years, not a single man among them would be alive. Yet he was the very man about to hasten their doom: sending some to die at sea, some on land, some in battle, and some in flight. In a matter of days, he would destroy the very men whose distant fate he wept for.

Why is it that even their joys are paralyzed by fear? Because their joys are built on unstable foundations; they are as quickly shaken as they are born. What do you think their darkest times are like, if even the moments they consider their happiest—the heights that elevate them above the rest of humanity—are fundamentally tainted?

"All the greatest blessings are a source of anxiety, and at no time should fortune be trusted less than when it is at its best."

To maintain prosperity requires even more prosperity; to support the prayers that have been answered, we must offer still more prayers. Everything that comes from chance is unstable, and the higher it rises, the harder it falls. Moreover, what is doomed to perish brings true pleasure to no one.

Therefore, the lives of those who work hard to acquire what they must work even harder to keep are not just short, but deeply wretched. Through massive effort they achieve what they want, and with massive anxiety they cling to what they have achieved. All the while, they ignore the one thing that will never return: time.

New preoccupations replace the old; hope leads to new hope, and ambition to new ambition. They do not seek an end to their misery; they merely change the cause of it.

Have we been tormented by our own public offices? The offices of others will consume even more of our time. Have we finished laboring as candidates? We begin canvassing for others. Have we escaped the burdens of being a prosecutor? We take on the burdens of a judge. Does a man step down as a judge? He becomes the president of the court. Does he grow weary of managing other people's wealth for a salary? He becomes paralyzed by managing his own.

Did the barracks set Marius free? The consulship keeps him just as busy. Does Quintius rush to finish his dictatorship? He will be dragged back to it from his plow. Scipio goes to fight the Carthaginians before he is old enough for such a massive undertaking. After conquering Hannibal and Antiochus, after becoming the glory of his own consulship and the guarantor of his brother's, he would have been placed alongside Jupiter if he hadn't stood in his own way. But the discord of civilians will vex their savior, and as an old man, he will find bitter comfort in stubborn exile, despite having scorned honors that rivaled the gods when he was young.

Reasons for anxiety will never be lacking, whether born from prosperity or from wretchedness. Life simply pushes forward through an endless succession of preoccupations. "We will always pray for leisure, but we will never actually enjoy it."
