Chapter XVIII: Paulinus' Advice to Retire

So, my dearest Paulinus, tear yourself away from the crowd. You have been tossed by too many storms in the time you have lived; it is time, at last, to withdraw into a peaceful harbor. Think of how many waves you have faced, how many storms you have weathered in your private life, and how many you have brought upon yourself in your public career. Your virtue has been proven through laborious, unending trials—now, see how it performs in leisure.

The greater part of your life, and certainly the better part, has been dedicated to the state; take now some of your time for yourself. I am not calling you to slothful inaction, nor suggesting you drown your boundless energy in sleep and the vulgar pleasures of the crowd. That is not true rest. In your release and retirement, you will discover tasks far greater than any you have pursued so energetically thus far.

I know you manage the world’s accounts as honestly as a stranger's, as carefully as your own, and as conscientiously as the state’s. You win affection in an office where it is nearly impossible to avoid hatred. Yet, believe me: "It is better to understand the ledger of one's own life than the ledger of the public grain market." Recall your sharp mind—so perfectly suited for the highest subjects—from a service that is honorable, yes, but hardly suited for a truly happy life. 

Remember that all your early training in the liberal arts was not designed merely to make it safe to entrust you with thousands of pecks of corn. You showed promise of something far loftier. The state will never lack men of tested worth and diligent industry. But remember: "Plodding oxen are far better suited for heavy loads than thoroughbred horses," and who would ever burden the swiftness of such noble creatures with a heavy pack?

Reflect, too, on the immense anxiety you endure by subjecting yourself to such a crushing burden: you are dealing with the belly of humanity. "A hungry populace will not listen to reason, cannot be appeased by justice, and will not yield to any entreaty."

Consider those few days after Gaius Caesar died. He was still grieving most deeply—if the dead can feel—because he knew the Roman people were still alive and had enough food left for seven or eight days. While he was busy building his pontoon bridges and playing games with the resources of the empire, we were threatened with the worst disaster that can strike even a besieged city: mass starvation. His imitation of a mad, arrogant foreign king nearly cost us the destruction of the city, famine, and the general revolution that follows.

Imagine the sheer terror of those in charge of the public grain supply at that moment. They had to face stones, swords, fire—and a Caligula. Through the greatest of deceptions, they hid this massive threat lurking in the very organs of the state. And they did so with good reason: "Certain diseases must be treated while the patient remains in ignorance; for many, the mere knowledge of their illness has been the cause of their death."
