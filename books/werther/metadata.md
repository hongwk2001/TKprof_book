## Book Overview & Blurb

### English
Few books have ever struck their readers as hard as *The Sorrows of Young Werther*. Published in 1774, when Goethe was twenty-four, it made him famous across Europe almost overnight — and made its hero's blue coat and yellow waistcoat the uniform of a generation.

Werther, a young artist of great feeling and no direction, arrives in a country village and falls in love with Lotte, a young woman already promised to another man. What follows is told almost entirely in his own letters: the first rapture of the summer, the slow closing of every door, and the unbearable clarity of a mind that can see its own end approaching and cannot look away.

Told in the intimate, unguarded voice of a man writing to his closest friend, Werther remains one of literature's most searching accounts of love, loneliness, and a heart out of step with the world it was born into.

## Structural Notes

* **Epistolary form.** The novel is 84 dated letters plus a closing third-person narration. Letters are *not* chapters — they range from 18 to 3,042 words. Chapters are formed by grouping letters into 15 dated spans (see `roadmap.md`).
* **Three sections.** BOOK I (19,137w, May 4 – Sep 10, 1771), BOOK II (11,641w, Oct 20, 1771 – Dec 6, 1772), THE EDITOR TO THE READER (11,607w). A 95-word PREFACE opens the book and is folded into Chapter 1.
* **Translation register.** Werther writes to an intimate friend (Wilhelm). Korean must use a plain diary/letter register (`-네`, `-다`), **not** 존댓말 — this differs from *The Heroes*, which is addressed to children.
* **Glossary (fixed renderings).** Werther 베르테르 · Charlotte/Lotte 샤를로테/로테 · Albert 알베르트 · Wilhelm 빌헬름 · Wahlheim 발하임 · Ossian 오시안 · the Count C— C 백작.
* **The Ossian interlude** (Chapter 14) is verse translated as prose. It must keep an elevated, archaic tone distinct from the letters.
