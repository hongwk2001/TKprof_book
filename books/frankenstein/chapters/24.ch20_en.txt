Chapter 20

I sat one evening in my laboratory. The sun had set, and the moon was just rising from the sea. I didn't have enough light to work, so I sat idle, wondering if I should stop for the night or try to finish quickly by working straight through. As I sat there, I started thinking about the consequences of what I was doing. Three years ago, I had been working exactly like this when I created a monster whose unmatched cruelty ruined my heart and filled it forever with the bitterest regret. Now I was about to create another being, and I had no idea what her personality would be. She could turn out to be ten thousand times more evil than her mate, enjoying murder and misery just for the fun of it. He had sworn to leave human society and hide in the wilderness, but she hadn't. Since she would likely become a thinking, reasoning creature, she might refuse to follow a deal made before she was even born. They might even hate each other. The creature who was already alive hated his own ugliness, and wouldn't he hate it even more when he saw it in female form? She might also find him disgusting and prefer the superior beauty of regular humans. She might leave him, leaving him alone once again, made even angrier by the fresh insult of being abandoned by one of his own kind.

Even if they left Europe to live in the uninhabited areas of the new world, their need for connection would inevitably lead to children. A race of devils would spread across the earth, making human existence terrifying and fragile. Did I have the right to inflict this curse on future generations just to save myself? I had been swayed by the creature's clever arguments before. I had been terrified by his evil threats. But now, for the first time, I fully grasped the wickedness of my promise. I shuddered at the thought that future generations might curse me as a plague—a selfish man who bought his own peace at the potential cost of all humanity.

I trembled and my heart sank when I looked up and saw the monster at the window in the moonlight. A horrifying grin wrinkled his lips as he watched me doing the work he had forced upon me. Yes, he had tracked me on my travels. He had lurked in forests, hidden in caves, and taken shelter in empty wastelands. Now he had come to check on my progress and demand that I keep my promise.

As I looked at him, his face showed nothing but pure evil and betrayal. Driven by a flash of sheer madness, I thought of my promise to create another monster just like him. Trembling with rage, I tore the unfinished creature to pieces. The wretch saw me destroy the being he needed for his future happiness, and he retreated with a howl of demonic despair and revenge.

I left the room, locked the door, and silently swore to myself never to return to this work. Then, with shaking steps, I went to my own bedroom. I was completely alone. There was no one around to lift the gloom or free me from the sickening weight of my darkest thoughts.

Several hours passed as I stood near my window looking out at the sea. The water was almost entirely still, the winds had died down, and all of nature seemed to rest beneath the quiet moon. Only a few fishing boats dotted the water, and occasionally a gentle breeze carried the voices of fishermen calling out to each other. I felt the deep silence, though I barely realized how heavy it was until the sound of oars paddling near the shore caught my attention. Someone landed a boat close to my house.

A few minutes later, I heard my door creak, as if someone was trying to open it quietly. I shook from head to toe. I had a strong feeling about who it was and wanted to wake one of the peasants living in a nearby cottage. But an overwhelming sense of helplessness pinned me to the spot. It felt just like those terrifying nightmares where you try to run from approaching danger but simply can't move.

Soon I heard footsteps coming down the hall. The door opened, and the monster I dreaded stepped inside. He shut the door, walked up to me, and said in a muffled voice,

"You have destroyed the work that you started. What are you trying to do? Do you dare break your promise? I have suffered through hard work and misery. I left Switzerland with you. I crept along the shores of the Rhine, hiding on its willow islands and crossing its hills. I've spent months living on the wild moors of England and in the empty wastelands of Scotland. I have endured endless exhaustion, freezing cold, and starvation. Do you dare destroy my hopes?"

"Get out! I am breaking my promise. I will never create another creature like you, equal in ugliness and evil."

"Slave, I tried to reason with you before, but you've proven you aren't worthy of my patience. Remember that I have power. You think you're miserable now, but I can make you so deeply unhappy that you will hate the light of day. You are my creator, but I am your master. Obey!"

"My moment of hesitation has passed, and your time of power is over. Your threats won't force me to do something so wicked. Instead, they only strengthen my decision not to make you a partner in crime. Should I coldly unleash a demon onto the world who loves death and misery? Get out! I won't change my mind, and your words only make me angrier."

The monster saw the stubbornness in my face and ground his teeth in helpless fury. "Should every man find a wife," he yelled, "and every beast have a mate, while I remain alone? I once had feelings of love, but people repaid them with hatred and disgust. Man! You may hate me, but watch out! You will spend your hours in terror and misery, and soon the blow will strike that rips your happiness away forever. Why should you be happy while I suffer in such intense agony? You can destroy my other desires, but I still have revenge. From now on, revenge will be sweeter to me than light or food! I may die, but first you, my tyrant and torturer, will curse the sun that shines on your misery. Watch out, because I have nothing to fear and that makes me powerful. I will stalk you with the cunning of a snake, just so I can bite you with its venom. Man, you will regret the pain you've caused me."

"Devil, shut up. Don't poison the air with your hateful words. I've told you my decision, and I'm not coward enough to back down from threats. Leave me alone. I will not change my mind."

"Fine. I will go. But remember this: I will be with you on your wedding night."

I lunged forward and shouted, "Villain! Before you guarantee my death, make sure you're safe yourself."

I tried to grab him, but he dodged me and sprinted out of the house. Moments later, I saw him in his boat. It shot across the water as fast as an arrow and quickly disappeared into the waves.

Everything was quiet again, but his words kept echoing in my head. I boiled with anger, wanting to chase down the murderer of my happiness and drown him in the ocean. I paced around my room quickly and anxiously, while my imagination tortured me with a thousand terrible scenarios. Why hadn't I chased him and fought him to the death? But I had let him go, and he had headed toward the mainland. I shuddered to think who his next victim might be as he sought his endless revenge. Then I remembered his words again: "I will be with you on your wedding night." That, apparently, was the date he had set for my doom. In that hour I would die, which would finally satisfy and extinguish his hatred. The thought of dying didn't scare me. But when I thought of my beloved Elizabeth, and the endless tears and sorrow she would suffer when her lover was violently ripped away from her, I began to cry. They were the first tears I had shed in months. I promised myself I wouldn't fall to my enemy without a brutal fight.

The night passed, and the sun rose over the ocean. I started to feel calmer—if you can call it calm when raging fury settles into deep despair. I left the house, the terrible scene of last night's argument, and walked along the beach. I almost saw the sea as a wall blocking me from the rest of humanity. Actually, I caught myself wishing it really was a wall. I wished I could spend the rest of my life on that barren rock—exhausting, sure, but free from any sudden shocks of misery. If I went back, it would only be to die or to watch the people I loved most get killed by a monster I had created myself.

I wandered around the island like a restless ghost, cut off from everything I loved and miserable because of it. By noon, as the sun climbed higher, I lay down on the grass and fell into a deep sleep. I had been awake all night. My nerves were shot, and my eyes burned from staying up and crying. The sleep I finally got refreshed me. When I woke up, I felt like a normal human being again, and I started thinking about everything that had happened much more clearly. Still, the monster's words echoed in my ears like a death bell. It all felt like a dream, yet it was as sharp and overwhelming as reality.

The sun was getting low, and I was still sitting on the beach. I was starving, so I was eating an oat cake when a fishing boat landed nearby. One of the men handed me a package. It contained letters from Geneva, plus one from Clerval begging me to join him. He said he was wasting his time where he was. Letters from his new friends in London were urging him to come back and finish setting up his business venture in India. He couldn't delay his trip any longer. But since his move to London might quickly be followed by his long voyage, he begged me to spend as much time with him as I could. Because of this, he asked me to leave my lonely island and meet him in Perth, so we could travel south together. This letter snapped me back to reality a bit, and I decided to leave the island in two days.

But before I left, I had a job to do that I dreaded even thinking about. I needed to pack up my chemistry equipment. To do that, I had to go back into the room where I had done my disgusting work, and I had to touch the tools that made me feel sick just looking at them. The next morning, right at sunrise, I gathered enough courage to unlock my lab door. The pieces of the half-finished creature I had destroyed were scattered all over the floor. I felt almost as if I had hacked apart a living human being. I took a moment to steady myself and then walked in. My hands shook as I carried the equipment out of the room. I realized I couldn't just leave the remains of my work behind to scare the local peasants. So, I packed the pieces into a basket along with a bunch of heavy rocks. I set it aside and decided to throw it all into the sea that very night. While I waited, I sat on the beach, cleaning and packing away my chemistry gear.

My feelings had completely changed since the night the monster showed up. Before that, I had viewed my promise with gloomy despair, thinking I had to keep it no matter what happened. But now I felt like a blindfold had been taken off and I was seeing things clearly for the first time. The thought of starting my work again didn't even cross my mind. The threat I had heard still weighed heavily on me, but I didn't think for a second that doing what he asked would actually stop it. I had convinced myself that creating another monster would be the most incredibly selfish and terrible thing I could do. I forced out any thoughts that suggested otherwise.

The moon came up between two and three in the morning. I put my basket into a small rowboat and sailed out about four miles from the shore. It was completely deserted out there. A few boats were heading back to land, but I steered away from them. I felt like I was about to commit a horrible crime, and I was terrified of running into anyone else. At one point, a thick cloud suddenly covered the moon, which had been shining brightly. I used that moment of darkness to dump my basket into the sea. I listened to the gurgling sound as it sank, and then I quickly sailed away. The sky stayed cloudy, but the air was clean and fresh, even though a chilly northeast wind was picking up. The breeze actually felt good and relaxed me so much that I decided to stay out on the water a little longer. I locked the rudder in place and lay down in the bottom of the boat. Clouds hid the moon, it was pitch black, and the only sound was the boat cutting through the waves. The gentle splashing rocked me to sleep, and I soon drifted off completely.

I don't know how long I slept, but when I woke up, the sun was already high in the sky. The wind was blowing hard, and the waves looked like they might flip my small boat at any second. I realized the wind was blowing from the northeast and must have blown me far away from where I started. I tried to turn the boat around, but I quickly saw that if I tried again, the boat would immediately flood with water. Stuck like this, my only choice was to let the wind carry me. I have to admit, I started to panic. I didn't have a compass, and I knew so little about the geography of this area that the sun wasn't much help. I worried I might be blown out into the open Atlantic and slowly starve to death, or simply drown in the endless, roaring waves crashing all around me. I had already been out there for hours and was agonizingly thirsty—just the beginning of my suffering. I looked up at the sky, which was full of fast-moving clouds. I looked at the sea. It was going to be my grave. "Fiend," I yelled, "your work is already done!" I thought about Elizabeth, my father, and Clerval—everyone I had left behind, the people the monster might brutally murder to satisfy his rage. This thought dragged me into such a deep, terrifying despair that even now, as my life is coming to an end, I still shudder just thinking about it.

A few hours dragged by like this. But gradually, as the sun began to set, the wind died down to a gentle breeze, and the crashing waves smoothed out. A heavy, rolling swell took their place. I felt sick and could barely hold onto the rudder, when I suddenly spotted a stretch of high land to the south.

Even though I was completely exhausted from the physical strain and the terrifying uncertainty of the past few hours, knowing I was going to survive flooded my heart with intense joy, and I burst into tears.

It's amazing how quickly our emotions can change, and how fiercely we cling to life even when we're absolutely miserable! I rigged up a makeshift sail using a piece of my clothing and desperately steered toward the land. It looked rugged and rocky, but as I got closer, I could clearly see signs of farming. I spotted ships near the shore and realized I had suddenly found my way back to civilization. I carefully followed the coastline and cheered when I finally saw a church steeple poking out from behind a small strip of land. I was incredibly weak, so I decided to sail straight for the town where I could easily get some food. Luckily, I had money with me. As I sailed around the point, I saw a neat little town with a nice harbor. I pulled in, my heart practically leaping out of my chest because I was so happy to be safe.

While I was tying up the boat and putting away the sails, a crowd of people gathered on the dock. They looked really surprised to see me, but instead of helping, they just whispered to each other and pointed. Any other time, their behavior might have made me a little nervous. But right then, I just noticed they were speaking English, so I spoke to them in English. "My good friends," I asked, "could you please tell me the name of this town and where exactly I am?"

"You'll find out soon enough," a man with a rough voice replied. "You might have landed in a place you won't like very much, but I promise you won't get a say in where you're staying."

I was shocked that a stranger would be so rude to me, and I felt uneasy when I noticed the angry, glaring faces of the people around him. "Why are you answering me so harshly?" I asked. "It's certainly not typical for English people to be so unwelcoming to strangers."

"I don't know what English people usually do," the man said, "but the Irish definitely hate criminals."

While this weird conversation was happening, I noticed the crowd getting bigger and bigger. They looked both curious and angry, which annoyed me and started to scare me a little. I asked for directions to the local inn, but nobody answered. When I tried to walk forward, the crowd muttered angrily as they followed and surrounded me. A mean-looking man stepped up, tapped me on the shoulder, and said, "Come along, sir. You need to come with me to Mr. Kirwin's to explain yourself."

"Who is Mr. Kirwin? Why do I have to explain myself? Isn't this a free country?"

"Sure, sir, it's free enough if you're an honest person. Mr. Kirwin is the local judge, and you need to answer for the death of a gentleman who was found murdered here last night."

This answer stunned me, but I quickly pulled myself together. I was innocent, and I knew I could easily prove it. So, I quietly followed my guide and was taken to one of the nicest houses in town. I was ready to collapse from exhaustion and hunger, but since I was surrounded by a crowd, I thought it was best to force myself to look strong. I didn't want anyone to mistake my physical weakness for guilt or fear. Little did I know that a total disaster was about to hit me just moments later—a disaster that would wipe out any fear of embarrassment or death and replace it with pure horror and despair.

I need to stop here for a moment. I have to gather all my strength to clearly remember and describe the terrifying events that I'm about to tell you.
