Letter 1

To Mrs. Saville, England.

St. Petersburg, Dec. 11, 17—.

You will be happy to hear that my journey has started smoothly, despite your dark warnings. I arrived here yesterday. My first task is to tell my dear sister that I am safe and feeling more confident about my mission.

I am already far north of London. As I walk the streets of Petersburg, I feel a cold northern breeze on my cheeks. It wakes me up and fills me with joy. Do you understand this feeling? This breeze comes from the regions I am traveling toward, giving me a taste of the icy climate. Inspired by this wind, my daydreams grow stronger. I try not to think of the North Pole as a frozen wasteland. Instead, I picture it as a place of beauty and wonder. There, Margaret, the sun is always visible, glowing warmly on the horizon. I choose to believe the explorers who say that snow and ice do not exist there. We might sail over a calm sea to a land more beautiful than any discovered so far. The things we find there could be completely unique. What might we expect in a land of endless daylight? I might discover the mysterious power that controls the compass. I could also learn things about the stars that can only be proven by this voyage. I will satisfy my deep curiosity by seeing a part of the world no one has ever visited. I might even walk on land where no human has ever stepped. These goals are enough to conquer my fear of danger or death. They make me as excited to start this hard journey as a child playing in a boat on a river. But even if my guesses are wrong, you cannot deny the great benefit I will bring to all humanity. I will either find a faster sailing route near the pole or solve the mystery of the compass.

These thoughts have calmed the nervousness I felt when I started writing. My heart now glows with an enthusiasm that lifts me up. Nothing calms the mind like a steady purpose. This expedition has been my favorite dream since childhood. I passionately read accounts of voyages trying to reach the North Pacific Ocean through the polar seas. You might remember that our good Uncle Thomas's library was entirely made up of books about exploration. My education was neglected, but I loved to read. I studied those books day and night. Reading them only increased the regret I felt when I learned my father's dying wish: he forbade my uncle from letting me become a sailor.

Those dreams faded when I first read poetry. The poems captured my soul and lifted it to heaven. I became a poet myself. For a year, I lived in a paradise of my own making. I imagined I might become as famous as Homer or Shakespeare. You know well how I failed and how disappointed I was. But right around that time, I inherited my cousin's fortune, and my thoughts returned to exploration.

Six years have passed since I committed to my current goal. I can still remember the exact moment I dedicated myself to this great enterprise. I started by toughening my body. I went on several whaling expeditions to the North Sea. I willingly endured cold, hunger, thirst, and lack of sleep. I often worked harder than the regular sailors during the day. At night, I studied math, medicine, and science—everything a sailor might need to know. Twice, I worked as a lower officer on a Greenland whaling ship and did an excellent job. I admit I felt proud when my captain offered me the second highest position on the ship and begged me to stay.

And now, dear Margaret, don't I deserve to achieve something great? My life could have been easy and luxurious, but I chose glory over wealth. Oh, I wish someone would encourage me! My courage and determination are strong, but my hopes go up and down, and I often feel depressed. I am about to start a long and difficult voyage. The emergencies we face will require all my strength. I will have to keep everyone else's spirits up, even when my own are failing.

This is the best time of year to travel in Russia. Sleds fly quickly over the snow. The ride is pleasant and much better than an English carriage. The cold isn't too bad if you wear furs, which I am already doing. Walking on a deck is very different from sitting still for hours without moving. If you don't move, your blood can freeze. I don't want to lose my life on the road between St. Petersburg and Archangel.

I will leave for Archangel in two or three weeks. My plan is to rent a ship there by paying the owner's insurance. Then I will hire experienced whale fishermen as sailors. I don't plan to sail until June. And when will I return? Ah, dear sister, how can I answer that? If I succeed, it will be many months, maybe years, before we see each other again. If I fail, you will see me soon, or never again.

Goodbye, my dear, excellent Margaret. May heaven bless you and keep me safe, so I can thank you again and again for your love and kindness.

Your affectionate brother,

R. Walton
