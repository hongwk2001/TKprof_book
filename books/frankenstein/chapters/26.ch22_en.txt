Chapter 22

The voyage ended, and we traveled to Paris. I soon realized I had pushed myself too hard and needed to rest before continuing. My father's care was endless, but he didn't know what was truly wrong with me. He tried to cure my incurable illness by suggesting I go out and enjoy society. I couldn't stand the sight of people. But it wasn't that I hated them! They were my fellow humans, and I felt a connection even to the worst of them, seeing them as wonderful, almost angelic creatures. I just felt I had no right to be around them. I had unleashed a monster into their world, one who loved to kill them and hear them suffer. They would all hate me and hunt me down if they knew what I had done and the crimes I had caused!

Eventually, my father gave in to my need to be alone. He tried his best to talk me out of my despair. Sometimes he thought I was feeling humiliated because I had been accused of murder, and he tried to show me that being proud was pointless.

"Sadly, Father," I said, "you know so little about me. It would be an insult to humanity if someone as awful as me felt pride. Poor, unhappy Justine was just as innocent as I was, yet she faced the same accusation and died for it. I am the reason she died. I murdered her. William, Justine, and Henry—they all died by my hands."

My father had heard me say this many times while I was in prison. When I blamed myself like this, he sometimes wanted an explanation. Other times, he just thought I was delirious. He figured that while I was sick, I had imagined it all and remembered the delusions as I recovered. I refused to explain and kept completely silent about the monster I had created. I was sure people would think I was insane, which was enough to keep me quiet. Besides, I couldn't bring myself to reveal a secret that would terrify anyone who heard it. So, I suppressed my desperate need for sympathy. I kept quiet even when I wanted nothing more than to share my deadly secret. Yet, sometimes the guilt was too much, and I would confess without meaning to. I couldn't explain my words, but just telling the truth helped ease the weight of my misery.

This time, my father looked completely bewildered. "My dearest Victor, what obsession is this? Please, my son, I beg you never to say such a thing again."

"I am not crazy!" I shouted fiercely. "The sun and the heavens saw what I did, and they know it's the truth. I am the killer of those innocent people. They died because of what I did. I would have died a thousand times over to save their lives. But I couldn't do it, Father. I couldn't sacrifice the entire human race."

My outburst convinced my father that I was losing my mind. He immediately changed the subject and tried to distract me. He wanted to help me forget everything that had happened in Ireland. He never brought it up, nor did he let me talk about my suffering.

As time passed, I calmed down. Misery still lived in my heart, but I stopped rambling about my crimes. Knowing what I had done was enough for me. Through sheer willpower, I fought the urge to confess everything to the world. I acted calmer and more composed than I had since my trip to the glacier.

A few days before we left Paris for Switzerland, I received this letter from Elizabeth:

"My dear Friend,

"I was thrilled to receive a letter from my uncle in Paris. You are no longer so far away, and I hope to see you in less than two weeks. My poor cousin, you must have suffered so much! I expect you look even worse than when you left Geneva. This winter has been miserable for me, full of anxious waiting. Still, I hope to see peace in your face and find that your heart has at least a little comfort and calm.

"However, I worry that the feelings that made you so miserable a year ago are still there, maybe even stronger now. I wouldn't bother you now, while you are dealing with so much pain. But a conversation I had with my uncle before you left means we need to clear some things up before we see each other again.

"You might be thinking, 'An explanation? What could Elizabeth possibly have to explain?' If you are, then my questions are answered and my doubts are gone. But you are far away, and you might be both dreading and looking forward to this explanation. Just in case, I can't put off writing this any longer. While you were gone, I often wanted to bring this up but never had the courage.

"You know well, Victor, that our parents always wanted us to marry. They told us this when we were young, and we grew up expecting it to happen. We were loving playmates as children, and I believe we became close, valued friends as we grew older. But just like a brother and sister can love each other deeply without wanting to get married, couldn't that be true for us, too? Tell me, dearest Victor. Please answer me honestly, for the sake of both our happiness. Are you in love with someone else?

"You have traveled and spent years at Ingolstadt. I admit, when I saw you so unhappy last autumn, running away from everyone, I couldn't help but wonder if you regretted our engagement. I thought you might feel forced to honor your parents' wishes, even if you didn't want to. But you shouldn't think that way. I do love you, my friend. In all my dreams of the future, you have always been by my side. However, I want you to be happy just as much as I want to be happy. Because of that, our marriage would make me miserable if it wasn't truly what you wanted. It makes me cry to think that, burdened as you are by such terrible tragedies, you might trap yourself out of a sense of duty, giving up the very love and happiness that could heal you. I care for you unconditionally, and the thought that I might be standing in the way of what you truly want breaks my heart. Victor, please know that your cousin loves you too much to ever want to see you suffer. Be happy, my friend. If you just tell me the truth, I promise nothing will ruin my peace of mind.

"Don't let this letter upset you. You don't have to answer tomorrow, or the next day, or even until you arrive, if writing to me would cause you pain. My uncle will update me on your health. If I see just one smile on your face when we finally meet, whether it's because of this letter or anything else I've done, that will be all the happiness I need.

"Elizabeth Lavenza.

"Geneva, May 18th, 17--."

This letter reminded me of something I had completely forgotten: the monster's threat. "I will be with you on your wedding night!" That was my death sentence. On that night, the demon would use every trick to destroy me and rip away the little bit of happiness that might have eased my pain. He had planned to finish his killing spree with my death. Fine, let him try. We would definitely fight to the death. If he won, I would finally be at peace, and he could no longer torture me. If I defeated him, I would be free. But what kind of freedom? It would be the kind of freedom a farmer feels when his family is slaughtered, his house burned down, and his land destroyed—homeless, broke, and entirely alone, yet free. That would be my freedom. Only with Elizabeth would I have something to live for, though I would still be haunted by crushing guilt and remorse until the day I died.

Sweet, beloved Elizabeth! I read her letter over and over again. Gentle feelings crept into my heart, daring to whisper beautiful dreams of love and joy. But it was too late; my fate was sealed, and I was doomed to lose all hope. Still, I would gladly die to make her happy. If the monster followed through with his threat, my death was certain. I wondered if marrying her would just speed up my death. I might die a few months earlier, but if my tormentor thought I delayed the wedding because of him, he would surely find an even worse way to get revenge. He swore to be there on my wedding night. But that promise didn't stop him from killing in the meantime. To prove he was still thirsty for blood, he murdered Clerval right after making his threat. Therefore, I decided that if marrying my cousin right away would make her or my father happy, I wouldn't let my enemy delay it for a single hour.

With my mind made up, I wrote back to Elizabeth. My letter was calm and loving. "I fear, my beloved girl," I wrote, "that we have very little happiness left in this world. But whatever joy I might still find is completely centered on you. Please, put your worries away. I dedicate my life and all my efforts to making you happy. I do have one secret, Elizabeth, and it is a terrible one. When I finally tell you, it will chill you to the bone. You won't be surprised by my misery anymore; you'll only wonder how I've survived everything I've been through. I promise to tell you this horrific story the day after we are married. We need to have complete trust in each other. But until then, please don't ask about it or even mention it. I am begging you, and I know you will understand."

About a week after receiving Elizabeth's letter, we finally returned to Geneva. The sweet girl welcomed me with open arms, though her eyes filled with tears when she saw how sickly and feverish I looked. I noticed a change in her, too. She was thinner and had lost much of the bright, lively energy I had always loved. But her gentleness and compassionate gaze made her the perfect companion for someone as broken and miserable as me.

The peace I briefly felt didn't last long. My memories drove me to madness, and whenever I thought about what had happened, I felt completely insane. Sometimes I would burn with furious anger, and other times I would sink into a deep depression. I wouldn't talk to or look at anyone. I would just sit perfectly still, overwhelmed by the countless tragedies that had destroyed me.

Only Elizabeth could pull me out of these dark moods. Her soft voice calmed me down when I was raging and made me feel human again when I was numb. She cried with me and for me. When my mind cleared, she would gently argue with me, trying to help me accept everything that had happened. It's easy for victims to accept their fate, but for the guilty, there is never any peace. The agony of my guilt poisoned whatever small comfort I might have found in letting myself grieve.

Shortly after we arrived, my father suggested that Elizabeth and I marry right away. I stayed quiet.

"Are you in love with someone else?" he asked.

"No one else in the world," I replied. "I love Elizabeth and I truly look forward to marrying her. Let's set a date. On that day, in life or in death, I will dedicate myself entirely to making her happy."

"My dear Victor, please don't talk like that," my father said. "We have suffered terrible tragedies, but that just means we need to hold on tightly to what we still have. We must give the love we had for those we lost to the ones who are still here. Our family might be smaller, but we will be tightly bound by our love and shared sorrow. Once time eases your pain, you will have a new family to care for, replacing those who were so cruelly taken from us."

Those were my father's wise words, but they only reminded me of the monster's threat. After all the unstoppable bloodshed he had caused, it shouldn't be a surprise that I thought of him as practically invincible. When he told me, "I will be with you on your wedding night," I saw it as my inescapable doom. Still, dying wouldn't be so bad if it meant I wouldn't lose Elizabeth. So, trying to look happy and content, I agreed with my father: if Elizabeth said yes, we would get married in ten days. And with that, I believed I had signed my own death warrant.

Good God! If I had known for even a second what my demonic enemy was actually planning, I would have left my country forever and wandered the earth as a lonely outcast rather than agree to this cursed marriage. But it was as if the monster used magic to completely blind me to his true intentions. I thought I was only marching to my own execution, but I was actually speeding up the death of someone far more precious to me.

As the wedding date got closer, my heart sank—maybe out of fear, or maybe it was a dark premonition. I tried to hide my dread by acting incredibly happy, which made my father smile with joy. But Elizabeth was far too perceptive to be fooled so easily. She looked forward to our wedding with a calm sort of contentment, mixed with a little anxiety. The tragedies of our past made her worry that our current happiness was just a fleeting dream, one that would soon vanish and leave us with nothing but everlasting regret.

We began preparing for the wedding. We welcomed visitors coming to congratulate us, and everything seemed cheerful and bright. I did my best to lock away the anxiety eating at my heart. I enthusiastically joined in on my father's planning, even though I knew these preparations might just be the decorations for my own funeral. Thanks to my father's hard work, the Austrian government returned a part of Elizabeth's inheritance. She now owned a small estate on the shores of Lake Como. We agreed that right after the wedding, we would travel to Villa Lavenza and spend our honeymoon by the beautiful lake.

In the meantime, I took every precaution to protect myself in case the monster attacked me out in the open. I carried pistols and a dagger everywhere I went. I was constantly on high alert, watching out for any of his tricks, which actually gave me some peace of mind. As the wedding approached, his threat started feeling more like a delusion, something I shouldn't even let ruin my peace. The happiness I hoped to find in my marriage felt more certain the closer the day came. Everyone spoke about it as a sure thing, something that absolutely nothing could stop.

Elizabeth seemed happy. My calm attitude did a lot to ease her mind. But on the day of the wedding—the day that would decide my fate—she seemed incredibly sad. She felt a strong sense of impending doom. Maybe she was also thinking about the terrible secret I promised to tell her the next day. My father, however, was absolutely thrilled. Caught up in the excitement of getting everything ready, he just assumed her sadness was normal pre-wedding jitters.

After the wedding, a large party gathered at my father's house. We decided that Elizabeth and I would start our trip by boat, sleep that night in Evian, and then continue our journey the next day. The weather was beautiful, and the wind was perfect. Everything seemed to bless our departure.

Those were the last moments of true happiness I would ever experience. We sailed along quickly. The sun was hot, but we were shaded by a canopy as we took in the beautiful scenery. Sometimes we sailed along one side of the lake, gazing at Mont Salève, the lovely shores of Montalègre, and, towering over everything in the distance, the stunning Mont Blanc with all the snowy mountains trying to match its height. When we moved toward the opposite shore, we could see the massive Jura mountains. Their dark peaks stood like an impossible wall, blocking anyone trying to leave the country or anyone trying to invade it.

I took Elizabeth's hand. "You look so sad, my love. If you only knew what I've suffered and what I still have to face, you would try to let me enjoy the peace and freedom from despair that this one day offers me."

"Be happy, my dear Victor," Elizabeth replied. "I hope there's nothing left to upset you. And please know that even if I don't look overjoyed, my heart is perfectly content. A little voice keeps whispering that I shouldn't rely too much on this bright future of ours, but I refuse to listen to it. Look at how fast we're sailing. See how the clouds, sometimes hiding and sometimes framing the peak of Mont Blanc, make everything look even more beautiful. Look at all the fish swimming in the clear water; you can even see every little pebble on the bottom. What a gorgeous day! Everything in nature seems so happy and peaceful!"

Elizabeth tried her best to distract both of us from our depressing thoughts. But her mood kept changing. Joy would light up her eyes for a moment, only to quickly fade back into distraction and deep thought.

The sun began to set. We passed the river Drance and watched it cut through the deep valleys and lower hills. The Alps come right up to the lake here, and we soon reached the huge wall of mountains that make up its eastern border. The church spire of Evian gleamed below the surrounding woods, right under the towering mountain ranges above.

The wind had been pushing us along incredibly fast, but at sunset, it dropped to a gentle breeze. The soft air barely rippled the water and rustled the trees as we neared the shore, bringing the sweet smell of flowers and fresh hay. The sun finally disappeared below the horizon as we landed. The moment I stepped onto the shore, all my worries and fears came rushing back—fears that were about to grab hold of me and never let go.
