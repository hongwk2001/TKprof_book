Chapter 19

London was our current resting place. We decided to stay for several months in this famous, incredible city. Clerval wanted to meet the brilliant and talented people living there, but that was a secondary goal for me. I was mostly focused on getting the information I needed to fulfill my promise. I quickly used the letters of introduction I had brought along, which were addressed to the most distinguished scientists of the time.

If this trip had happened back when I was a happy student, I would have enjoyed it beyond words. But a shadow had fallen over my life. I only visited these scholars for the information they could give me on the dark subject I was so deeply invested in. Being around other people just irritated me. When I was alone, I could focus my mind on nature and the world around me. Henry's voice calmed me, and I could sometimes trick myself into a fleeting sense of peace. But seeing crowds of busy, cheerful faces only brought back my despair. I felt an impossible barrier separating me from the rest of humanity. This barrier was sealed with the blood of William and Justine. Thinking about what had happened to them filled me with absolute agony.

In Clerval, I saw a reflection of who I used to be. He was curious and eager to learn. He found endless amusement and education in observing the different customs around him. He was also pursuing a long-held goal. He planned to visit India, believing his knowledge of its languages and society could help advance European trade and colonization. Britain was the only place where he could push this plan forward. He stayed constantly busy. The only thing dampening his enjoyment was my constant depression. I tried to hide my sadness as much as I could, so I wouldn't ruin the fun for someone starting a new chapter of life without any dark memories. I often made excuses not to join him, pretending I had other plans, just so I could stay alone. During this time, I started gathering the materials I needed for my new creation. The process felt like the torture of a dripping tap on my skull. Every thought I gave to the project brought extreme agony. Every time I even hinted at it, my lips trembled and my heart raced.

After spending a few months in London, we got a letter from someone in Scotland who used to visit us back in Geneva. He described the beauty of his homeland and asked if that was enough to tempt us to travel north to Perth, where he lived. Clerval was eager to accept the invitation. Even though I hated being around people, I wanted to see mountains and streams again. I longed for the incredible natural beauty I used to love.

We had arrived in England in early October, and it was now February. We decided to start heading north in another month. We didn't plan to take the direct route to Edinburgh. Instead, we wanted to visit Windsor, Oxford, Matlock, and the Cumberland lakes, aiming to finish the tour around late July. I packed up my chemical equipment and the supplies I had gathered. I planned to finish my work in some hidden corner of the Scottish Highlands.

We left London on March 27th and stayed a few days at Windsor, wandering through its beautiful forest. The scenery was completely new for us mountain dwellers. We were fascinated by the massive oak trees, the abundant wildlife, and the herds of elegant deer.

From Windsor, we traveled to Oxford. Entering the city, we couldn't help but think about the historic events that had taken place there over a century and a half ago. This was where Charles I had gathered his army. Oxford had stayed loyal to him even after the rest of the country turned against his cause to fight for Parliament and freedom. Remembering the doomed king and his followers—the friendly Falkland, the arrogant Goring, his queen, and his son—made every corner of the city feel special. The spirit of the past lived on in Oxford, and we loved exploring its historical echoes. But even without that history, the city was gorgeous enough to win us over. The colleges were ancient and charming, and the streets were practically grand. The beautiful River Isis flowed alongside it through lush, green meadows, widening into a calm stretch of water that mirrored the city's impressive towers, spires, and domes nestled among old trees.

I enjoyed the scenery, but my happiness was poisoned by memories of the past and dread for the future. I was built for a peaceful, happy life. When I was young, I never felt dissatisfied. If I ever got bored, looking at beautiful nature or studying human achievements always lifted my spirits. But now, I felt like a tree struck by lightning. The blast had burned right to my soul. I knew I would only survive long enough to show the world what I was about to become: a miserable, broken ruin of a man, pathetic to everyone else and unbearable to myself.

We spent a lot of time in Oxford, wandering the surrounding areas and trying to find every spot connected to that dramatic period of English history. Our little adventures often dragged on as we kept finding new things to see. We visited the tomb of the famous Hampden and the battlefield where he died. For a brief moment, seeing these monuments to freedom and sacrifice lifted me out of my pathetic fears. Just for a second, I dared to shake off my chains and feel truly free. But the iron had sunk too deep into my skin. I quickly collapsed back into my miserable, terrified self.

We were sad to leave Oxford, but we moved on to Matlock for our next stop. The countryside around the village looked a lot like Switzerland, just on a smaller scale. The green hills were missing the distant, snow-capped Alps that always towered over the pine forests back home. We visited the amazing local caves and small natural history exhibits, which were set up just like the ones in Servox and Chamonix. Hearing Henry say "Chamonix" made me physically shake. I rushed us out of Matlock because it suddenly reminded me of that awful place.

Continuing north from Derby, we spent two months in Cumberland and Westmorland. I could almost pretend I was back in the Swiss mountains. The lingering patches of snow on the northern slopes, the lakes, and the rushing rocky streams were all familiar and comforting. We even made some friends there who almost managed to make me happy. Clerval, of course, was having a much better time than I was. He thrived around other talented people, discovering skills and depth in himself that he never knew he had. "I could live here forever," he told me. "In these mountains, I would hardly even miss Switzerland or the Rhine."

But he soon realized that a traveler's life brings a lot of pain along with the fun. A traveler's emotions are constantly stretched thin. Just as you start to relax and settle into a place you enjoy, you have to pack up and leave for something new. Then that new place grabs your attention, only for you to abandon it again for the next destination.

We had barely finished touring the lakes of Cumberland and Westmorland, and were just starting to care for the locals, when it was time to meet our friend in Scotland. We packed up and moved on. Honestly, I wasn't sad to leave. I had been putting off my promise for a while now, and I was terrified of how the monster would react. What if he stayed in Switzerland and took his revenge on my family? This thought haunted me, ruining every moment of peace I managed to find. I waited for my mail with a panicked impatience. If my letters were late, I was miserable and drowning in paranoia. When they finally arrived, I was almost too scared to look at Elizabeth's or my father's handwriting to see if they were still alive. Sometimes I convinced myself the monster was following me and might punish my delay by killing Henry. When that fear took over, I wouldn't leave Henry's side. I followed him like a shadow to protect him from his imaginary killer. I felt like I had committed some terrible crime and the guilt was eating me alive. I hadn't technically done anything wrong, but I had brought a deadly, horrible curse down on myself all the same.

I dragged myself through Edinburgh with tired eyes and a heavy mind. It was a shame, because the city was incredible enough to fascinate anyone. Clerval didn't love it quite as much as Oxford, since he preferred Oxford's ancient history. Still, the beautiful layout of Edinburgh's New Town, its romantic castle, and the stunning scenery around Arthur's Seat, St. Bernard's Well, and the Pentland Hills won him over. He was cheerful and impressed. I, however, just wanted to reach the end of our journey.

We left Edinburgh after a week and traveled through Coupar, St. Andrews, and along the River Tay until we reached Perth, where our friend was waiting. But I was in no mood to chat and laugh with strangers. I couldn't fake the polite good humor expected of a guest. I told Clerval I wanted to tour Scotland by myself. "You go enjoy yourself," I told him, "and we can meet back up here later. I might be gone for a month or two. But please, don't try to follow me. Just leave me alone in peace for a little while. When I come back, I hope I'll be in a better mood and better company for you."

Henry tried to talk me out of it, but when he saw my mind was made up, he stopped arguing. He just begged me to write often. "I would much rather wander around alone with you," he said, "than stay here with these Scottish people I don't even know. Please hurry back, my friend. I won't feel at home again until you return."

After leaving my friend behind, I decided to find a completely isolated spot in Scotland to finish my work alone. I was certain the monster was following me and would show up the second I was done so he could claim his mate.

With that plan in mind, I traveled through the northern Highlands and settled on one of the most remote Orkney Islands to do my work. It was the perfect place for something so dark. The island was basically just a giant rock constantly pounded by the ocean waves. The soil was completely dead. It barely grew enough grass for a few sad cows, or enough oats for the people who lived there. The entire population was just five people. They were practically starving, with thin, bony limbs that showed how little they ate. Whenever they wanted luxuries like vegetables or bread, or even just fresh water, they had to travel five miles to the mainland to get them.

There were only three pathetic huts on the entire island, and one of them was empty when I arrived. I rented it immediately. It only had two rooms, and they were disgusting and falling apart. The thatched roof had caved in, the walls were completely bare, and the door was hanging off its hinges. I paid to have it fixed, bought some basic furniture, and moved in. Normally, a rich stranger moving in like this would have shocked the locals, but they were too numbed by their brutal poverty to care. I lived there completely ignored and left alone. They barely even thanked me for the small amounts of food and clothing I gave them. That's how much extreme suffering can dull people's minds.

In this hideout, I spent my mornings working. But in the evenings, if the weather was decent, I walked along the rocky beach and listened to the waves crashing at my feet. It was a repetitive but constantly shifting sight. I thought about Switzerland. It was nothing like this bleak, depressing landscape. Switzerland had rolling hills covered in vineyards and cozy cottages scattered across the valleys. Its beautiful lakes mirrored a soft, blue sky. Even when the Swiss winds whipped up the water, it was like a playful child compared to the violent, roaring ocean.

That was how I split up my days when I first arrived. But the further I got into my work, the more disgusting and frustrating it became. Sometimes I couldn't even force myself to go into my lab for days at a time. Other times, I worked day and night just to get it over with. It was a truly filthy, horrible process. During my first experiment, I had been blinded by a crazy, passionate frenzy. I was so obsessed with the final result that I completely ignored how horrific the actual work was. But now, I was doing it in cold blood. I often felt physically sick at what my hands were building.

Stuck in that place, doing the most disgusting job imaginable, and completely isolated without a single distraction, my mental state started breaking down. I became restless and intensely paranoid. Every second, I expected the monster to appear. Sometimes I just sat staring at the floor, terrified to look up in case he was standing right in front of me. I was even scared to walk too far away from the other villagers. I worried that if I was completely alone, he would show up to demand his mate.

Despite it all, I kept working. The project was actually quite far along. I looked forward to finishing it with a shaky, desperate hope that I didn't dare question. But at the same time, I had this dark, vague feeling that something terrible was going to happen. It was enough to make my heart ache in my chest.
