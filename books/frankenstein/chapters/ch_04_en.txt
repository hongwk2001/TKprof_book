Letter 4

To Mrs. Saville, England.

August 5, 17—.

Something so strange has happened to us that I have to write it down, even though you will likely see me before you read these papers.

Last Monday (July 31st), we were nearly surrounded by ice. It trapped the ship on all sides, leaving barely enough water to float. We were in a dangerous spot, especially since a thick fog rolled in. We stopped moving and waited, hoping the weather would clear.

Around two o'clock, the fog lifted, revealing massive, broken plains of ice stretching as far as we could see. Some of my men groaned, and I started to feel anxious. But then a strange sight caught our attention and made us forget our own trouble. Half a mile away, we saw a low sled pulled by dogs heading north. A huge man-like creature was steering it. We watched the traveler through our telescopes until he disappeared behind the distant hills of ice.

This sight amazed us. We thought we were hundreds of miles from any land, but this suggested we weren't. We couldn't follow him, though, because we were stuck in the ice.

A couple of hours later, we heard the ocean swell, and by evening, the ice broke apart, freeing our ship. We decided to stay put for the night, afraid of hitting loose chunks of ice in the dark. I used the time to rest for a few hours.

The next morning, as soon as it was light, I went up to the deck. All the sailors were gathered on one side, talking to someone in the water. It was another sled, just like the first one, stranded on a large piece of ice that had drifted toward us during the night. Only one dog was still alive, but there was a man inside the sled. The sailors were trying to convince him to board the ship. Unlike the first traveler, this man looked like a European. When I appeared, the ship's captain said, "Here is our captain. He won't let you die on the open sea."

Seeing me, the stranger spoke in English with a foreign accent. "Before I board your ship," he said, "could you please tell me where you are going?"

Imagine my shock! A man on the edge of death was asking where we were going before accepting rescue. I told him we were on a voyage of discovery toward the North Pole.

He seemed satisfied and agreed to come aboard. Good God, Margaret! If you had seen the man who negotiated his own rescue, you would have been stunned. His limbs were almost frozen, and his body was terribly thin from suffering and exhaustion. I have never seen a man in such awful shape. We tried to carry him into the cabin, but the fresh air made him pass out. We brought him back to the deck, rubbed him with brandy, and forced him to swallow a little to wake him up. Once he showed signs of life, we wrapped him in blankets and set him near the kitchen stove. Slowly, he recovered and ate some soup, which helped a lot.

Two days passed before he could speak. I worried his suffering had driven him mad. As he recovered, I moved him to my own cabin and took care of him as much as my duties allowed. He is the most fascinating person I have ever met. His eyes usually look wild, even crazy. But when someone is kind to him or helps him, his face lights up with unmatched sweetness and gratitude. Most of the time, though, he is sad and hopeless. Sometimes he grinds his teeth, as if the weight of his troubles is too much to bear.

When my guest felt a little better, I had to keep the men away from him. They wanted to ask him a million questions, but I knew he needed complete rest to recover. Once, however, the lieutenant asked why he had come so far on the ice in such a strange vehicle.

His face instantly darkened, and he replied, "To look for someone who ran away from me."

"Did the man you were chasing travel the same way?"

"Yes."

"Then we think we saw him. The day before we found you, we saw a man driving a dog sled across the ice."

This caught the stranger's attention immediately. He asked many questions about the path the demon (as he called him) had taken. Later, when we were alone, he said, "I have probably made you and your men very curious. But you are too polite to ask."

"Of course. It would be rude and cruel of me to bother you with my questions."

"And yet, you saved me from a terrible situation and kindly brought me back to life."

Soon after, he asked if I thought the breaking ice had destroyed the other sled. I told him I couldn't be sure since the ice broke near midnight, and the other traveler might have reached safety by then. But I really didn't know.

From that moment on, the stranger seemed to come back to life. He desperately wanted to go on deck to watch for the other sled. But I convinced him to stay inside because he is still too weak for the cold air. I promised to have someone keep watch and tell him instantly if anything appeared.

That is all I have to record about this strange event so far. The stranger is slowly getting healthier but is very quiet. He seems uncomfortable when anyone but me enters his cabin. Yet his manners are so polite and gentle that all the sailors care about him, even though they barely speak to him. As for me, I am starting to love him like a brother. His deep sadness fills me with sympathy. He must have been an incredible person in his prime, because even now, as a broken man, he is charming and kind.

I told you in a previous letter, dear Margaret, that I wouldn't find a friend on the open ocean. Yet I have found a man whom I would have been proud to call my brother before misery broke his spirit.

I will keep writing about the stranger whenever something new happens.

August 13, 17—.

My affection for my guest grows every day. I admire and pity him deeply. How can I watch such a noble person destroyed by misery without feeling heartbroken? He is so gentle and wise. He is highly educated, and when he speaks, his words are carefully chosen but flow quickly and beautifully.

He has mostly recovered from his illness and spends all his time on deck, watching for the sled that passed before his. Even though he is miserable, he still cares about other people's goals. He often talks to me about my mission, which I explained to him openly. He listened closely to my reasons for hoping to succeed and to every detail of my plans. His interest made me open up completely. I told him passionately how I would gladly sacrifice my wealth, my life, and all my hopes to succeed. I said that one man's life or death is a small price to pay for the knowledge and power I want to gain over the forces of nature. As I spoke, his face turned dark. He tried to hide his feelings by covering his eyes, but I stopped speaking when I saw tears pouring through his fingers. A groan burst from his chest. He finally spoke, his voice shaking: "You unhappy man! Do you share my madness? Have you drunk the same poison? Listen to my story, and you will throw that poison away!"

His words made me incredibly curious. But his outburst of grief exhausted him, and he needed many hours of rest and calm conversation to settle down.

Once he calmed down, he seemed angry at himself for losing control. Pushing past his despair, he steered the conversation back to me. He asked about my past, and I told him my story quickly. It made me reflect on my life. I talked about wanting a friend, craving a deep connection with someone who understood me, and believing that a person couldn't be truly happy without one.

"I agree with you," the stranger replied. "We are unformed, half-finished creatures unless a wiser, better, and dearer friend helps fix our flaws. I once had a friend who was the greatest person alive, so I know what true friendship is. You have hope, the whole world ahead of you, and no reason to despair. But I have lost everything and cannot start over."

As he said this, his face settled into a calm, deep sadness that broke my heart. He stopped talking and soon went to his cabin.

Even though he is broken, no one appreciates nature more than he does. The starry sky, the ocean, and all the incredible sights here still lift his spirits. Such a man lives two lives: he suffers terrible misery and disappointment, but when he retreats into himself, he is like an angel surrounded by a protective glow that keeps pain and foolishness away.

Will you laugh at how much I admire this wandering stranger? You wouldn't if you met him. You are refined and well-read, which makes you perfectly suited to appreciate this extraordinary man. Sometimes I try to figure out what makes him so much better than anyone else I have ever met. I think it is his deep intuition, his quick and perfect judgment, and his clear understanding of how things work. On top of that, he is a great speaker with a voice that sounds like beautiful music.

August 19, 17—.

Yesterday, the stranger said to me, "Captain Walton, you can probably tell that I have suffered terrible and unusual tragedies. I once decided to let my secrets die with me, but you have changed my mind. You are searching for knowledge and wisdom, just like I did. I sincerely hope that getting what you want doesn't destroy you like it destroyed me. I don't know if my story will help you. But since you are on the same path and facing the same dangers that ruined me, I think you might learn a lesson from what happened. It could guide you if you succeed, or comfort you if you fail. Prepare to hear things that sound impossible. If we were in a normal place, I might worry that you wouldn't believe me or would laugh at me. But many things seem possible in these wild, mysterious regions that would sound crazy to people who don't understand nature's strange powers. Besides, my story has its own internal proof that it is true."

You can imagine how happy I was to hear this. But I didn't want him to relive his pain just to tell me. I really wanted to hear the story, partly out of curiosity and partly because I desperately wanted to help him if I could. I told him this.

"Thank you for caring," he replied, "but it won't help. My fate is almost sealed. I am waiting for just one thing to happen, and then I can die in peace. I know you want to help," he added, seeing that I wanted to interrupt, "but you are wrong, my friend—if you will let me call you that. Nothing can change my destiny. Listen to my story, and you will see that everything is already set in stone."

He then said he would start his story the next day when I had free time. I thanked him warmly. I have decided to write down everything he says each day, as closely to his exact words as possible, whenever my duties allow. If I am too busy, I will take notes. You will surely enjoy reading this manuscript. But for me—knowing him and hearing it straight from his mouth—it will be incredibly powerful to read in the future! Even now, as I start writing, I can hear his deep voice in my ears. His bright, sad eyes are looking right at me. I can see his thin hand moving as he talks, his face glowing with inner fire. His story must be strange and terrifying. The storm that destroyed this great man must have been a nightmare!
