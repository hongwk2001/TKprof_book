Chapter 3

When I turned seventeen, my parents decided I should attend the University of Ingolstadt. I had only gone to schools in Geneva, and my father believed I needed to experience the world outside our home country to complete my education. My departure date was set for early in the year. But before that day arrived, I faced the first tragedy of my life—a dark warning of the misery that was coming.

Elizabeth caught scarlet fever. She was incredibly sick and in real danger of dying. As she lay in bed, everyone begged my mother to stay away from her so she wouldn't catch the sickness. At first, my mother agreed. But when she heard that her favorite child might actually die, she couldn't stop herself. She nursed Elizabeth day and night. Her careful attention beat the disease, and Elizabeth survived. But the cost was deadly. Three days later, my mother got sick. Her fever was severe, and the doctors' faces showed that she wouldn't survive. Even on her deathbed, this incredible woman stayed brave and kind. She took my hand and Elizabeth's hand and held them together. "My children," she said, "my greatest hope for the future has always been that you two would marry. That hope will now have to comfort your father. Elizabeth, my love, you must take my place and care for my younger children. Oh, I hate being taken away from you! I have been so happy and so loved—it is so hard to leave. But I shouldn't think this way. I will try to accept my death cheerfully and hope we will meet again in the next world."

She died peacefully, her face still looking loving even after she was gone. I don't need to describe the pain of losing someone so close—the massive hole it leaves in your soul, or the pure despair on everyone's faces. It takes so long for your brain to accept that someone you saw every day is gone forever. You can't believe their bright eyes are closed and their familiar voice is silenced forever. That is what the first few days feel like. But as time passes and the reality sets in, the true, bitter grief begins. Yet, who hasn't lost someone they loved? Why should I explain a pain that everyone has felt or will feel someday? Eventually, grief stops being an overwhelming necessity and just becomes a quiet sadness. People start to smile again, even if it feels a little wrong at first. My mother was dead, but we still had lives to live and duties to finish. We had to keep moving forward and try to feel lucky for the family we still had left.

My trip to Ingolstadt, delayed by my mother's death, was finally rescheduled. My father gave me a few weeks to grieve before leaving. It felt deeply wrong to leave my quiet, mourning home so quickly and rush into a busy new life. I had never experienced deep sadness before, and it scared me. I didn't want to leave my family, and most of all, I wanted to stay until my sweet Elizabeth felt a little better.

She hid her own pain to comfort the rest of us. She faced life bravely and took over my mother's duties with energy. She devoted herself completely to her uncle and cousins. She was never more beautiful than during this time, when she forced herself to smile just to make us feel better. She ignored her own grief to help us forget ours.

Finally, the day I had to leave arrived. Clerval spent the last evening with us. He had begged his father to let him come with me to the university, but it didn't work. His father was a narrow-minded merchant who thought higher education was a waste of time that ruined young men. Henry was heartbroken that he couldn't get a real education. He didn't say much, but the fire in his eyes showed he was absolutely determined not to get stuck working in business forever.

We stayed up late, unable to pull ourselves away from each other or say goodbye. When we finally said it, we all pretended to go to sleep, though none of us actually did. At dawn, when I walked down to the carriage, everyone was waiting for me. My father blessed me one last time, Clerval squeezed my hand, and Elizabeth begged me to write often while fussing over my bags like a true friend.

I threw myself into the carriage and sank into a dark depression. I had always been surrounded by friendly people who loved making each other happy, and now I was completely alone. At the university, I would have to make my own friends and protect myself. Because I grew up in such a quiet, private home, I hated meeting strangers. I loved my brothers, Elizabeth, and Clerval—my "old familiar faces"—but I felt totally unready to deal with new people. Those were my thoughts as the journey began. But as the miles passed, my mood lifted. I was desperate to learn. Back home, I often felt frustrated being stuck in one place, dreaming of going out into the real world. Now, my dream was coming true, and it would be foolish to regret it.

The trip to Ingolstadt was long and tiring, leaving me plenty of time to think. Finally, I saw the town's tall white church steeple. I got out of the carriage and was taken to my lonely new room to spend the evening however I wanted.

The next morning, I delivered my introduction letters and went to meet some of the main professors. Chance—or rather, the evil Angel of Destruction that had controlled me since I left home—led me straight to Mr. Krempe, the natural philosophy professor. He was an ugly, rough man, but he knew everything about science. He asked me what I had studied so far. I answered casually, even a bit sarcastically, naming the ancient alchemists I had read. The professor stared at me. "Have you," he asked, "really spent your time studying such nonsense?"

I said yes. "Every minute," Krempe snapped angrily, "every second you wasted on those books is completely lost. You've filled your head with dead theories and useless names. Good God! What cave have you been living in? Didn't anyone tell you that those ideas are a thousand years old and totally worthless? I didn't expect to find someone reading Albertus Magnus and Paracelsus in this modern, scientific age. My dear sir, you have to start your education completely over."

He stepped away, wrote down a list of modern science books I needed to buy, and sent me away. Before I left, he mentioned that he was starting a general science lecture series the next week, and another professor, Mr. Waldman, would teach chemistry on the other days.

I went back to my room, not feeling too disappointed. Like I said, I had already decided those ancient authors were useless. But the meeting didn't make me want to study modern science, either. Krempe was a short, squat man with a rough voice and an ugly face, so he didn't exactly inspire me. I've probably over-explained how I felt about science when I was young, but it's important. As a child, modern science bored me. Because I was young and had no one to guide me, I had dug into the past and traded real discoveries for the crazy dreams of ancient alchemists. I honestly looked down on modern science. When the ancient masters studied nature, they were trying to gain immortality and ultimate power. Even if they failed, their goals were huge and exciting. But modern scientists seemed to only care about proving those big dreams were impossible. I felt like I was being asked to trade grand, magical mysteries for boring, everyday facts.

I spent my first two or three days in Ingolstadt thinking about this while exploring the town. But when the next week started, I remembered Krempe's lectures. I absolutely refused to go listen to that arrogant little man brag at the front of a room. But then I remembered he mentioned Mr. Waldman, whom I hadn't met yet because he had been out of town.

Partly out of curiosity and partly because I was bored, I went to the lecture hall. Mr. Waldman walked in shortly after. He looked completely different from his partner. He seemed about fifty years old, with an incredibly kind face. He had a little gray hair on the sides, but the back was still black. He was short but stood very straight, and his voice was the most pleasant I had ever heard. He started the lecture by summarizing the history of chemistry and the great men who had advanced it. He then gave a quick overview of where the science was now and explained some basic terms. After showing us a few simple experiments, he ended the class with a passionate speech praising modern chemistry. I will never forget his words:

"The ancient teachers of this science," he said, "promised impossible magic and delivered nothing. Modern scientists promise very little; they know you can't turn lead into gold or live forever. But these modern philosophers, whose hands are always dirty and who spend their lives staring into microscopes, have actually performed miracles. They dig into the deepest corners of nature and show how she hides her secrets. They climb into the sky; they have figured out how blood pumps through the body and what the air is made of. They have gained incredible, almost unlimited power. They can control lightning, copy earthquakes, and even recreate the invisible world."

Those were the professor's words—or rather, the words of Fate, spoken to destroy me. As he talked, I felt like my soul was fighting a physical enemy. One by one, he was pressing the buttons that controlled my mind. By the time he finished, my head was filled with one massive, overwhelming thought. If so much has been done, the soul of Frankenstein screamed, I will do more, so much more! I will follow the path they started, carve a new way, unlock unknown powers, and reveal the universe's deepest mysteries to the world.

I left the lecture absolutely thrilled and went to visit Mr. Waldman that same night. In private, he was even kinder and more charming than he was in class. The serious attitude he had during the lecture was replaced by total friendliness. I told him the same story about my past studies that I had told Krempe. He listened closely and smiled when I mentioned Cornelius Agrippa and Paracelsus, but he didn't insult them like Krempe did. He said, "Modern scientists owe a lot to the endless energy of those ancient men. They laid the groundwork for everything we know now. They did the hard work of uncovering the facts, leaving us the easy job of organizing them and giving them new names. Even when geniuses look in the wrong direction, their work almost always helps humanity in the end." I listened to him speak without any arrogance or bragging. I politely told him that his lecture had completely changed my mind about modern chemistry. I hid how wildly excited I actually was—which I only managed to do because I was young and easily embarrassed—and asked him which books I should buy.

"I am happy," Mr. Waldman said, "to have a new student. If you work as hard as you are smart, you will definitely succeed. Chemistry is the most exciting area of science right now, which is why I focus on it. But I don't ignore the other subjects. You'd be a terrible chemist if you only knew chemistry. If you want to be a true man of science, and not just some guy doing party tricks, I highly suggest you study all areas of science, including math."

He then took me into his laboratory and showed me his machines. He told me what I needed to buy for myself and promised I could use his equipment once I knew enough not to break it. He gave me the list of books I asked for, and I left.

That was the end of a day I will never forget. It was the day my future was decided.
