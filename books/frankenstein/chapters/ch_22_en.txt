Chapter 18

Days and weeks passed after my return to Geneva. I couldn't gather the courage to start my work again. I feared the disappointed monster's revenge, but I couldn't overcome my disgust for the task he forced on me. I realized that I couldn't build a female companion without dedicating several more months to deep study and difficult experiments. I had heard about some discoveries made by an English philosopher, and that knowledge was crucial to my success. I sometimes thought about getting my father's permission to visit England for this reason. However, I used any excuse to delay. I dreaded taking the first step in a project that seemed less urgent as time went on.

A real change had come over me. My health, which had been failing, was now much better. Whenever I managed to forget my dark promise, my spirits rose. My father noticed this change and was pleased. He tried to think of the best way to wipe out the lingering traces of my depression. Every so often, it would return in intense waves, blocking out the sunshine with a consuming darkness. During those times, I sought absolute solitude. I spent entire days alone in a small boat on the lake. I just watched the clouds and listened to the rippling waves, feeling quiet and drained. But the fresh air and bright sun almost always restored my calm. When I returned home, I greeted my friends with a quick smile and a much lighter heart.

After I returned from one of these outings, my father called me aside to speak with me.

"I'm happy to see, my dear son, that you're enjoying your old hobbies again and seem more like yourself," he said. "Yet, you are still unhappy and continue to avoid our company. For a while, I had no idea what was causing this. Yesterday, though, a thought struck me. If my guess is right, I ask you to be honest with me. Hiding the truth about this would be pointless, and it would only bring terrible misery to all of us."

I shook violently at his opening words, but my father continued.

"I admit, my son, that I have always looked forward to your marriage with our dear Elizabeth. I saw it as the foundation of our family's comfort and the support of my old age. You two were close from the time you were babies. You studied together and seemed perfectly matched in your personalities and tastes. But human planning is blind. The very things I thought would guarantee this match might have completely ruined it. Perhaps you only see her as a sister and have no desire to make her your wife. Or maybe you've met someone else you love. If you feel bound to Elizabeth out of a sense of duty, that inner conflict might be causing the deep pain you seem to be feeling."

"My dear father, please don't worry," I replied. "I love my cousin deeply and truly. I have never met another woman who commands my admiration and affection like Elizabeth does. All my hopes for the future are completely tied to the expectation that we will marry."

"Hearing you say that, my dear Victor, gives me more joy than I've felt in a long time," he said. "If that's how you feel, we will certainly be happy, no matter what shadows current events cast over us. But it's exactly this gloom holding onto you that I want to clear away. Tell me, then, would you object to having the wedding right away? We have faced misfortune, and recent tragedies have shattered the everyday peace I need in my old age. You are younger. Still, since you are financially secure, I don't think an early marriage would interfere with your future career plans or ambitions. Please don't think I'm trying to force this happiness on you, or that a delay on your end would seriously upset me. Take my words openly, and I beg you to answer me with complete honesty."

I listened to my father in silence. For a while, I couldn't form a reply. A flood of thoughts raced through my mind as I tried to figure out what to do. Tragically, the idea of marrying Elizabeth right away filled me with absolute horror. I was chained to a dark promise I hadn't fulfilled yet, and I didn't dare break it. If I did, I couldn't imagine the disasters that would crash down on me and my loyal family! How could I celebrate a wedding with this deadly weight still hanging around my neck, crushing me to the ground? I had to finish my horrible task. I had to let the monster leave with his mate before I could let myself enjoy the peace of marrying Elizabeth.

I also remembered that I needed to either travel to England or start a long correspondence with the scientists there. Their knowledge and discoveries were absolutely essential to my current project. Trying to learn everything through letters would be slow and frustrating. Besides, I was completely disgusted by the idea of doing this vile work in my father's house, right under the noses of everyone I loved. I knew a thousand terrible accidents could happen. Even the smallest mistake could expose my horrifying secret and traumatize my entire family. I also knew that during this unholy work, I would often lose control of my emotions. I wouldn't be able to hide the agony taking over my mind. I had to get away from everyone I loved while I did this. Once I started, the work would go quickly, and I could finally return to my family in peace. With my promise fulfilled, the monster would leave forever. Or, as I foolishly hoped, some accident might destroy him in the meantime, ending my slavery once and for all.

These fears shaped my answer to my father. I told him I wanted to visit England, but I hid my real reasons. I made up an excuse that wouldn't arouse any suspicion. I argued for the trip so passionately that my father easily agreed. After seeing me suffer through such deep, madness-like depression for so long, he was just happy I could look forward to anything. He hoped a change of scenery and new distractions would completely cure me before I came home.

I was allowed to choose how long I would be gone. We figured I would be away for a few months, or a year at most. My father did take one kind precaution, though: he made sure I wouldn't travel alone. Without telling me first, he and Elizabeth arranged for Clerval to meet me in Strasbourg. This ruined the isolation I needed for my dark project. However, at the start of my trip, having my friend around wouldn't get in the way. Honestly, I was glad to be spared hours of lonely, maddening thoughts. Plus, Henry might act as a shield against my enemy. If I were alone, wouldn't the monster force his repulsive presence on me to check on my progress and remind me of my duty?

So, I was headed for England. We agreed that my marriage to Elizabeth would happen as soon as I returned. My father's old age made him hate the idea of waiting any longer. As for me, there was only one reward I expected from my awful labor, only one comfort for my unique suffering. It was the hope that once I was freed from this miserable slavery, I could finally marry Elizabeth and forget the past entirely.

I started packing for my trip, but one terrifying thought haunted me. While I was gone, my friends would be completely unaware of their enemy and utterly defenseless against him. My leaving might even provoke him into attacking them. But he had promised to follow me wherever I went, so wouldn't he track me to England? That thought was horrifying on its own, but it was also comforting because it meant my friends would be safe. I agonized over the chance that the opposite might happen. Still, throughout this entire period of being enslaved by my own creation, I just let my immediate impulses guide me. My gut feeling strongly suggested that the monster would follow me, sparing my family from his deadly traps.

I left my home country again near the end of September. The trip was my idea, so Elizabeth agreed to it. Still, she was deeply troubled by the thought of me facing misery and grief without her there to help. It was her careful planning that had arranged for Clerval to travel with me. Men are often blind to the thousands of little details that draw a woman's careful attention. She desperately wanted to beg me to come home quickly. Instead, a thousand conflicting emotions left her speechless as she gave me a tearful, silent goodbye.

I threw myself into the carriage that would take me away. I barely knew where I was going and didn't care what was happening outside the window. I only remembered one thing, and it brought me bitter agony: I had ordered my chemical instruments to be packed for the trip. Filled with bleak thoughts, I traveled through beautiful, majestic landscapes, but I stared blankly ahead without really seeing them. I could only think about my final destination and the horrific work waiting for me there.

After several days of numb, exhausted travel across many miles, I arrived in Strasbourg. I waited there for two days before Clerval finally arrived. Sadly, the contrast between us was staggering! He was thrilled by every new sight. He was overjoyed to see a beautiful sunset, and even happier to watch the sun rise on a new day. He constantly pointed out the shifting colors of the landscape and the changing sky to me. "This is what it means to live!" he cried. "Now I am truly enjoying life! But you, my dear Frankenstein, why are you so sad and full of despair?" In reality, I was drowning in dark thoughts. I didn't notice the evening star setting or the golden sunrise reflecting off the Rhine River. You, my friend, would find Clerval's journal far more entertaining than my memories; he watched the scenery with genuine emotion and delight. I, on the other hand, was a miserable wreck, haunted by a curse that destroyed any chance of happiness.

We had agreed to take a boat down the Rhine River from Strasbourg to Rotterdam, where we would catch a ship to London. During the trip, we passed many islands covered in willow trees and saw several beautiful towns. We stayed in Mannheim for a day, and on the fifth day after leaving Strasbourg, we reached Mainz. The Rhine becomes much more scenic past Mainz. The river moves faster there, winding between steep, beautifully shaped hills. We saw many ruined castles perched on the edges of cliffs, surrounded by high, dark, and unreachable woods. This part of the Rhine truly offers a diverse landscape. In one spot, you can see rugged hills and ruined castles looking out over massive drop-offs, with the dark river rushing below. Then, just around the bend, the view suddenly changes to thriving vineyards on green slopes, a winding river, and busy towns.

We traveled during the wine harvest and heard the songs of the workers as we drifted down the stream. Even I, depressed and constantly tormented by dark feelings, felt a spark of pleasure. I lay at the bottom of the boat, staring up at the cloudless blue sky, soaking in a peace I hadn't felt in a long time. If I felt that way, it's hard to describe how Henry felt. He acted like he'd been transported to a fairy tale, experiencing a kind of pure joy that few people ever know. "I have seen the most beautiful sights in my own country," he said. "I've visited the lakes of Lucerne and Uri. There, the snowy mountains drop straight down into the water, casting dark, impenetrable shadows. It would look gloomy and mournful if it weren't for the bright, green islands that cheer up the view. I've seen that lake whipped up by a storm, with the wind tearing water into funnels that look like waterspouts on the open ocean. The waves furiously crash against the base of the mountain where a priest and his mistress were supposedly buried by an avalanche. People say you can still hear their dying voices when the night wind pauses. I have seen the mountains of Valais and the Pays de Vaud. But this place, Victor, pleases me more than all of those wonders combined. The mountains of Switzerland are more majestic and bizarre, but the banks of this beautiful river have a charm I've never seen before. Look at that castle hanging over the cliff! And that one on the island, almost completely hidden by those lovely trees! And now look at that group of workers coming out of the vineyards, and that village half-tucked into the side of the mountain. Oh, surely the spirit that guards this place is much friendlier to humanity than the harsh spirits ruling the glaciers and unreachable peaks of our home."

Clerval! My beloved friend! Even now, it brings me joy to write down your words and to focus on the praise you so truly deserve. He was a man created from the very poetry of nature. His wild, enthusiastic imagination was balanced by his sensitive, caring heart. His soul overflowed with deep affection. His friendship was so devoted and incredible that cynical people claim it only exists in fairy tales. But even human connections weren't enough to satisfy his eager mind. While others simply admired nature, he loved it with a burning passion.

And where is he now? Is this gentle, wonderful person lost forever? Has that brilliant mind—so full of ideas and magnificent imagination, a mind that built entire worlds—truly died? Does he only exist in my memory now? No, that can't be true. Your beautiful, perfectly made body has decayed, but your spirit still visits and comforts your unhappy friend.

Forgive this outburst of sorrow. These useless words are only a small tribute to Henry's unmatched worth, but they soothe my heart, which overflows with the agony of his memory. I will get back to my story.

Past Cologne, we reached the flat plains of Holland. We decided to travel the rest of the way by carriage, as the wind was blowing against us and the river current was too slow to help.

The journey lost much of its visual charm there, but we arrived in Rotterdam a few days later. From there, we caught a ship to England. It was on a clear morning in late December that I first saw the white cliffs of Britain. The banks of the Thames River offered a completely new landscape. They were flat but fertile, and nearly every town seemed tied to some historical event. We saw Tilbury Fort and thought of the Spanish Armada. We passed Gravesend, Woolwich, and Greenwich—famous places I had heard about even back home.

Finally, we saw the many church steeples of London. St. Paul's Cathedral towered above them all, alongside the famous Tower of London, a landmark of English history.
