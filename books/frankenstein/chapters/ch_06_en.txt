Chapter 2

We grew up together. There was less than a year between our ages. I don't even need to say that we never fought or argued. Harmony was the core of our friendship, and the differences in our personalities only pulled us closer. Elizabeth was calm and focused. But despite my fierce emotions, I could concentrate more intensely and was obsessed with learning. She loved reading poetry and letting her imagination soar. She found endless joy and wonder in the incredible Swiss landscape around our home—the towering mountains, the changing seasons, the sudden storms, the quiet winters, and the lively Alpine summers. While she happily admired how beautiful everything looked, I wanted to figure out how it all worked. To me, the world was a secret waiting to be solved. My earliest memories are filled with curiosity, an intense drive to learn nature's hidden rules, and the pure joy of discovering them.

When my younger brother was born seven years later, my parents completely gave up traveling and settled down in Switzerland. We owned a house in Geneva and a country home on the eastern shore of the lake, just a few miles from the city. We mostly lived in the country house, and my parents led a very quiet, private life. I also disliked crowds and preferred to connect deeply with just a few people. Because of this, I didn't care much for my schoolmates. But I formed a very close friendship with one boy. Henry Clerval was the son of a merchant from Geneva. He was incredibly talented and imaginative. He loved adventure, hardship, and danger just for the thrill of it. He read everything he could find about knights and romance. He wrote heroic songs and started writing his own stories about magic and knightly quests. He made us act in plays and dress up as famous characters from King Arthur's Round Table and other legendary knights who fought in the Crusades.

No child could have had a happier childhood than mine. My parents were completely kind and forgiving. We never felt like they were strict rulers controlling our lives; instead, they were the ones creating all the fun and happiness we experienced. When I spent time with other families, I clearly saw how lucky I was, and that made me love my parents even more.

Sometimes my temper was fiery, and my emotions were intense. But strangely, I didn't waste that energy on childish games. Instead, I poured it into an eager desire to learn—but not just anything. I have to admit, I didn't care at all about languages, governments, or politics. I wanted to learn the secrets of heaven and earth. Whether I was studying the physical world, the hidden forces of nature, or the mystery of the human soul, I was always searching for the deepest secrets of the universe.

Meanwhile, Clerval focused on human behavior. He cared about the busy real world, the bravery of heroes, and the actions of men. His ultimate dream was to become a famous hero, someone history would remember as a brave adventurer who helped humanity. And the saintly Elizabeth shone like a bright lamp in our peaceful home. She shared our feelings. Her smile, her soft voice, and the sweet look in her angelic eyes were always there to bless and cheer us up. She was the living spirit of love, softening and drawing us in. I might have become grumpy from studying too much, or rough because of my intense emotions, if she hadn't been there to calm me down with her gentleness. And Clerval—could anything bad touch his noble spirit? Probably not. But he might not have become so kind and generous if she hadn't taught him that true goodness is more important than dangerous adventures. She made helping others the real goal of his massive ambition.

I love thinking about my childhood, back before tragedy infected my mind and turned my bright dreams of helping the world into dark, selfish obsessions. Plus, by explaining my early days, I am also explaining the small steps that led to my future misery. When I try to figure out where the dangerous obsession that ruined my life came from, I trace it back to small, almost forgotten moments. It started like a tiny mountain stream, but as it grew, it turned into a raging river that swept away all my hopes and joys.

Science is what controlled my fate, so I want to explain exactly how I became obsessed with it. When I was thirteen, we all went on a fun trip to the baths near Thonon. The weather was so bad that we had to stay inside the inn for a whole day. While trapped inside, I happened to find a book by Cornelius Agrippa. I opened it without caring much, but the theories he tried to prove and the incredible things he claimed soon turned my boredom into total excitement. It felt like a new light was shining in my mind. Jumping with joy, I ran to show my father my discovery. My father barely glanced at the cover and said, "Ah! Cornelius Agrippa! My dear Victor, do not waste your time upon this; it is sad trash."

If my father had taken the time to explain that Agrippa's ideas had been completely proven wrong, I would have dropped the book. If he had told me that modern science was much more powerful and useful than ancient magic, I would have happily gone back to my normal studies. It's even possible that my thoughts would never have taken the deadly turn that eventually destroyed me. But since my father barely looked at the book, I didn't think he actually knew what was inside it. So, I kept reading it as eagerly as possible.

When I got home, the first thing I did was buy all of Agrippa's books, and then books by Paracelsus and Albertus Magnus. I read and studied these wild, imaginative writers with pure joy. They felt like secret treasures that only I knew about. I have already said that I always wanted to uncover the secrets of nature. Despite the hard work and amazing discoveries of modern scientists, their classes always left me frustrated and unsatisfied. Sir Isaac Newton famously said he felt like a child picking up shells on the beach while the massive, unexplored ocean of truth lay before him. To my young mind, even the best modern scientists seemed like beginners playing on that same beach.

An uneducated farmer understood the basic elements and how to use them. The smartest modern scientist didn't know much more. They had peeled back a tiny corner of nature, but the real magic was still a complete mystery. They could cut things open, study the pieces, and give everything names. But they had absolutely no idea what actually caused things to happen. I looked at the walls that seemed to keep humans from truly understanding nature, and in my ignorance, I felt cheated.

But here were ancient books and men who claimed they had gone deeper and knew more. I blindly believed everything they wrote and became their loyal student. It might seem crazy that this happened in the eighteenth century. But while I went to normal schools in Geneva, I was mostly self-taught when it came to the things I actually cared about. My father wasn't a scientist, so I was left to struggle on my own, combining a child's ignorance with a student's desperate thirst for knowledge. Following my new favorite authors, I tried incredibly hard to find the philosopher's stone and the elixir of life. Soon, I only cared about the elixir of life. Money was boring to me, but imagine the glory if I could cure every disease and make humans immortal!

That wasn't my only crazy dream. My favorite authors frequently promised that I could raise ghosts or devils, and I desperately tried to make it happen. When my spells failed, I blamed myself for making a mistake, not the books for being fake. So for a long time, I wasted my energy on useless, outdated magic, mixing up a thousand contradictory theories like a complete amateur. I was drowning in a swamp of weird knowledge, guided only by my wild imagination and childish logic, until an accident changed my path again.

When I was about fifteen, we were at our house near Belrive during a terrifying thunderstorm. It rolled in from behind the Jura mountains, and thunder exploded from all directions at once. I stood at the door and watched it with curiosity and joy. Suddenly, a massive bolt of lightning struck an old, beautiful oak tree about twenty yards from the house. When the blinding light faded, the oak tree was gone, leaving nothing but a burned stump. When we looked at it the next morning, we saw the tree hadn't just been broken; it had been completely shredded into thin strips of wood. I had never seen anything so totally destroyed.

I already knew the basics of electricity. But a smart scientist happened to be visiting us at the time. Excited by the destroyed tree, he started explaining a new and amazing theory about electricity and galvanism. Everything he said made Cornelius Agrippa, Albertus Magnus, and Paracelsus look like fools. Strangely, realizing my heroes were wrong made me hate all my studies. It suddenly felt like we would never truly know anything. The subjects I had loved for so long suddenly seemed pathetic. With the dramatic mood swings common in teenagers, I completely gave up studying nature. I decided it was a flawed, useless subject that could never uncover real truth. In this bitter mood, I switched to studying math, believing it was the only subject built on a solid foundation and worth my time.

Our minds are built so strangely, and the tiny threads that pull us toward success or ruin are incredibly fragile. Looking back, it feels like this sudden change of heart was the work of my guardian angel—a final, desperate attempt to save me from the disaster that was already looming over me. I felt incredibly calm and happy after giving up those frustrating ancient books. It felt like the universe was trying to teach me that studying those secrets would bring evil, and ignoring them would bring happiness.

It was a strong effort by the forces of good, but it failed. Destiny was too powerful, and its unbreakable rules had already decided that I would be completely and terribly destroyed.
