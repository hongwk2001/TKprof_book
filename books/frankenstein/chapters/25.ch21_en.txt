Chapter 21

I was soon brought before the magistrate. He was an old, kind man with calm and mild manners. However, he looked at me with some severity. Then, turning to the men holding me, he asked who the witnesses were for this case.

About half a dozen men came forward. The magistrate chose one of them to testify. The man stated that he had been out fishing the night before with his son and brother-in-law, Daniel Nugent. Around ten o'clock, they noticed a strong north wind picking up, so they headed for port. It was a very dark night, as the moon hadn't risen yet. They didn't land at the main harbor. Instead, they landed at a familiar creek about two miles south. He walked ahead, carrying some fishing gear, while the others followed at a distance. As he walked along the beach, he tripped over something and fell flat on the ground. His companions hurried over to help him. By the light of their lantern, they saw he had tripped over a man's body. The man looked completely dead. At first, they assumed he had drowned and washed ashore. However, when they checked closer, his clothes were completely dry. His body wasn't even cold yet. They immediately carried him to an old woman's nearby cottage and tried to revive him, but it was too late. He was a handsome young man, about twenty-five years old. He had clearly been strangled. There were no signs of violence other than dark finger marks on his neck.

The first part of his testimony didn't interest me at all. But when he mentioned the finger marks, I remembered my brother's murder and grew extremely agitated. My limbs trembled, and my vision blurred, forcing me to lean on a chair for support. The magistrate watched me sharply. Naturally, he took my reaction as a sign of guilt.

The son confirmed his father's story. But when Daniel Nugent was called, he swore that right before his brother-in-law fell, he saw a boat just offshore with only one man in it. Based on what he could see by starlight, it was the exact same boat I had just arrived in.

A woman testified that she lived near the beach. She had been standing at her door, waiting for the fishermen to return, about an hour before she heard about the body. She saw a boat with just one man in it launch from the exact spot where the corpse was later found.

Several other men testified about my arrival. They agreed that the strong north wind had likely blown me around for hours, forcing me back to where I started. They also pointed out that I had probably brought the body from somewhere else. Since I clearly didn't know the coastline, they guessed I sailed into the harbor without realizing how close it was to where I dumped the corpse.

After hearing this evidence, Mr. Kirwin ordered that I be taken to the room where the body lay. He wanted to see how I would react to seeing it. My extreme agitation earlier had probably given him this idea. The magistrate and several others escorted me to the inn. I was amazed by the strange coincidences of the night. Still, I felt completely calm about the outcome. I knew I had an alibi, since I had been talking to several people on the Orkney Islands right around the time the body was found.

I entered the room and was led to the coffin. How can I even describe what I felt? I am paralyzed with horror just thinking about it. I cannot remember that terrible moment without shuddering in agony. The interrogation, the magistrate, and the witnesses all vanished from my mind like a dream when I saw Henry Clerval's lifeless body lying before me. I gasped for breath and threw myself onto him. "Have my murderous plots killed you too, my dearest Henry?" I cried. "I have already destroyed two people. Other victims are waiting to die. But you, Clerval, my friend, my savior—"

My body could no longer handle the agony. I started convulsing violently and had to be carried out of the room.

A terrible fever followed. I lay on the edge of death for two months. I later learned that my delirious ravings were terrifying. I called myself the murderer of William, Justine, and Clerval. Sometimes I begged my nurses to help me destroy the monster that tormented me. Other times, I felt the creature's fingers already wrapping around my neck, and I screamed in terror. Fortunately, I was raving in my native language, so only Mr. Kirwin understood me. Still, my wild gestures and bitter cries were enough to frighten everyone else in the room.

Why didn't I just die? I was more miserable than anyone had ever been. Why couldn't I just fade into nothingness and finally rest? Death takes so many vibrant children, shattering their parents' hopes. So many young brides and lovers are full of life one day, only to become worm food the next! What kind of monster was I made of, that I could survive so many shocks? It was like being broken on the wheel over and over again.

But I was doomed to live. Two months later, I woke up as if from a dream. I was in a prison, lying on a terrible bed, surrounded by guards, locks, and cold stone walls. I remember it was morning when my mind finally cleared. I had forgotten exactly what happened. I just felt like a massive tragedy had crushed me. But when I looked around at the barred windows and the filthy cell, it all came rushing back. I let out a bitter groan.

The noise woke up an old woman sleeping in a chair next to me. She was a hired nurse and the wife of one of the guards. Her face showed all the cruelty you'd expect from someone in her position. Her features were hard and harsh, like someone used to seeing misery without ever feeling sorry for it. Her voice was completely indifferent. She spoke to me in English, and I realized I had heard her voice during my feverish nightmares.

"Are you feeling better now, sir?" she asked.

I replied weakly in English. "I think so. But if it's all true, if I wasn't just dreaming, I'm sorry I'm still alive to feel this much pain and horror."

"Well," the old woman replied, "if you mean the gentleman you murdered, you'd probably be better off dead. Things aren't going to end well for you! But that's none of my business. I was hired to nurse you back to health, and I'm doing my job with a clear conscience. The world would be a better place if everyone did the same."

I turned away in disgust. How could anyone be so heartless to someone who had just barely survived? But I was too exhausted to think about everything that had happened. My whole life felt like a dream. Sometimes I even doubted if any of it was real, because it all felt too surreal.

As my memories sharpened, my fever flared up again. Darkness closed in on me. No one was there to comfort me with a loving voice or hold my hand. A doctor visited and prescribed medication, and the cruel nurse prepared it. The doctor clearly didn't care whether I lived or died, and the nurse looked downright brutal. After all, who would care about a murderer except the executioner waiting to collect his paycheck?

Those were my initial thoughts, but I soon found out that Mr. Kirwin had actually been incredibly kind. He had arranged for me to have the best room in the prison, even though it was still awful. He was the one who hired the doctor and the nurse. It's true that he rarely visited me. He genuinely wanted to help people in pain, but he had no desire to watch a murderer scream and rave in agony. He stopped by occasionally to make sure I wasn't being neglected, but his visits were very brief and rare.

One day, during my slow recovery, I was sitting in a chair. My eyes were half open, and I looked as pale as a corpse. I was completely overwhelmed by gloom and misery. I kept thinking I should just seek death rather than stay in such a miserable world. At one point, I even thought about pleading guilty and accepting the death penalty, feeling much guiltier than poor Justine ever was. I was deep in these thoughts when my door opened and Mr. Kirwin walked in. He looked deeply sympathetic. He pulled a chair up next to mine and spoke to me in French.

"I know this place must be awful for you," he said. "Is there anything I can do to make you more comfortable?"

"Thank you, but none of that matters to me," I replied. "There is no comfort left for me anywhere in the world."

"I know a stranger's sympathy won't help much when you're facing such a bizarre tragedy," he said. "But I hope you'll be out of this depressing place soon. I'm sure we can easily find evidence to clear your name."

"That's the least of my worries," I answered. "A strange chain of events has made me the most miserable man alive. After all the torture I've been through, death sounds like a relief."

"It really is incredibly tragic," he agreed. "By some freak accident, you washed up on this famously friendly shore, only to be immediately arrested for murder. Then, the very first thing you saw was the body of your own friend. He was murdered in such a baffling way, almost as if some demon deliberately left him in your path."

Even through my misery, I was shocked by how much he knew about me. I must have looked visibly stunned, because he quickly explained himself.

"As soon as you fell ill, they brought me all the papers you had on you," he said. "I read them hoping to find a way to contact your family and tell them what happened. I found several letters, including one that was clearly from your father. I immediately wrote to him in Geneva. Almost two months have passed since I sent that letter. But you're sick. You're shaking even now. I shouldn't upset you."

"This suspense is a thousand times worse than the truth!" I begged. "Tell me who died now. Whose murder am I mourning this time?"

"Your family is completely fine," Mr. Kirwin said gently. "In fact, a friend has come to visit you."

I don't know why, but I immediately assumed the monster had come to mock me. I was terrified he was here to brag about killing Clerval and force me to finish his horrible demands. I covered my eyes and screamed in agony.

"Oh! Take him away!" I cried. "I can't look at him! For God's sake, don't let him in!"

Mr. Kirwin looked very worried. He obviously took my outburst as a confession of guilt. He spoke in a much harsher tone.

"I would have thought that seeing your father would make you happy, not cause such violent disgust," he said severely.

"My father!" I cried, as all my terror melted into pure joy. "Is my father really here? How wonderful! But where is he? Why isn't he here yet?"

My sudden change in mood relieved the magistrate. He probably thought my screaming was just another fever dream. He instantly became friendly again. He left the room with my nurse, and a moment later, my father walked in.

Nothing in the world could have made me happier. I reached out for him and cried out.

"Are you safe? And Elizabeth? And Ernest?"

My father calmed me down and assured me everyone was fine. He tried to cheer me up by talking about things I loved back home. But he quickly realized it was impossible to be happy in a prison cell. "What an awful place to be stuck in, my son," he said, staring sadly at the bars on the windows and the filthy cell. "You traveled to find happiness, but tragedy just keeps following you. And poor Clerval—"

Hearing my murdered friend's name was just too much for me to handle. I broke down in tears.

"It's true, Father," I cried. "Some horrible curse is hanging over me. I must have to live to see it through, otherwise I would have died right there on Henry's coffin."

We weren't allowed to talk for long. I was still so weak that they had to keep me as calm as possible. Mr. Kirwin came in and insisted that I shouldn't exhaust myself. Still, seeing my father was like seeing a guardian angel, and it helped me slowly start to recover.

As my physical illness faded, a deep, dark depression completely swallowed me. I was constantly haunted by the bloody image of Clerval's murdered corpse. My panic attacks over it were so bad that my friends were terrified I would relapse and die. Why had they even bothered to save my miserable life? It must have been so I could finally fulfill my doomed destiny, which is now almost over. Soon, very soon, death will finally end my heartbeat and lift this crushing weight off my shoulders. Once I get justice, I can finally rest. Back then, death still felt far away, even though I constantly prayed for it. I would sit in total silence for hours, praying for some massive catastrophe to just bury both me and my monster in the rubble.

The trial season finally arrived. I had been in prison for three months. Even though I was still incredibly weak and could easily relapse, I had to travel almost a hundred miles to the county courthouse. Mr. Kirwin took charge of gathering witnesses and building my defense. Thankfully, I was spared the humiliation of a public trial. The grand jury threw out the case entirely when they saw proof that I was on the Orkney Islands when Henry's body was found. Two weeks after I was transferred, they released me from prison.

My father was overjoyed that my name was cleared and I was finally a free man, able to return home. I didn't feel the same way. A prison cell or a palace felt exactly the same to me now. My life was permanently poisoned. Even though the sun was shining, all I could see was pitch black darkness, lit only by two pairs of glaring eyes. Sometimes they were Henry's beautiful dark eyes, fading into death. Other times, they were the disgusting, watery eyes of the monster, just like the night I brought him to life in Ingolstadt.

My father tried his best to cheer me up. He talked about Geneva, and how I'd soon see Elizabeth and Ernest again. But I just groaned in response. Occasionally, I did wish I could be happy, and I would think warmly about my sweet cousin. I was desperately homesick for the blue lake and the rushing Rhone river that I had loved so much as a child. Most of the time, however, I was just totally numb. A prison felt just as good as the most beautiful scenery in the world. This numbness was only broken by sudden bursts of absolute panic and despair. During those moments, I often tried to kill myself. My father had to watch me constantly just to keep me from violently ending my own life.

Still, I had one responsibility left, and remembering it finally pulled me out of my selfish depression. I had to get back to Geneva immediately to protect my family and hunt down the murderer. If I could just find his hiding place, or if he dared to show his face again, I would shoot straight and finally destroy the abomination I had created. My father wanted to wait before traveling, terrified that the trip would kill me. I was a complete wreck—just a shadow of a man. All my strength was gone. I was basically a walking skeleton, constantly burning with a fever day and night.

However, I begged him to leave Ireland with such frantic impatience that he finally gave in. We booked passage on a ship heading to Le Havre and sailed away from the Irish coast with a good wind behind us. It was midnight. I lay on the deck, watching the stars and listening to the crashing waves. I was grateful for the darkness hiding Ireland from my view. My heart raced with a feverish kind of joy at the thought of finally seeing Geneva again. The past felt like a terrible nightmare. But the very ship I was on, the wind blowing me away from that hated island, and the dark sea all around me proved it was all too real. Clerval, my best friend, really was dead—murdered by me and my monstrous creation. I replayed my entire life in my head: my quiet, happy childhood in Geneva, my mother's death, and leaving for college at Ingolstadt. I shuddered as I remembered the insane obsession that drove me to build my hideous enemy. I thought of the night he finally woke up. I couldn't bear to think about it anymore. A thousand emotions crashed over me, and I sobbed bitterly.

Ever since recovering from my fever, I had been taking a small dose of laudanum every night. It was the only way I could get enough sleep to stay alive. Crushed by all these tragic memories, I took a double dose and quickly fell into a deep sleep. But even sleep couldn't save me from my misery. I had a thousand terrifying dreams. Towards morning, a horrible nightmare gripped me. I felt the monster's hands wrapping around my throat, and I couldn't break free. Groans and screams echoed in my ears. My father, who was watching over me, saw me thrashing around and woke me up. I was surrounded by the crashing waves, with a cloudy sky overhead. The monster wasn't there. For just a moment, I felt a strange sense of safety—like a temporary truce had been called before the inevitable disaster waiting for me. My mind clung to that fleeting calm, letting me forget everything for a little while. I suppose human brains are just built to protect themselves that way.
