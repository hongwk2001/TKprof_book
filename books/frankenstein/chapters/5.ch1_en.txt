Chapter 1

I was born in Geneva. My family is one of the most distinguished in that republic. For many years, my ancestors served as counselors and leaders. My father also held several honorable public positions. Everyone who knew him respected his honesty and his endless dedication to his work. He spent his younger years completely focused on his country's affairs. Because of this, he married late in life.

The story of his marriage shows what kind of man he was, so I must share it. One of his closest friends was a merchant named Beaufort. Through a series of bad luck, Beaufort lost his thriving business and became poor. Beaufort was a proud man. He couldn't stand living in poverty and being forgotten in the same city where he was once famous for his wealth. So, after paying all his debts honorably, he moved with his daughter to the town of Lucerne, where he lived in total poverty, hiding from the world. My father loved Beaufort deeply and was heartbroken by his friend's disappearance. He hated the false pride that made Beaufort push away his friends. My father immediately set out to find him, hoping to use his own money and connections to help Beaufort start over.

Beaufort had hidden himself well, and it took my father ten months to find him. Thrilled, my father rushed to the house on a poor street near the Reuss river. But when he went inside, he found only misery and despair. Beaufort had saved very little money, just enough to survive for a few months while he looked for a respectable job. But sitting idle with too much time to think only made his grief worse. After three months, the depression took such a heavy toll that he became bedridden, completely unable to work.

His daughter cared for him with the greatest love. But she watched in despair as their small amount of money ran out with no way to get more. However, Caroline Beaufort was incredibly strong, and her courage grew to face this hardship. She found plain sewing work, braided straw, and managed to earn barely enough to keep them alive.

Several months passed this way. Her father grew worse, taking up more of her time, which meant she earned less money. Ten months later, her father died in her arms, leaving her an orphan and a beggar. This final blow crushed her. She was kneeling by Beaufort's coffin, weeping bitterly, when my father walked into the room. He arrived like a guardian angel to the poor girl, who placed herself in his care. After burying his friend, my father took her to Geneva and placed her with a relative. Two years later, Caroline became his wife.

There was a big age difference between my parents, but it only seemed to make their bond stronger. My father's strong sense of justice meant he had to deeply respect someone before he could love them. Maybe in the past, he had loved someone who turned out to be unworthy, making him value true goodness even more. He loved my mother with a deep gratitude and worship, not just the blind fondness of an older man. He respected her virtues and wanted to make up for the sorrows she had suffered. This gave his love an incredible grace. Everything was arranged for her comfort. He protected her like a gardener sheltering a delicate, exotic plant from rough winds, surrounding her with everything that could bring her joy. Her health and even her usually steady spirit had been shaken by her hardships. During the two years before they married, my father slowly gave up his public duties. Right after their wedding, they traveled to the warm climate of Italy. They hoped the beautiful scenery and interesting sights would help heal her weakened body.

From Italy, they traveled to Germany and France. I, their oldest child, was born in Naples and traveled with them as a baby. For several years, I was their only child. Even though they loved each other deeply, they seemed to have endless love left over to pour onto me. My earliest memories are of my mother's gentle hugs and my father's kind smiles. I was their plaything and their idol. But I was also their child—an innocent creature given to them by heaven. They knew my future happiness depended on how they raised me. Because they took this responsibility so seriously and loved me so much, they constantly taught me patience, charity, and self-control. Yet, they guided me so gently that my entire childhood felt like one long string of joyful moments.

For a long time, they only had me to care for. My mother desperately wanted a daughter, but I remained an only child. When I was about five years old, we were traveling near the Italian border and spent a week near Lake Como. My parents loved to visit the cottages of poor families. For my mother, this was more than a duty; it was a passion. Remembering how she had suffered and been saved, she felt driven to act as a guardian angel to others. During a walk, they noticed a particularly miserable little cabin in a valley. A group of half-naked children outside showed that the family lived in extreme poverty. One day, while my father was away in Milan, my mother and I visited the cabin. Inside, a hardworking, exhausted peasant and his wife were feeding a tiny meal to five hungry children. One of the children instantly caught my mother's eye. She looked completely different from the others. While the four other children were dark-eyed and tough little street urchins, this girl was thin and very fair. Her hair was bright gold, looking like a crown despite her ragged clothes. She had a wide, clear forehead, bright blue eyes, and such sweet lips that she seemed like an angel sent straight from heaven.

Seeing my mother's amazement, the peasant woman quickly told the girl's story. She wasn't their biological child; she was the daughter of a nobleman from Milan. Her German mother had died giving birth to her. The baby was given to these good people to nurse when they were better off, right after their own first child was born. The girl's father had fought for Italy's freedom but was crushed when the movement failed. No one knew if he was dead or rotting in an Austrian prison. His property was taken, leaving his child an orphan and a beggar. She stayed with her foster parents, growing up in their rough home, looking like a beautiful rose growing in a patch of dark thorns.

When my father returned from Milan, he found me playing in our villa's hall with a child more beautiful than a painting. She seemed to glow, and she moved as gracefully as a mountain deer. My mother quickly explained. With my father's permission, my mother convinced the peasant family to let her adopt the girl. They loved the sweet orphan and felt she was a blessing to them. But they knew it would be wrong to keep her in poverty when she could have a rich, protected life. After talking to their village priest, Elizabeth Lavenza moved into our home. She became my more than sister—the beautiful, adored partner in everything I did.

Everyone loved Elizabeth. The passionate, almost worshipful way everyone treated her became my pride and joy. The night before she arrived, my mother playfully told me, "I have a pretty present for my Victor—tomorrow he will have it." The next day, when she introduced Elizabeth as her promised gift, I took her words literally. With total childish seriousness, I believed Elizabeth belonged to me—mine to protect, love, and care for. I took every compliment given to her as if it were praising something I owned. We called each other cousin, but no word could describe our true bond. She was more than a sister to me, and I knew she would be mine alone until the day I died.
