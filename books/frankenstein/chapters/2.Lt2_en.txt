Letter 2

To Mrs. Saville, England.

Archangel, March 28, 17—.

Time passes so slowly here, surrounded by frost and snow! Yet I have taken another step toward my goal. I have rented a ship and am busy hiring my crew. The men I have hired so far seem reliable and extremely brave.

But I have one unmet need, and it feels like a terrible problem. I have no friend, Margaret. When I am glowing with the excitement of success, there will be no one to share my joy. If I am hit by disappointment, no one will be there to cheer me up. I will write my thoughts down, of course. But paper is a poor substitute for real connection. I want a companion who understands me and whose eyes answer mine. You might think I am being dramatic, my dear sister, but I bitterly feel the lack of a friend. I have no one here who is gentle yet brave, smart and educated, and who shares my tastes to help me improve my plans. A friend like that could help fix your poor brother's flaws! I am too eager to act and too impatient with problems. But it is an even bigger problem that I educated myself. For the first fourteen years of my life, I ran wild and read nothing but Uncle Thomas's travel books. Then I discovered famous poets. By the time I realized I needed to learn other languages, I was too old to get the most out of it. Now I am twenty-eight, and I am less educated than many fifteen-year-old schoolboys. I may think more deeply, and my dreams may be grander, but they lack balance. I badly need a friend who is smart enough not to call me foolish, and who cares enough to help me steady my mind.

Well, these complaints are useless. I definitely won't find a friend on the open ocean, or even here in Archangel among the merchants and sailors. Yet even these rough men have some goodness in them. My lieutenant, for instance, is incredibly brave and driven. He desperately wants glory, or rather, to advance in his career. He is an Englishman, and despite his rough edges and prejudices, he is a noble person. I met him on a whaling ship. Finding him unemployed here, I easily hired him.

The ship's captain is an excellent, gentle man who disciplines with kindness. This, along with his honesty and bravery, made me really want to hire him. Growing up isolated, raised by you, made me sensitive. I strongly hate the usual brutality seen on ships. I never believed it was necessary. So, when I heard about a captain known for his kind heart and the respect he got from his crew, I felt very lucky to hire him. I first heard about him from a lady whose happiness he saved. Here is his story. Years ago, he loved a young Russian lady. He saved up a lot of prize money, and her father agreed to the marriage. Before the wedding, the captain saw her crying. She threw herself at his feet and begged him to spare her. She confessed she loved another man who was poor, so her father would never approve. My generous friend comforted her. When he learned her lover's name, he gave up his own plans. He had already bought a farm to live on. But he gave it all to his rival, along with the rest of his money, and asked the father to let her marry the man she loved. The father stubbornly refused, feeling honor-bound to the captain. When the captain saw the father wouldn't change his mind, he left the country and didn't return until he heard the girl had married the man she wanted. "What a noble guy!" you will say. He is. But he is also completely uneducated. He is very quiet and careless, which takes away some of the charm you would expect from someone so generous.

Don't think that just because I complain, I am giving up. My plans are as solid as fate. My trip is only delayed because of the weather. The winter has been terrible, but spring looks promising. They say it's an early spring, so I might sail sooner than planned. I won't do anything careless. You know you can trust my carefulness when other people's lives are in my hands.

I can't describe how I feel as the trip gets closer. It is a mix of pleasure and fear. I am heading into the unknown, to "the land of mist and snow." But I won't kill an albatross, so don't worry about me returning as cursed as the Ancient Mariner. You will smile at the reference, but I have a secret. My obsession with the dangerous ocean comes from that poem. I don't fully understand what is driving me. I am a hardworking, practical person. But I also have a deep belief in the impossible that pulls me away from normal life and into the wild, unexplored regions.

But enough of that. Will I see you again after crossing massive oceans and returning via the southern tips of Africa or America? I shouldn't expect such a massive success, but I can't bear to think about failing. Please keep writing to me whenever you can. I might receive your letters exactly when I need them to boost my spirits. I love you very much. Remember me fondly if you never hear from me again.

Your affectionate brother,
Robert Walton
