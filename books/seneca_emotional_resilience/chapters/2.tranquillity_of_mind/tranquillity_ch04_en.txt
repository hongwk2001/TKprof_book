[Chapter 4]

My dear Serenus, to me, Athenodorus seems to have surrendered too completely to his times and run away too quickly. I will not deny that sometimes you have to retreat. But you should retreat slowly, without losing your honor or your military discipline as a soldier. Enemies respect people more and keep them safer when they negotiate with their weapons still in their hands. I believe this is what virtue and anyone who practices virtue should do.

If Fortune gains the upper hand and takes away your power to act, you should not just turn your back, throw away your weapons, and run looking for a hiding place. There is no place where Fortune cannot track you down. You should be careful about taking public office, but after deep thought, you must find a way to be useful to your country. If you cannot serve in the army, you should run for an honorable civilian office. If you have to live in a private position, you should become a lawyer. If you are condemned to silence, you should help your fellow citizens with quiet advice. If it is too dangerous to even go out in public, you should still be a good companion, a loyal friend, and a thoughtful guest in people's homes, at public shows, and at parties.

Let us say you lose your status as a citizen. Then you should at least fulfill your role as a human being. We bravely refuse to be locked inside the walls of a single city. We interact with all the countries of the world and declare ourselves citizens of the whole world so that we can have a wider stage to show our virtue. If the judge's bench is closed to you, or if you are banned from speaking to the public or running for office, then look away from Rome and look at the huge territories and many nations spread out before you. It is impossible that so many doors to your ambition are closed that not a single one is left open.

But make sure all these bans are not your own fault. You refuse to lead public affairs unless you can be a consul, a high-ranking official, or a top leader. That is as ridiculous as refusing to serve in the army unless you can be a general or a military tribune. Even if others stand on the front lines and fate places you in the third row with the older veterans, do your duty right where you are with your voice, your encouragement, your good example, and your spirit. Even if someone gets both hands cut off, they can still find a way to help their side in a battle just by standing their ground and cheering on their friends. You should do the same thing. Even if Fortune pulls you back from the front line, stand your ground and cheer on your friends. If someone covers your mouth, keep standing there and help your side in silence.

The service of a good citizen is never wasted. He does good just by being heard and seen, by his facial expressions, his gestures, his quiet determination, and the very way he walks. Some medicines help us not just by their taste and touch, but by their smell. In the same way, virtue spreads usefulness all around, even if it is hidden far away. Whether virtue moves freely and enjoys all its rights, or is barely allowed to show itself and has to lower its sails in a storm, whether it is quietly locked in a small room without work, or out in the open—no matter how it appears, virtue always does good.

The example of someone who knows how to rest nobly is definitely valuable. Therefore, whenever unexpected roadblocks or political situations keep you from living an active life, the best plan is to mix your free time with your work. No one is ever so completely blocked from everything that there is absolutely no room left for honorable action.
