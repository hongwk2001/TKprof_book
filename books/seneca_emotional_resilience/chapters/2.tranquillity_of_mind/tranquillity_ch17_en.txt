Chapter 17

If you try hard to hide your feelings and live a fake life just to look good to others, as many people do, without ever showing your true self, this also becomes a rich source of trouble. Constantly watching yourself is painful, and you will always be afraid of being caught doing something different from your usual habits. Actually, if we imagine that everyone looking at us is judging our real value, we can never feel at ease. Things happen often that strip away people's disguises, no matter how much they hate taking them off. Furthermore, even if all your hard work to hide yourself succeeds, life is neither happy nor safe if you must always wear a mask. There is great joy in honest straightforwardness, which is its own decoration and hides no part of its character.

However, even this kind of life, which hides nothing from anyone, runs some risk of being looked down on. Because there are people who despise whatever comes too close to them. But there is absolutely no danger that virtue will be looked down on when it appears right before our eyes. And it is much better to be made fun of for being simple than to carry the heavy burden of constant fakeness. Even so, we must keep a balance in this matter, because there is a big difference between living simply and living sloppily.

Furthermore, we must often pull back deep inside ourselves. Mixing with people who are different from us turns upside down everything we have put in order, wakes up feelings that were resting, and rubs against any weak or poorly healed wounds in our minds. Nevertheless, we should mix these two things properly and spend our lives taking turns between being alone and being in a crowd. The first will make us long for the company of people, and the second will make us long for time with ourselves, so one will cancel out the harm of the other. When you are tired of the crowd, being alone will heal you; and when you are tired of being alone, the crowd will heal you.

Also, we should not always keep our minds stretched tight with the same tension; sometimes we must relax our minds through entertainment. Socrates was not ashamed to play with little boys. Cato used to calm his tired mind with wine after working hard on matters of state. Scipio used to dance to the sound of music using his strong, soldier-like legs. He did not dance with the weak and limping steps that people use today—shaking their bodies more weakly than women even when just walking. Instead, he danced with manly leaps, the way the ancient people did during playtime and festivals, thinking it was perfectly fine even if his enemies saw him doing it.

The human mind must rest. After resting, the mind wakes up better and more energetic. You should not force crops out of rich soil. If you constantly force it to produce heavy harvests, its richness will soon run out. In the same way, endless hard work destroys the energy of our minds, but after a short rest and some comfort, the mind will get its strength back. Constant hard work causes a kind of numbness and dullness. People would not crave play and entertainment so much if these things did not have a natural charm. Of course, constantly indulging in them takes away all the mind's seriousness and strength. Sleep is also necessary to recover, but if you extend your sleep day and night, it soon becomes death. There is a big difference between holding something loosely and letting it go completely.

The creators of our laws set up festivals to publicly encourage people to have fun, believing it was necessary to take turns between work and play. As I mentioned before, great men used to give themselves a few days off every month, and some divided every single day into playtime and work time. For example, I remember that the great speaker Asinius Pollio did not do any work after the tenth hour (around 4 PM). He wouldn't even read letters after that time just in case a new problem came up. Instead, during those two hours, he would shake off all the tiredness he had built up during the day. Some people take a break in the middle of the day and leave only light work for the afternoon. Our ancestors also made a rule that no new issues could be brought up in the Senate after the tenth hour. Soldiers take turns keeping watch, and those who have just come back from real fighting are allowed to sleep all night without being disturbed.

We must humor our minds and allow them to rest from time to time. Rest acts like food for the mind and brings back its strength. Taking a walk outside is also good. The open sky and fresh breeze will lift and refresh our spirits. Sometimes we get our strength back by taking a carriage ride, traveling, changing the air, or having meals with people and drinking a little more wine than usual. Sometimes we even need to drink until we are drunk—not so much that we drown in wine, but just enough to be completely soaked. This is because wine acts as a medicine for sadness, washing away worries and driving them out from the bottom of our minds, just like it cures some sicknesses.

The creator of wine is called 'Liber' (The Liberator), not because he gave our tongues the freedom to speak, but because he frees the mind from the chains of worry. He sets the mind free, gives it life, and makes it braver in everything it tries to do. However, moderation is good in both freedom and wine. It is believed that Solon and Arcesilaus drank a lot. Cato was accused of being drunk. But anyone who uses this to criticize Cato will find it easier to turn that criticism into a compliment than to prove Cato actually did something wrong. Still, because we fear the mind might pick up bad habits, we shouldn't drink often. But sometimes, we must force our minds into playfulness and honesty, throwing away our boring sobriety for a little while.

That is, if we believe the Greek poet who said, "Sometimes it is a joy to be mad." Also, Plato always knocked on the door of poetry in vain when he was completely sober. Or if we trust Aristotle, there has never been a great genius without a little bit of madness. The mind cannot use high and noble language—higher than the words of the ordinary crowd—unless it is excited. When the mind kicks away the boring surroundings of everyday habits and rises up, full of divine fire, only then can it sing a song too grand for mortal lips. As long as the mind stays inside itself, it cannot reach any brilliant heights. It must step off the normal path that everyone else takes, drive itself into a frenzy, chew on its bit, and gallop away, lifting its rider to heights it would have been too scared to climb alone.

My dear Serenus, I have now explained to you what things can protect the mind's peace, what can bring our peace back, and what can block the bad habits that secretly destroy it. But always remember this: unless we guard our shaking minds with intense and constant care, none of these things are strong enough to let us keep such a short-lived blessing.
