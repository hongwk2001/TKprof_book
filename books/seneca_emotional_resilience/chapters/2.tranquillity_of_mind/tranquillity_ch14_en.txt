Chapter 14

Furthermore, we should develop an easygoing attitude and not become too attached to the role fate has given us. Instead, we should willingly move into whatever new situation chance leads us to, and not fear changes in our purpose or our position in life. However, we must not let ourselves be controlled by changing moods, because changing moods are the biggest enemy of a peaceful mind. Stubbornness, from which fate often forces out some compromise, naturally makes a person anxious and unhappy. But a changing mood, which can never control itself, is even worse. Both of these traits—refusing to change anything, and being unhappy with everything—are enemies of peace.

Our minds should always stop staring at outside things and turn to look at themselves. Let your mind trust itself, take joy in itself, and admire its own accomplishments. Avoid other people's business as much as possible and devote yourself only to yourself. Do not let your mind feel a sense of loss, and find a good way to look at even bad situations.

Zeno, the founder of our Stoic school, heard the news of a shipwreck where he lost all his property. He just said, "Fate is ordering me to travel lighter on the path of philosophy." A tyrant threatened Theodorus not only with execution but also by saying he wouldn't even give him a funeral. Theodorus answered, "Do whatever you want. My half-pint of blood is in your power. But as for the funeral, you are truly foolish if you think I care whether I rot above ground or below it."

Julius Kanus was a specially great man. Even the fact that he was born in our time cannot stop us from admiring him. He had a long argument with Gaius Caesar (Caligula). As he was leaving, Gaius, who was as cruel as the ancient Greek tyrant Phalaris, said to him, "So you don't trick yourself with any foolish hopes, I have ordered you to be executed." Kanus answered, "Thank you, most excellent emperor."

I am not sure exactly what he meant, but several ways to explain his actions come to mind. He might have wanted to scold the emperor, showing how terrible the emperor's cruelty was if death actually became a favor. Or maybe he was mocking the emperor's everyday madness. After all, even people whose children were killed and whose property was taken often thanked the emperor. Or, he may have happily accepted death, seeing it as freedom. Whatever he meant, it was a brave and generous answer.

Someone might say that after this, Gaius might have let him live. But Kanus wasn't afraid of that. It was well known how strictly Gaius carried out such orders. Kanus spent the ten days before his execution without showing any sadness at all. The way he spoke and acted, and how peaceful he was, is truly amazing.

When the guard in charge of a group of people to be executed ordered Kanus to join the others, Kanus was playing checkers. Upon being called, he counted his pieces and told the person he was playing with, "Be careful not to lie after I die and say you won." Then, he turned to the guard and said, "Please be a witness that I am one piece ahead of him." Kanus didn't just play a game on the board; he treated his own death like a lighthearted game.

His friends were sad that they were about to lose such a great man. He told his friends there was no reason to be sad. His friends had been asking if the soul lives forever, and he answered that he would find out very soon. He never stopped searching for the truth until his final moment, using his own death as a topic of discussion. His philosophy teacher went with him. When they got close to the hill where sacrifices were offered every day to our god Caesar, the teacher asked Kanus what he was thinking about and what he was imagining.

Kanus answered, "In that final moment that passes the fastest, I have decided to watch and see if my soul can actually feel itself leaving my body." And he promised that if he found anything out, he would come to his friends and tell them what the condition of departed souls is. Look, here was peace right in the middle of a storm. Here was a soul worthy of eternal life. He used his own fate as proof of the truth. At the final step of his life, even as his breath stopped, he did an experiment. Not only did he continue learning until he died, but he learned something from death itself. No one has ever pushed a philosopher's life further than this. I will not rush to finish the story of a great man who deserves so much respect. Oh, most noble heart, the greatest among all of Gaius's victims, I will tell your story to all future generations.
