Chapter 11

My words here apply only to imperfect, ordinary, and unsettled people. They do not apply to the wise man, who does not need to walk with careful and cautious steps. The wise man is so full of self-confidence that he does not hesitate to face fate directly and will never give in to it. Also, he has no reason to fear fate at all. This is because he considers his possessions, wealth, and high office—and even his body, his eyes, his hands, and everything that makes our lives precious, even himself—as uncertain things that can be lost at any time. He lives as if he has borrowed them, always ready to return them cheerfully whenever the owner asks for them back.

But this does not mean the wise man thinks of himself as cheap. He knows he is not his own, but he performs all his duties carefully and thoughtfully, just like a sincere and honest person taking care of someone else's property. When ordered to give these things up, he will not complain about fate. He will say, "Thank you for the things I have possessed. I managed your property well and increased it a lot. But since you order it, I will gladly and thankfully return it. If you still want me to keep anything of yours, I will keep it for you. If you have other plans, I will give back everything into your hands—my silver, my house, and everything in it."

If nature wants to take back what she once trusted us with, let us say the same to her. "Take back my soul, which is better than when you gave it to me. I will not avoid or hesitate. What you gave me before I could even think, I am now ready to return of my own free will. Take me."

There can be no hardship in returning to the place you came from. A person who does not know how to die well can never live well. Therefore, we should take away the original value we place on this thing called life and consider the breath of life as something cheap.

Cicero once said, "We dislike a gladiator who tries to save his life by any means, but we look favorably on a gladiator who fights bravely and recklessly, completely risking his life." You can be sure that the exact same thing happens to us. We often die because we are afraid of death. Fate, which views our lives as an arena show for its own entertainment, says, "You cowardly and base creature, I will not spare you. Because you don't know how to offer your neck to the blade, you will be stabbed, torn, and suffer more wounds. But you, who accept the blow calmly without turning your head away or trying to block it with your hands, will live longer and die with less pain."

A person who fears death can never act like someone who is truly alive. But someone who knows that this fate was given to them the moment they were born will live according to it. Through this mental toughness, they gain the advantage that nothing will ever happen to them unexpectedly. They expect that anything that can happen will happen to them, and by looking ahead, they remove the poison from all bad things. Misfortune makes no difference to someone who expects it and is ready to meet it. Bad things only fall harshly on those who live without thinking and care only about happiness.

Sickness, captivity, disaster, and fire are never unexpected. I always knew what messy companions nature tied me to. I have often heard the sound of weeping for the dead in my neighborhood. The torches and candles carried in front of the coffin of someone who died before their time have often passed my door. The loud crash of falling buildings has often rung in my ears. The darkness of night has taken away many people I grew close to in the Roman forum, the Senate, and in society, breaking the hands that were firmly held in friendship. If the dangers that have always circled around me finally attack me, I should not be surprised at all.

Most people never even think about storms when they set sail. Even if it is from a bad writer, I will not be ashamed to quote a good saying. Publilius was a more powerful writer than any comic or tragic author. When he moved past silly jokes and lines meant for the crowd, he wrote many verses that are too noble for comedy and fit perfectly in tragedy. Among them is this line: "The misfortune that happened to someone else can happen to any of us."

If a person takes these words to heart and always looks at all the overflowing misfortunes of others, remembering that there is no guarantee those things won't happen to them, they will be fully armed against misfortune long before it attacks. Training your mind to endure danger only after the danger has arrived is too late. You might say, "I didn't know this would happen," or "I never believed this could happen." But there is absolutely no reason it shouldn't happen. Poverty, hunger, and begging always follow right behind wealth. Behind the red robes, the augur's staff, and all the high offices of the nobles, there are rags, banishment, brands of shame, thousands of disgraces, and total rejection waiting. Next to every kingdom, ruin, crushing defeat, tyrants, and butchers are always ready. And these things are not separated by long periods of time. There is only an hour's gap between sitting on a throne and grabbing someone else's knees to beg for mercy.

So, keep in mind that every position in life is temporary, and what has happened to anyone can happen to you. You may be rich, but you are not richer than Pompeius. Yet when Gaius Caesar (Caligula), his old relative and new master, took Pompeius's house and opened up Caesar's house to him, Pompeius did not even have bread and water. Even though he owned so many rivers that started and flowed inside his lands, he had to beg for a few drops of water. While his heir was arranging a public funeral for a man who didn't have enough food, Pompeius died of hunger and thirst in his relative's palace.

You may have held high offices, but they were never as important, surprising, or wide-reaching as those held by Sejanus, the emperor's closest aide. Yet, on the very day the Senate disgraced him, the crowd tore him to pieces. Of the man who had received everything gods and humans could give, the executioner couldn't find a single piece of his body big enough to drag to the Tiber River.

You may be a king. I won't tell you to look at the example of Croesus, who had huge wealth but had to watch his own funeral pyre be lit and put out while he was still alive, living longer than not only his kingdom but his own death. I also won't tell you to look at Jugurtha, whom the Romans saw as a prisoner in the exact same year they had feared him. We have seen King Ptolemy of Africa and King Mithridates of Armenia being watched by Gaius's guards. The first was exiled, and the second chose exile to make it more honorable. In this world of constant upside-down changes, if you don't expect that whatever can happen will happen to you, you are giving bad luck the power to attack you. But a person who looks ahead and sees it coming can destroy that power.
