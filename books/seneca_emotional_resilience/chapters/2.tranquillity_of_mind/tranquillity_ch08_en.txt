[Chapter 8]

Now let us move on to property, the most fertile source of human sadness. If you compare all the other misfortunes we suffer, like death, sickness, fear, regret, and the pain of hard work, to the misery our money causes us, the pain of money will easily outweigh all the rest.

Therefore, think about how much less sad it is to have never had money than to lose it. When we realize this, we will understand that the poorer we are, the less we have to lose, and therefore the less pain will torture us. If you think rich people handle financial losses more bravely than poor people, you are mistaken. A wound causes the same amount of pain to a massive body as it does to a tiny one. Bion had a great saying: when hair gets pulled out, it hurts exactly the same whether you have a full head of hair or you are bald. You can be certain that this is true for both the rich and the poor; their pain is exactly the same. Money is superglued to both groups, and neither can rip it away without feeling it. But as I said, it is much easier and more tolerable not to get property in the first place than to lose it. Therefore, you will notice that people who have never been smiled upon by Fortune are actually more cheerful than those who have been abandoned by her.

Diogenes, a man of infinite spirit, realized this. He made sure that absolutely nothing could be taken from him. You can call this safety from loss poverty, desperation, need, or any other insulting name you want. But unless you can find me someone else who has absolutely nothing to lose, I will consider that man happy. Unless I am wrong, being the only person who cannot be hurt among millions of greedy people, scammers, and robbers is a privilege fit for a king. If anyone doubts Diogenes' happiness, they might as well doubt whether the immortal gods are happy just because they do not own farms, gardens, expensive estates rented to strangers, or massive loans in the financial markets.

If you look at wealth with eyes full of wonder, you should be ashamed of yourself. Look at the universe. You will see gods who have no property at all, giving everything away while keeping nothing for themselves. The person who strips away all accidental decorations is not a poor man; he is exactly like the immortal gods. Do not call Demetrius, Pompey's freed slave, a happier man. He was not embarrassed to be richer than Pompey himself, and he received a daily list of his slaves like a general getting an army roster. But for a long time, the only wealth he actually deserved was a single pair of underlings and a room just slightly bigger than what the other slaves had.

On the other hand, Diogenes' only slave ran away from him. When they found the runaway slave, Diogenes decided he was not even worth bringing back. He said, "If Manes can live without Diogenes, it would be disgraceful if Diogenes could not live without Manes." To me, it sounds like he was saying, "Fortune, mind your own business. Diogenes has nothing left that belongs to you. My slave did not run away; he left me as a free man."

A household full of slaves requires food and clothes. You have to feed all those hungry mouths. We have to buy clothes for them, watch their hands because they are prone to stealing, and rely on the services of people who curse us while they cry. The person who owes nothing to anyone, except for the things that are easiest to give up, is so much happier!

But since we do not have this kind of mental strength, we should at least shrink the size of our property so we are less exposed to Fortune's attacks. A person who can hide inside his own armor is much better suited for war than a massive giant whose huge body sticks out everywhere, making him an easy target. The absolute best amount of property to have is enough to keep us out of poverty, but not much more than that.
