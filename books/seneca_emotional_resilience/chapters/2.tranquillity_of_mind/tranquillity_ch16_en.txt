Chapter 16

Now, let's move to a topic that naturally makes us sad and anxious for a good reason: when good people come to a miserable end. When Socrates is forced to die in prison, when the honest and clean Roman politician Rutilius must live in exile, when Pompeius and Cicero have to offer their necks to the swords of their own followers, and when the great Cato, a living example of virtue, falls on his own sword and tears apart both himself and the republic at the same time—we cannot help but feel sad that fate hands out its gifts so unfairly. What can a good person possibly hope to get when they see the best people meeting the worst fates?

Well, look at how each of them endured their fate. If they endured it bravely, then long in your heart for courage as great as theirs. If they died like cowards, acting weak, then nothing was lost. Either they deserved you admiring their courage, or they didn't deserve you wanting to imitate their cowardice. What could be more shameful than the fact that great men dying bravely makes other people cowardly?

Let us praise a person who deserves such constant praise, and say: "The braver you are, the happier you are! You have escaped all accidents, jealousy, and disease. You have escaped from prison. The gods did not think you deserved bad luck; rather, they thought fate no longer deserved to have power over you." But if someone shrinks back at the moment of death and longs for life, we must pull them down.

I will never cry for someone who dies cheerfully, nor will I cry for someone who dies crying. The first wipes away my tears, and the second, by crying his own tears, makes himself unworthy of anyone else crying for him. I will not cry for Hercules because he was burned alive, or for Regulus because he was pierced by many nails, or for Cato because he tore open his own wounds a second time. All of these men discovered a way to earn immortality by sacrificing just a short amount of time, and through death, they gained eternal life.
