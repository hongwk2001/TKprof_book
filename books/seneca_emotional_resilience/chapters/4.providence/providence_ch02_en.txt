[Chapter 2]

There is a reason why many things turn out badly for good men. No evil can actually happen to a good man, because opposites cannot mix. Just as rivers, rain, and mineral springs do not alter the taste of the sea, adversity does not affect the mind of a brave man. His mind maintains its balance and colors everything that happens, because it is more powerful than external circumstances.

This does not mean he feels no pain, but he conquers it. He calmly rises above attacks, viewing all misfortunes as tests of his firmness. Anyone with honorable ambition longs for meaningful work and is eager to do his duty despite danger. For a hard-working man, idleness is a punishment.

Look at athletes who focus on their physical strength. They fight the strongest opponents and demand their trainers use full force during practice. They endure blows and maltreatment. If they cannot find a single match, they fight several at once. Their strength and courage fade without an opponent. They can only prove how strong they are by showing how much they can endure.

Good men should act the same way. Do not fear troubles and difficulties, and do not complain about your fate. Accept whatever happens and force it to become a blessing. It does not matter what you bear, but how you bear it.

Notice how differently fathers and mothers raise their children. Fathers wake them early for tasks, keep them busy even on holidays, and push them until they sweat or even cry. Mothers, however, want to cuddle them, keep them out of the sun, and never want them to struggle or work.

God has a fatherly mind toward good men and loves them with a strong spirit. He says, "Let them be exercised by labors, sufferings, and losses, so they may gather true strength." People who have had too much easy living break down not just from hard work, but from simple movement and their own weight. Unbroken prosperity cannot handle a single blow. But a man who constantly fights his misfortunes develops a thicker skin. He yields to no disaster, and even if he falls, he keeps fighting on his knees.

You may wonder why God appoints fortune as an adversary to the good men He loves. He wants them to achieve the highest goodness. I am not surprised if the gods sometimes enjoy watching great men struggle with misfortune. We are often delighted when a brave young man fights off a wild beast or stands his ground against a lion. The nobler the man, the more attractive the sight. But these are just human amusements.

A sight truly worthy of a god's attention is a brave man matched against evil fortune, especially if he chose the challenge. Jupiter himself could find no nobler spectacle on earth than Cato. After his party was repeatedly defeated, Cato still stood upright among the ruins of the republic.

He said, "Even if one man controls everything, if his legions guard the land and his fleets guard the sea, and Caesar's soldiers surround the city gates, Cato has a way out. With one hand, he will open a wide path to freedom. His sword, kept clean and innocent even in civil war, will perform a noble deed. It will give Cato the freedom it could not give his country. Begin the work you have contemplated so long, my soul, and leave the world of men. Petreius and Juba have already fallen by each other's hands. That was a brave pact with fate, but not fitting for my greatness. It is as disgraceful for Cato to beg for his death as it would be to beg for his life."

The gods must have watched with great joy as Cato arranged the escape of others and pursued his studies on his final night. He drove the sword into his own breast, taking his holy life. I think his first wound was not fatal because the gods wanted to see his courage in action again. It takes a greater mind to return to death a second time. The gods could not fail to watch their pupil leave life in such a noble way. Men are raised to the level of gods by a death that is admired even by those who fear them.
