[Chapter 5]

Add to this that it is an advantage for everyone that the best men should be on active duty and perform hard work. God has the same purpose as the wise man. He wants to prove that the things the crowd covets and dreads are neither good nor bad in themselves. If He only bestows them on good men, it will seem they are good things. If He only inflicts them on bad men, it will seem they are bad things. Blindness would be a curse if no one lost their eyes except those who deserved it, so let innocent men be doomed to darkness. Riches are not a true good. Therefore, let a pander possess them, so that men who consecrate money in the temple may see the same money in a brothel. God can discredit objects of desire most effectively by bestowing them on the worst men and keeping them from the best.

You might say it is unjust that a good man should be enfeebled, chained, or stabbed, while bad men walk around safely. Yet it is not considered unjust that brave men must bear arms, spend nights in camps, and stand guard with bandaged wounds, while cowards and profligates live at ease in the city. It is also not considered unjust that noble maidens must be woken at night to perform religious services, while fallen women sleep soundly. Hard work calls for the best men. The Senate often debates all day while scoundrels amuse themselves, lurk in taverns, or enjoy pleasant company.

The same thing happens in the great commonwealth of the world. Good men labor and spend themselves of their free will. They are not dragged along by fortune, but follow her and match her steps. If they knew how, they would outstrip her. I remember hearing a spirited saying from Demetrius, the stoutest-hearted of men. He said, "Immortal Gods, my only complaint is that you did not make your will known to me earlier. I would have gladly entered the life I have now been called to. If you want to take my children, I brought them up for you. If you want a part of my body, take it. It is no great thing to offer, as I will soon be done with the whole of it. If you want my life, there is no reason for me to hesitate to return what you gave me. Whatever you ask, you shall receive with my good will. I would rather give it than be forced to hand it over. You did not need to take it away, because you could have received it from me. Yet even now you cannot truly take anything from me, because you cannot rob a man unless he resists."

I am not constrained to do anything. I suffer nothing against my will. I am not God's slave, but His willing follower. I am even more willing because I know that everything is ordained by an eternal law. Destiny guides us, and the length of every man's life is decided at birth. Every cause depends on an earlier cause. One long chain of destiny decides all public and private things. Therefore, we must patiently endure everything, because events come by a regular law, not by chance. It has long been settled what will make you rejoice and what will make you weep. Although individual lives seem different, they all add up to the same thing. We soon perish, and the gifts we receive soon perish. We have no reason to be angry. We have no reason to lament. We are prepared for our fate. Let nature do what she wants with our bodies. Let us be cheerful whatever happens, and bravely reflect that nothing of our own truly perishes.

The duty of a good man is to submit himself to fate. It is a great consolation to be swept away along with the entire universe. The law that dictates how we must live and die is laid upon the gods as well. One unchangeable stream carries both men and gods. The creator and ruler of the universe gave laws to the fates, but He is also guided by them. He commanded only once, and now He always obeys.

You might ask why God was so unjust in distributing fate, assigning poverty, wounds, and early deaths to good men. A workman cannot alter his materials. Some qualities cannot be separated from others. Dull minds are made of sluggish elements. It requires stronger material to form a man worthy of careful attention. His path will not be straightforward. He must go up and down, be tossed about, and guide his ship through troubled waters. He must make his way in spite of fortune. He will face hard things that he must soften, and rough things that he must smooth. Fire tests gold, and misfortune tests brave men. Look how high virtue has to climb, and you will see it has no safe path.

The path is steep at first. The strong horses can barely crawl along. The middle of the path goes through the highest sky, where looking down at the earth and sea brings terror. The end of the path is a steep downhill slope that needs careful steering, where even the ocean depths tremble as the sun plunges down.

When the brave young Phaethon heard this, he said, "I have no problem with the road. I will climb it. It is worth going through these places, even if I fall." His father tried to scare him by warning him of the fierce beasts in the sky, like the Bull and the Lion, but Phaethon just said, "Harness the chariot. The things you use to scare me only encourage me. I want to stand where the Sun himself trembles to stand." It is the way of cowards to follow the safe track. Courage loves a lofty path.
