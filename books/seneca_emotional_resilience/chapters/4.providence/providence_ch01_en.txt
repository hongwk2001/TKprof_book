[Chapter 1]

You have asked me, Lucilius, why so many evils happen to good men if the world is ruled by providence. At present, it is obvious that a guardian maintains this great universe. The movements of the stars do not depend on accidental impulses. Objects moved by chance often fall into confusion and soon stumble. Meanwhile, this swift and safe movement goes on, governed by eternal law. It carries so many things on sea and land, and so many brilliant lights shining in order in the skies. This regularity does not belong to matter moving at random. Particles brought together by chance could not arrange themselves with such art. They make the heavy earth remain unmoved while the heavens hasten around it. They make the seas pour into the valleys to temper the climate of the land, without overflowing from the rivers that feed them. They cause huge plants to grow from tiny seeds.

Even phenomena that seem confused and irregular, like showers of rain, lightning, volcanic eruptions, and earthquakes, do not happen without reason. Though they occur suddenly, they have their causes. The same applies to things that amaze us, such as warm springs in the sea and new islands rising in the ocean. Anyone who watches the tide retreat and return will believe it obeys a hidden law. The waves contract and drive inwards, then burst forth to regain their bed, always waxing and waning in regular proportion. They follow the pull of the moon, which controls the ocean.

I will readily reconcile you to gods who are excellent to excellent men. The nature of the universe never allows good to be injured by good. A friendship exists between good men and the gods, brought about by virtue. More than friendship, it is a relationship and likeness. The good man differs from a god only in time. He is a pupil, rival, and true offspring of God. His glorious parent trains him severely, insisting strictly on virtuous conduct, just as strict fathers do.

When you see good men toiling, sweating, and struggling upwards, while bad men run riot in pleasure, think about how families operate. We are pleased by modesty in our sons, but we tolerate forwardness in our slaves. We hold our sons in check with a strict rule, while we encourage the boldness of slaves. Be sure that God acts in the same way. He does not pet the good man. He tries him, hardens him, and fits him for Himself.
