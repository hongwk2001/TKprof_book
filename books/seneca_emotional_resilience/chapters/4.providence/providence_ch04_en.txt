[Chapter 4]

Prosperity comes to the mob and to low-minded men just as it does to great men. But only great men can conquer the disasters and terrors of mortal life. To always be prosperous and pass through life without mental distress is to remain ignorant of half of nature. You may be a great man, but I cannot know it if fortune gives you no chance to show your virtue. You have entered the arena, but no one else is there. You have the crown, but not the victory. I do not congratulate you as a brave man, but merely as someone who has gained a title. You have gained dignity, but that is all. I think you are unhappy because you have never been unhappy. You have passed through life without meeting an opponent. No one will know your powers, not even you.

A man cannot know himself without a trial. No one ever learned what he could do without putting himself to the test. Because of this, many men have willingly exposed themselves to misfortunes that no longer came their way. They sought a chance to make their virtue shine before the world, rather than letting it get lost in darkness. Great men often rejoice at the crosses of fortune just as brave soldiers rejoice at wars. I once heard a gladiator named Triumphus complaining about the lack of games during the reign of Tiberius Caesar. He said, "What a glorious time has passed."

Valor is greedy for danger. It thinks only of its goal, not of what it will suffer, because the suffering is part of its glory. Soldiers pride themselves on their wounds and joyously display the blood on their armor. A soldier who returns unwounded may have fought bravely, but the one who returns wounded is admired more. God favors those He wishes to receive the highest honors by giving them the chance to perform difficult, courageous deeds. You can only judge a pilot in a storm, and a soldier in a battle.

I cannot know if you could endure poverty with a great spirit if you overflow with riches. I cannot tell if you could firmly handle disgrace, dishonor, and public hatred if you grow old surrounded by applause. I cannot know how calmly you would endure losing children if you see all your children around you. I have heard you console others, but I need to see if you can console yourself and forbid yourself to grieve. Do not dread the things the gods use as spurs to our minds. Misfortune is virtue's opportunity.

The truly unhappy men are those stupefied by too much enjoyment, kept becalmed in a quiet sea of sluggish contentment. Whatever happens to them will feel strange. Misfortunes press hardest on those who are unaccustomed to them. The yoke feels heavy to a tender neck. A new recruit turns pale at the thought of a wound, but a veteran looks boldly at his own flowing blood because he knows he has often won victories after losing blood.

In the same way, God hardens, reviews, and exercises those He loves. Those He seems to indulge and spare are simply being kept unprepared for their coming misfortunes. You are mistaken if you think anyone is exempt from misfortune. The man who has prospered for a long time will eventually get his share. Those who seem to have been spared have merely had their troubles delayed.

There is a reason God afflicts the best men with bad health, sorrow, or other troubles. In an army, the most hazardous tasks are given to the bravest soldiers. A general sends his best troops to ambush the enemy at night, reconnoiter the line of march, or drive hostile garrisons from strongholds. None of these soldiers say the general dealt harshly with them. They say he judged them well. Those who are asked to suffer things that make the weak cry should also say, "God has thought us worthy to test how much human nature can endure."

Avoid luxury and soft enjoyment. These things soften the mind and leave men unconscious, as if in continual drunkenness, unless something reminds them of our shared human condition. A man who has always been guarded from the wind by glass windows, whose feet are warmed by constant heat, and whose rooms are heated by warm air beneath the floor, cannot face a gentle breeze without danger. All excess is harmful, but excess comfort is the most harmful of all. It affects the brain, leads to vain imaginings, and clouds the difference between truth and falsehood. It is better to endure continual misfortune with virtue by your side than to burst with an endless feast of good things. An overloaded stomach tears apart, while death treats starvation more gently.

The gods deal with good men just as schoolmasters deal with their pupils. They demand the most work from those who show the most promise. The Spartans do indeed love their children when they test their mettle with public flogging. Their own fathers urge them to endure the rod bravely, and when they are torn and half dead, they ask them to offer their wounded skin for more blows. We should not wonder if God severely tests noble spirits. There is no easy proof of virtue.

Fortune lashes and mangles us, and we must endure it. It is not cruelty, but a struggle. The more often we fight, the braver we will become. The strongest part of the body is the part used most frequently. We must trust ourselves to fortune so she can harden us against herself. By degrees, she will make us a match for her. Familiarity with danger teaches us to despise it. The bodies of sailors are hardened by the sea, and the hands of farmers by work. Soldiers have strong arms to throw spears, and runners have active legs. The part you exercise becomes the strongest. Through endurance, the mind learns to despise the power of misfortune.

You can see what endurance does by looking at tribes that are naked and made stronger by poverty. Look at the nations beyond the Roman Empire, like the Germans and the nomad tribes along the Danube. They suffer through eternal winter in a dismal climate. The barren soil barely feeds them. They keep off the rain with leaves or thatch, run across frozen marshes, and hunt wild beasts for food. You should not think they are unhappy. There is no unhappiness in what has become a natural habit. Men slowly find pleasure in doing what necessity first forced them to do. They have no homes except where weariness stops them for the day. They must find their coarse food with their own hands. The harsh climate is terrible, and they wear no clothes. This is the normal life for all these races.

You should not wonder that good men are shaken so they can be strengthened. A tree that is never blown by the wind is not firm or strong. The very act of being shaken stiffens it and forces it to plant its roots more securely. Trees that grow in sheltered valleys are brittle. It is to the advantage of good men to live amidst alarms. This teaches them to patiently bear what is only evil to those who endure it poorly.
