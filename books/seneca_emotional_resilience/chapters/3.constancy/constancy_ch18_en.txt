[Chapter 18]

Among all the other vices that overflowed in the Emperor Gaius Caesar, he was obsessed with a strange, arrogant desire to mark everyone with some kind of ridiculous insult. This was highly ironic, because he himself was the perfect subject for mockery. The paleness that proved his madness was ugly, and a savage glare lurked under his old woman's brow. His head was horribly misshapen, bald, and dotted with just a few carefully arranged hairs. Add to this a neck thick with bristles, thin legs, and monstrous feet.

It would take forever if I tried to list all the insults he heaped on his parents, his ancestors, and people from every class of society. Therefore, I will just mention the ones that ultimately destroyed him.

One of his closest friends was Valerius Asiaticus, a proud-spirited man who would never quietly endure another person's insults. At a drinking party—which in this case meant a public gathering—Caesar loudly ridiculed the way Asiaticus's wife behaved in bed. Good gods! Imagine a man having to hear that the emperor knew this, and then having the emperor publicly describe the adultery and his own disappointment to the woman's husband. It does not matter if the victim is a man of consular rank or a supposed friend. This is intolerable.

Then there was Chaerea, a military tribune. He had a weak voice that did not match his courage. It sounded feeble and a little suspicious unless you actually knew about his military achievements. When Chaerea asked for the daily watchword, Caesar gave him "Venus" one day and "Priapus" the next. By using different terms, the emperor constantly implied that the hardened soldier was effeminate. Keep in mind that Caesar was doing this while dressed in sheer clothes, wearing sandals and heavy jewelry.

Eventually, Caesar pushed Chaerea to draw his sword so that he would never have to ask for the watchword again. Chaerea was the first of the conspirators to raise his hand, and he cut straight through Caesar's neck with a single blow. After that, many other swords—wielded by men who had public or private insults to avenge—were thrust into his body. However, the man who struck the first blow was the exact one Caesar thought was the least manly of them all.

Caesar interpreted everything as an insult. This is not surprising, because those who are the most eager to insult others are usually the least able to endure insults themselves. He flew into a rage when Herennius Macer greeted him as "Gaius." Even the chief centurion of his veterans did not get away safely for calling him "Caligula." The emperor had been born in a military camp and raised as the child of the legions, so he had been called that name his whole life. It was the only name the troops really knew him by. But after he became emperor, he considered "Caligula" a reproach and a dishonor.

Let people with wounded spirits console themselves with this thought. Even if your own easygoing temper decides not to take revenge, someone else will eventually punish the impertinent, proud, and insulting man. Those vices never stop at just one victim or one single offensive act.

Let us look instead at the examples of people whose endurance we admire. Think of Socrates, who calmly accepted the public jokes of the comedians. He laughed just as hard at their plays as he did when his wife, Xanthippe, dumped a bucket of dirty water over his head. When someone insulted Antisthenes by pointing out that his mother was a barbarian from Thrace, he simply replied that the mother of the gods also came from Mount Ida.
