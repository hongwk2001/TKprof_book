[Chapter 10]
Now that we have finished the first part of our topic, let us move on to the second part. Here, we will use some of our own arguments but mostly rely on standard Stoic ideas to prove that the wise man cannot be insulted.

An insult is a lighter form of an injury. It is something we should just complain about and move on from rather than try to get revenge for. Even the law does not consider insults worthy of any special punishment. The feeling of being insulted comes from a petty mind that shrinks back whenever someone treats it with disrespect. People say things like, "He invited everyone else but did not invite me to his house today," or "He arrogantly turned his head away," or "He openly laughed when I was speaking," or "At the dinner party, he sat me in the lowest seat instead of the seat of honor." These kinds of complaints are just the whining of an easily bruised ego.

These things mostly bother people who are rich and pampered. People who face real hardships do not have the time to care about such small slights. People who have weak and girlish characters, and who get lost in imaginary problems because they have never experienced a real injury, get angry over these small things because they are too lazy. Either way, the vast majority of these small slights are just misunderstandings.

Therefore, a person who gets angry over an insult shows that they lack both good sense and self-confidence. They are sure they are being ignored, and that annoyance makes them feel like their value has dropped, causing them to shrink back and step down to a lower position. But the wise man is ignored by no one. He knows his own greatness, he is certain that he has not given anyone the power to shake him, and he completely ignores things that should be called mental anxiety rather than actual pain. He does not even have to "overcome" insults because he does not even feel them in the first place.

Other things, like physical pain or weakness, losing a friend or a child, or the ruin of his country because of war, can hit the wise man without shaking his core principles. I am not saying that the wise man feels no emotion at all toward these things. We do not pretend that the wise man is as hard as stone or iron. True virtue is always aware of the fact that it is enduring something. The wise man takes a few hits, but once he takes a hit, he rises above it, heals it, and puts an end to it. On the other hand, he does not even feel those small insults. He does not even need to use his usual firmness to endure insults. He completely ignores insults or thinks they are only worth laughing at.
