[Chapter 5]
If you agree, my dear Serenus, let us clearly separate an injury from an insult. An injury is naturally much more serious. An insult is less important. It only bothers people who have thin skin because it makes them angry without actually causing any real harm. But the human mind is often so weak that many people think nothing is worse than an insult. You can even find slaves who say they would rather be whipped than slapped in the face, or who think it is easier to endure beatings and death than to hear insulting words.

We have reached such a foolish point that we suffer not just from the pain itself, but even from the thought of pain. We act just like children who get terrified when they see the dark, an ugly mask, or a distorted face. Children cry when they hear words they do not like or see certain gestures, and they shrink back in fear because of their mistaken panic.

The goal of an injury is to do evil to someone. However, there is no room for evil in wisdom. To a wise man, the only true evil is doing something bad or shameful, and that kind of evil cannot enter a mind filled with virtue and honor. If there can be no injury without evil, and no evil without doing something bad, then it means that no injury can ever reach the wise man. For an injury to happen, someone must suffer an evil. But since a wise man cannot suffer any evil, an injury has no effect on him.

Every injury brings down the person who suffers it. No one can receive an injury without losing something, whether it is their dignity, a part of their body, or something they own. But the wise man loses nothing. He has invested everything inside himself, and he has trusted nothing to the goddess of Fortune. His true wealth is perfectly safe. He is satisfied with his virtue, and virtue does not need any decorations that luck brings. Therefore, his virtue can never increase or decrease. It has already reached the absolute top, so there is no room to add anything more. The goddess of Fortune can only take away what she has given. Since she did not give the wise man his virtue in the first place, she can never take it away.

Virtue is free and unbroken. It does not shake or tremble. Virtue is hardened so strongly against bad luck that it cannot even be bent, let alone defeated. Virtue looks calmly at the tortures prepared for it, without changing its expression at all, whether it faces misery or pleasure.

Therefore, the wise man never loses anything that he would truly miss. He belongs completely to virtue, and he can never be separated from it. He only enjoys all other outside things according to the changes of luck, and he knows very well that no one cries over losing something that was never truly theirs to begin with. As long as the wise man's virtue is safe, his true possessions are also safe. So if an injury cannot destroy the wise man's true possessions, it is absolutely impossible to injure him.

When Demetrius, who was called the destroyer of cities, captured the city of Megara, he asked the philosopher Stilbo if he had lost anything. Stilbo answered, "No. I have all my property with me." He said this even though his inherited wealth was all stolen, his daughters were attacked by the enemy soldiers, and his country fell under foreign control. Demetrius asked this question from a high throne, surrounded by the spears of his victorious army. But Stilbo took the victory right out of the king's hands. He proved that even though the city fell, he himself was not conquered and was completely safe. He had true wealth that no one could ever touch.

Stilbo did not think of the things that were being stolen and scattered as his own property. He saw them as simple things that come and go depending on the changes of luck, so he did not love them as if they were his own. The ownership of everything that comes from the outside world is always slippery and unstable.
