[Chapter 16]

If even Epicurus—the philosopher who allowed for bodily pleasure more than anyone else—takes such a bold stance on injuries, there is no reason to call the Stoic view unbelievable or contrary to human nature. He says that the wise person can endure an injury. We say that for the wise person, an injury does not even exist.

You have no reason to claim that this goes against nature. We do not deny that being beaten, struck, or losing a limb is unpleasant. We simply say that none of these things count as an injury. We do not take away the feeling of pain. We only take away the label of "injury." As long as our virtue remains intact, we cannot receive an injury.

Since both schools agree on despising injuries, we can look to see which one is closer to the truth. You might ask what the difference is between the two. It is exactly the same difference you see between two very brave gladiators. One hides his wound and holds his ground. The other turns to the shouting crowd, signals that his wound is nothing, and refuses to ask them to stop the fight for him.

You do not need to consider our disagreement a big deal. The only important thing—and the only thing you should actually care about—is what both schools of philosophy urge you to do. Both urge you to despise injuries and insults. Insults are essentially just the shadows and outlines of injuries. You do not even need to be a wise person to despise them. You just need to be a sensible person.

A sensible person can say to himself, "I must consider whether these things are happening to me deservedly or undeservedly. If they are deserved, then it is not an insult, but a fair judgment. If they are undeserved, then the person doing the injustice should blush, not me."

It is seriously worth considering what an insult actually is. Someone makes a joke about my bald head, my bad eyesight, my skinny legs, or my short height. There is no insult in telling me something that everyone already knows. We laugh at these exact same things when we talk one-on-one, but we get angry when someone says them in front of a crowd. We do not give others the right to say the things we normally say about ourselves. We enjoy polite jokes, but we get angry if they cross the line.
