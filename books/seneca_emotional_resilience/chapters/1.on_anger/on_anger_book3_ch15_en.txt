[Chapter 15]
I am sure Harpagus must have given similar advice to the king of Persia and suffered a similar fate. The furious king cooked Harpagus's children and served them to him at a banquet, occasionally asking if he liked the seasoning. Then, when the king saw that Harpagus was full of his own misery, he ordered the children's heads to be brought in and asked how he enjoyed the meal.

This miserable man did not lose his quick wit for a moment, and his expression never changed. "Every meal served at the king's table is a delight," he said. With this flattery, he avoided being invited back to eat the rest of his children's meat.

I am not saying a father shouldn't condemn the king's actions or seek the revenge a bloodthirsty monster deserves. However, I can draw one conclusion from this story. It proves that even the anger triggered by unheard-of brutality can be hidden, and a person can force themselves to say the exact opposite of what they truly feel. This method of controlling anger is necessary, at least for people who choose this kind of life and are allowed to eat at the king's table. This is how they must eat and drink, how they must answer, and how they must laugh at their own deaths.

We will look later at whether staying alive is even worth going to such lengths. That is a different issue entirely. Let us not comfort such a miserable group of people or encourage them to obey the orders of a butcher. Instead, let's point out that no matter how much a person's situation looks like slavery, the path to freedom is always open to them as long as their mind is not sick. If a person suffers when they can easily end their misery by taking their own life, it is their own fault.

To the man serving a king who points arrows at the hearts of his friends, and to the man serving a master who feeds a father the hearts of his own children, I would say this: "You madman, you have no reason to groan. You must not wait for some enemy to destroy your entire nation to get revenge, or for a powerful king from a distant land to arrive. Everywhere you look, you can see an end to your suffering. There is a path to freedom at the bottom of that cliff. Freedom sits at the bottom of that sea, that river, and that well. Freedom hangs from the branches of that stunted, sick, dying tree. Your own throat, your neck, and your heart are countless escape routes from slavery. If you think these methods I am pointing out are too hard and require too much strength and courage, and you ask which path leads to freedom, I will answer that any vein in your body is a road to freedom."
