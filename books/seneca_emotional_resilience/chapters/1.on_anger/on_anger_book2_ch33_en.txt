[Chapter 33]

Our opponent might say, "People will treat us with more respect if we take revenge for our injuries." If we must use revenge, we should use it only as a practical remedy, without any anger. Do not think of revenge as a fun or pleasant thing, but simply as a useful tool. However, it is often better to pretend you were never injured at all than to seek revenge. When powerful people do you wrong, you must not only endure it, but you must endure it with a bright, cheerful face. If a powerful person thinks they successfully hurt you, they will likely just do it again. This is the absolute worst trait of people whose minds have been poisoned by arrogant wealth and power: they actually hate the people they have injured. Everyone knows the famous story of an old courtier. When someone asked him how he managed the rare achievement of living to an old age while serving in the royal court, he replied, "By accepting wrongs and returning thanks for them."

Revenge is often so unhelpful that sometimes you shouldn't even admit you've been hurt. The infamous Roman emperor Caligula (also known as Gaius Caesar) was once annoyed by the fancy clothes and neat hair of a young man, the son of a famous Roman knight named Pastor. The emperor threw the young man in prison. When Pastor begged the emperor not to harm his son, Caligula ordered the son to be executed immediately, as if the father's plea had just reminded him to do it. Then, to smooth over his cruel behavior, the emperor invited Pastor to dinner that very night. Pastor arrived looking completely peaceful, showing no anger at all. Caligula raised a glass to him and ordered a guard to watch Pastor closely. The miserable father played along, drinking the wine as if he were drinking his own son's blood. The emperor sent him perfume and a flower crown, and ordered the guard to see if Pastor would actually wear them. He wore them. On the exact same day he lost his son—before he even had the chance to bury him—the old man sat as one of a hundred guests. Despite being elderly and suffering from gout, he drank an enormous amount of wine, more than would have been proper even at a child's birthday party. Throughout the whole night, he didn't shed a single tear. He didn't let even the smallest trace of his grief slip out. He enjoyed the dinner just as though his begging had actually saved his son's life.

You might ask why he did this. The answer is that he had a second son. In the ancient Greek epic, the Iliad, King Priam hid his anger, hugged the knees of his enemy Achilles, kissed the deadly hands that were stained with his son's blood, and shared a meal with his son's killer. That is true! But in that story, there was no perfume and no flower crowns. His fierce enemy actually offered him food with kind words, rather than forcing him to chug huge cups of wine with a guard standing over him. If Pastor only cared about his own safety, the father would have spat in the tyrant's face. But love for his remaining son crushed his anger. He deserved the emperor's permission to leave the party and gather his son's remains. Instead, the emperor tormented the old man by constantly offering him more drinks and suggesting he use the wine to wash away his sorrow. Pastor, in return, pretended to be in a fantastic mood, acting as if he had completely forgotten everything that had happened that day. If he had been a grumpy guest to his eldest son's murderer, he would have lost his second son as well.
