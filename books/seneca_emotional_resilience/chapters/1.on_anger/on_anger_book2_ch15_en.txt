[Chapter 15]

Our opponent says, "Look at especially angry, free peoples like the Germans or the Scythians, and you will see that there is certainly something noble in anger." The reason for this is that people with strong, bold minds are easily prone to anger before they are tamed by discipline. Just as strong bushes grow in good soil even in the wild, and trees stand tall in fertile land, certain passions take root only in better temperaments. Similarly, a naturally bold temperament creates a quick temper. It is hot and fiery, and does not have any mean or petty qualities. But as is true for all people who stand out only because of natural advantages without training, their energy is directed the wrong way. If their minds are not controlled, their brave nature degenerates into a habit of recklessness and foolish daring.

Our opponent might argue that a gentle nature is connected to softer vices like a weak mind, softness, and timidity. This is true, and therefore I can often point out a good temperament through the flaws of the temperament itself. But just because these flaws are evidence of a superior nature does not change the fact that they are vices. Furthermore, all peoples who are as wild and free as lions or wolves can command just as little as they can obey. Their minds are uncivilized, fierce, and difficult to manage. A person who cannot be ruled cannot rule, either. As a result, the empires of the world have almost always remained in the hands of peoples who enjoy milder climates. Those who live near the frozen North have savage temperaments.

As the poet said:

"Their nature closely resembles the sky they live under."
