[Chapter 29]

It is shameful to hate someone you actually praise. It is even more shameful to hate someone when they actually deserve your pity. Suppose a prisoner of war is suddenly reduced to slavery. If he still holds onto a shred of his freedom and does not run quickly enough to perform disgusting or exhausting tasks, we should figure out whether he truly cannot do it, or if he just does not want to. Maybe he has grown slow from a long period of rest, and simply cannot run fast enough to keep up with his master's horse or carriage. Maybe he is completely overcome by sleep after being forced to stay awake for many days and nights. Maybe he was taken away from easy city service and put into hard farm labor, and he simply doesn't have the strength to do it well. In all these cases, we should pardon him. We would forgive many of our slaves if we actually took the time to judge their situation before getting angry at them.

Instead, we just blindly follow our first angry impulse. Then, even if we realize we got worked up over absolutely nothing, we stay angry anyway. We do this because we do not want to admit that we got angry for no good reason. The most unfair part is that the sheer injustice of our anger actually makes us hold onto it even harder. We nurse our anger and keep it burning, as if being violently angry somehow proves that we were right to be angry in the first place.
