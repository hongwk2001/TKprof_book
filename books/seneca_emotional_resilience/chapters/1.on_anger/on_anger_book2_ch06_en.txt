[Chapter 6]

Our opponents argue, "Just as virtue praises what is honorable, it must be angry at what is disgraceful." This is as ridiculous as saying that virtue must possess both baseness and greatness at the same time. While rejoicing in good deeds is grand and glorious, being angry at another's sins belongs only to a petty and narrow mind. Virtue never makes the mistake of imitating the very evil it tries to suppress. Virtue considers anger itself to be a punishable offense because anger is often a greater crime than the faults it gets angry at. Rejoicing and being glad are proper and natural functions of virtue. Getting angry is just as unsuitable for virtue's dignity as being sad. Sadness is the companion of anger, and all anger eventually ends in sadness, whether from regret or failure.

Secondly, if it is a wise person's duty to be angry at sin, they will be angrier at greater sins and will end up being angry very often. Thus, the wise person would not merely feel anger, but would become a hot-tempered person who gets angry at the drop of a hat. However, if we believe that great and frequent anger cannot exist in the mind of a wise person, we must free them from this passion completely. If a person must get angry in proportion to each individual bad action, their anger would have no limit. If they got equally angry at both large and small crimes, they would be unfair. If they exploded in rage every time a crime deserved it, they would become the angriest person in the world.
