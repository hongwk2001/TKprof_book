[Chapter 16]

Therefore, we do not need an angry punisher to discipline those who do wrong and act badly. Because anger is a disease of the mind, it is not right to punish one sin with another sin.

Someone might argue that we should get angry at robbers or poisoners. But when I need to make myself bleed for a medical reason, I do not get angry at myself. I see all forms of punishment as a cure.

If you have just started making mistakes, and even if you make them often but have not done anything seriously wrong, I will try to correct you by scolding you privately first, and then publicly. If you have gone too far to be corrected with words, you must be controlled through public disgrace. For a person at the next stage, stronger and more physical actions are needed. They should be sent away to a place like a desert. The complete wickedness of the person at the next stage requires a harsher cure, so chains and a public prison must be used.

Finally, there is the person with an unfixable, evil mind who only adds crime to crime. You have reached a point where arguments about right and wrong do not work on you at all. For you, the sin itself is enough reason to commit the sin. You have soaked your entire mind so deeply in evil deeds that the evil cannot be washed away without tearing out your heart. You poor person, you must have been wishing for death for a long time! We will do you a favor. We will take away the madness you are suffering from, and give you the only good thing left for you—death—since you have lived as a disaster to yourself and others for so long.

I must not be angry at the very moment I am doing that person a favor. Sometimes, putting a person to death is the truest form of mercy.

If I were a professional doctor entering a hospital or a rich person's house, I would not prescribe the exact same treatment for patients suffering from completely different diseases. I see different kinds of vices in many different minds, and I am called to heal the entire body of the citizens. I must find the right cure for each disease. One person can be cured by appealing to their sense of honor, another by being sent away, another by pain, another by poverty, and another by the sword.

Therefore, if it becomes my duty as a judge to wear black robes and blow a trumpet to gather the crowd, I will walk to the judge's seat with a calm face, not with a furious or hostile mind. Instead of an angry voice, I will hand down the formal sentence in a heavy and gentle voice, and I will order the execution firmly but without anger. Even when I order a criminal to be beheaded or a traitor to be pushed off a cliff, I will be completely free from anger. I will act with the exact same expression and feelings I have when stepping on a snake or a poisonous creature.

Some people say we need anger to hand down a punishment. However, the law never gets angry at people it does not know, has never seen, and hopes will never exist in the future. We should adopt the mindset of the law. The law does not get angry. It simply defines what a crime is.

If it were right for a good person to get angry at an evil crime, it would also have to be right to be jealous of evil people's success. Honestly, nothing makes you more furious than seeing the very people who deserve the worst misery actually prosper and enjoy unfair success. However, just as a good person looks at the crimes of evil people without anger, they will also look at their wealth without jealousy. A good judge condemns bad actions but does not hate them.

Our opponent might argue that when a wise person deals with these issues, their mind will be affected and they will be more excited than usual. I agree. He will experience a light, passing emotion. As Zeno said, even after a wound is completely healed, a scar remains even in a wise person's mind. A wise person will still feel traces and shadows of passion, but they will be completely free from the passion itself.
