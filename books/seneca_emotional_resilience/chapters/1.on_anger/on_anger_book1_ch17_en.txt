[Chapter 17]

Aristotle says that certain emotions act as weapons if you use them properly. This would be true if those emotions were like real weapons of war that the user could pick up and put down whenever they wanted. But the weapons Aristotle gives to virtue fight on their own. These weapons do not wait for a person to pick them up. The weapon is not owned by the person; the weapon owns the person.

We do not need any outside weapons at all. Nature has armed us perfectly by giving us reason. Nature has placed this strong, unbreakable weapon in our hands, and it completely obeys our will. This weapon is not unpredictable and never betrays its master. Reason is enough on its own not just to plan for the future, but to manage all our affairs.

Nothing is more foolish than reason asking anger for protection. It is like trusting something uncertain to protect something certain, trusting something unreliable to protect something reliable, or trusting a sick person to protect a healthy person. Even in matters where anger seems most needed, reason is much stronger on its own. When reason decides something must be done, it pushes through to the end. Because reason cannot find a better replacement for itself, it stubbornly sticks to the plan it made.

Anger, on the other hand, often collapses out of pity. Anger does not have solid strength. It just swells up like an empty balloon, and starts fiercely like a sudden gust of wind blowing violently from a swamp but not lasting long. Anger begins with a huge rush, but it loses power and gets exhausted too quickly. The very same anger that was imagining new and cruel tortures just a moment ago suddenly becomes soft and gentle when it is actually time to hand down the punishment.

Emotion cools down quickly, but reason stays consistent. Even if anger keeps burning, it often stops after killing only two or three people, even though many more deserve to die. The first attack of anger is as fierce as a snake whose first bite is full of poison, but becomes harmless after a few strikes when its poison runs out.

Because of this, people who are equally guilty are not punished equally. Often, a person who committed a smaller crime gets a heavier punishment simply because they were caught when the anger was fresh. Anger is completely unpredictable. Sometimes it goes way over the line, and other times it falls far short of its duty. Anger gets lost in its own feelings and makes judgments based on its changing moods, refuses to listen to evidence, gives the accused no chance to defend themselves, stubbornly clings to false assumptions, and refuses to change its mind even when it is clearly wrong.
