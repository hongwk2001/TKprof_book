[Chapter 10]

You should realize that it is wrong to be angry at people's mistakes. It is unreasonable to get angry at someone who trips in the dark, a deaf person who cannot hear commands, or children who forget their duties to play games or make silly jokes with their friends. It is equally ridiculous to get angry at people who are weakened by sickness, old age, or exhaustion. Among all of humanity's misfortunes is the fact that human understanding is so confused that people not only fail to stop themselves from going down the wrong path, but they actually love doing it. To avoid getting angry at individuals, you must forgive the whole group. You must grant pardon to all of humanity. If you get angry at young or old people because they do wrong, you must also get angry at infants, who will inevitably do wrong soon. No one gets angry at young children because they lack the understanding to tell right from wrong. But being human is an even greater and better excuse than being a child.

We are born as creatures vulnerable to mental disorders as much as physical ones. This is not because we are foolish or dull, but because we misuse our sharp intellect and lead each other into vice by our bad examples. A person who follows others who have already gone astray certainly has an excuse for wandering off the main road. The strictness of a general might be necessary for individual deserters, but when the entire army deserts, he has no choice but to pardon them. What stops the wise person's anger is the sheer number of people sinning. The wise person knows perfectly well how unfair and dangerous it is to be angry at a vice shared by everyone.

Whenever the philosopher Heraclitus went outside and saw the masses of people living miserably—or rather, dying miserably—around him, he would weep. He pitied everyone who met him with a happy and joyful face. He had a gentle but overly weak nature, and he himself was one of those he should have wept for. On the other hand, it is said that Democritus could not stop laughing whenever he appeared in public. To him, the serious affairs of the people did not seem serious at all. There is no place for anger. Everything should either make us weep or make us laugh. The wise person will not be angry at sinners, because they know that no one is born wise; a person becomes wise. Because they perfectly understand the condition of human life, they know that very few wise people are produced in any age. A normal person does not get angry at Providence or nature. It is foolish to be surprised that bushes in the forest do not bear fruit, or to wonder why thorns and briars are not covered with useful berries. No one gets angry when nature is the excuse for a flaw.

Therefore, the wise person—who maintains tranquility of mind, deals with mistakes leniently, and acts not as an enemy of sinners but as someone who improves them—will go out into the world every day with this mindset: "I will encounter drunkards, lechers, the ungrateful, the greedy, and many people driven mad by ambition." They will view all these people gently, just as a doctor views their patients. When water gushes through a crack in a ship, the sailor does not get angry at the crew or the ship itself. Instead, they try to fix it. They block the water, bail it out, plug every visible hole, and work constantly against the unseen leaks that seep into the bottom of the boat. The fact that water keeps coming in as fast as they bail it out does not slow down their efforts. We need a long, sustained struggle against permanent and widespread evils—not to stamp them out completely, but simply to keep them from overwhelming us.
