[Chapter 11]
It is better not to see or hear everything. Many causes of unpleasantness can just pass us by, and a person who ignores them simply does not worry about them. If you do not want to be easily angered, do not pry too deeply into things. A person who tries to find out what people say about them, and digs up even secret, malicious rumors, is only destroying their own peace of mind. Some comments can be interpreted as insults. Therefore, it is best to set some aside, laugh off others, and forgive the rest.

Just as most things can be brushed off with a joke, there are many ways to control anger. It is said that when Socrates was slapped in the face, he simply remarked that it was a pity a person could not know when they should wear a helmet outside. It matters far more how you endure an injury than how you received it in the first place. When I see that even tyrants—swollen with pride because their success was matched by their ability to escape punishment—sometimes held back their natural cruelty, I fail to understand how practicing self-control can be considered so difficult.

At any rate, the story goes that when a drunk guest once severely criticized the cruelty of Pisistratus, the tyrant of Athens, many people in the room offered to grab the traitor for him. People said all sorts of things to stir up his anger, but he calmly endure it. He told the people urging him on that he would not be angry with the man, just as he would not be angry at someone who ran into him while wearing a blindfold.
