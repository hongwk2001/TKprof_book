[Chapter 3]
You might say, "There is no doubt that anger is powerful and destructive, so please tell us how to cure it." But as I mentioned in my earlier books, the philosopher Aristotle defends anger and argues that we should not completely wipe it out. He claims that anger acts as a spur for virtue, and if we take it away, our minds become defenseless and slow to attempt great things. Therefore, we must prove just how ugly and fierce anger really is. We need to clearly show how monstrous it is for one person to violently rage against another. We must see clearly how an angry person rushes with frantic violence to destroy both themselves and their enemies, tearing down the very foundations that will ultimately bring them down as well.

We cannot call a person sane if they are blindly driven like they are caught in a violent storm, becoming a slave to a reckless mental illness. This person does not ask someone else for revenge; they handle it themselves. They radiate anger in both their thoughts and actions, destroying the people closest to them—the very people they will soon bitterly cry over losing. No one should treat this emotion as a helper or companion to virtue. Anger disturbs our calm, rational judgment, and without that reason, virtue can accomplish nothing. The temporary strength a sick person gets from a fever is neither lasting nor healthy; it is only powerful in destroying the person.

You do not need to think I am wasting my time complaining about anger, as if everyone already agrees it is a bad thing. There is still a famous philosopher who gives anger a specific role, claiming it is useful and provides energy for battles, business, and anything that requires courage. We must expose the uncontrollable, frantic madness of anger so it does not trick anyone into thinking it can be useful in certain situations. We need to remind people of its true nature by comparing it to the rack, the rope, the dark dungeon, and the cross. We must show the fires lit around people, the iron hooks dragging the living and the dead alike, the various chains and harsh punishments, the torn limbs, the branding on a slave's forehead, and the dens of wild beasts. Anger should be pictured standing among these torture devices, letting out a terrifying and dark growl. The emotion of anger itself is far more horrifying than any weapon it uses to express its rage.
