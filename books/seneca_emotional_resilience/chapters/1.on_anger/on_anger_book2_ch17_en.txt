[Chapter 17]

Our opponent says, "An orator sometimes speaks better when he is angry." This is not true. He speaks better when he *pretends* to be angry. Even actors draw applause from the audience not when they are truly angry, but when they perform the role of an angry person brilliantly. Likewise, whether speaking before a jury, at a public assembly, or in any situation where we need to influence the minds of others to do what we want, we must pretend to feel anger, fear, and pity ourselves before we can make others feel them. Often, pretending to have a passion accomplishes what the passion itself could not.

He also says, "A mind that cannot feel anger is weak." This would be true if there were nothing stronger than anger to support the mind. A person should not be a robber or a victim, and should not have a soft heart or a cruel one. The former belongs to a mind that is too weak, and the latter to a mind that is too hardened. The wise person must maintain moderation. When things need to be done with some energy, they should call upon true strength, not anger, as their helper.
