[Chapter 14]

Therefore, anger must never become a habit for us. However, when we need to wake up the dull mind of the person we are speaking to, we may sometimes pretend to be angry, just as we use spurs and torches to rouse a slow horse.

We must sometimes use fear with people who will not listen to reason. But actually getting angry is just as useless as being sad or afraid.

You might argue that situations arise that make us angry.

Yes, they do. But that is exactly when we must suppress our anger the most. It is not difficult to conquer our own minds. Think of athletes who pour all their energy into the basest parts of their bodies. They still endure blows and pain to exhaust the strength of the person hitting them. They do not fight back when anger tells them to, but only when the right opportunity comes.

It is said that Pyrrhus, the most famous trainer for gymnastic games, constantly reminded his students never to lose their temper. Anger ruins their technique and makes them think only about how to hurt someone. As a result, reason often advises patience, while anger advises revenge. By giving in to anger, we expose ourselves to much worse misfortunes when we could have survived the initial one.

Some people were driven into exile because they could not stand a single insulting remark. Others plunged into the deepest misery because they refused to silently endure even the slightest wrong. And some ended up wearing the yoke of slavery because they were too proud to compromise even a tiny part of their complete freedom.
