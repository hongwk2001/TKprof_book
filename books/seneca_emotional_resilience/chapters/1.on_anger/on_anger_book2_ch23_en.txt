[Chapter 23]

Everyone knows the story of the tyrant-slayer who was caught before he could finish his mission. The tyrant Hippias tortured the assassin to force him to name his accomplices. Instead, the assassin deliberately pointed to the tyrant's friends standing nearby, calling out the names of everyone the tyrant loved most. As the tyrant ordered each man to be executed on the spot, he finally asked the prisoner if anyone else was left. The assassin replied, "Only you are left, because I have not left a single person alive who actually cares about you." 

Look how anger actually made the tyrant help his own assassin and murder his own guards with his own hands!

Alexander the Great showed far more courage in a similar situation. His mother once wrote him a letter warning him that his doctor, Philip, might poison him. After reading the letter, Alexander confidently drank the medicine Philip handed to him without even flinching. He placed absolute trust in his friend. He believed that his friend was an innocent man and that showing this trust would ensure he stayed innocent. I praise Alexander's behavior even more because he was a man who normally flew into a rage faster than anyone. This kind of self-control is incredibly rare among kings, which makes it all the more praiseworthy. 

Julius Caesar, the great Roman leader who was famous for his mercy during the civil war, did the exact same thing. He burned an entire bundle of letters written to his enemy, Pompey. These letters were written by people who were supposedly neutral or secretly siding against Caesar. Even though Caesar rarely became violent when he was angry, he chose to completely remove the temptation to be angry in the first place. He figured that the most merciful way to forgive someone was simply never to know what their crime was.
