[Chapter 17]
Those kings, who had no education and no background in refined literature, were incredibly cruel when they were angry. Now I will show you Alexander the Great, who had just stepped out of the classroom of Aristotle. During a meal, he stabbed his closest childhood friend, Cleitus, to death with his own hand. He killed him because Cleitus did not flatter him enough and was too slow to change from a free Macedonian into a Persian slave.

He even locked up Lysimachus, a friend who was just as close to him as Cleitus, in a cage with a lion. Lysimachus fortunately escaped the lion's teeth, but when he later became a king himself, he wasn't any gentler. He cut off the nose and ears of his friend Telesphorus of Rhodes, leaving him mutilated. Once Telesphorus's slashed and disfigured face no longer looked human, Lysimachus locked him in a cage for a long time as if he were some strange new animal. He was left to roll around in starvation and his own waste, making the punishment even worse!

On top of that, his hands and knees developed thick calluses because the cramped cage forced him to use them instead of his feet. His sides were covered in sores from rubbing against the walls. His appearance was not only terrifying but also deeply shocking. The punishment he received turned him into a monster that people couldn't even pity. But no matter how unhuman Telesphorus looked after suffering all this, the man who inflicted this torture was far less human.
