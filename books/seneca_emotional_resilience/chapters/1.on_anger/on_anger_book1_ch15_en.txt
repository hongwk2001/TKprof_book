[Chapter 15]

Therefore, people who have done wrong should be corrected using both gentle and strict methods, through warning and force. Through punishment, not anger, they can be reborn as better people for their own sake and for the sake of others. No one gets angry at a patient while bandaging their wound.

Someone might say, "But some people cannot be fixed. They have absolutely no gentleness in them, and there is no hope they will ever improve."

If that is true, and if they are likely to corrupt everyone they meet, they must be removed from human society. They must stop being bad people in the only way possible for them. But even this must be done without hatred. While I am doing him the greatest favor by rescuing him from himself, there is no reason for me to hate him. No one hates their own leg when it has to be amputated. That is not an act of anger, but simply a tragic method for healing.

We put down mad dogs, slaughter fierce bulls, and cull sick sheep so they do not spread the disease to the rest of the flock. We destroy deformed offspring, and sometimes we drown children if they are born weak or deformed. Separating the useless from the healthy is not an act of anger, but an act of reason.

Nothing is uglier than the anger of the person handing down a punishment. When a sentence is passed with calm and careful judgment, the punishment has a much stronger power to correct. This is why Socrates told his slave, "I would beat you if I were not angry." He delayed the slave's punishment until he was calmer, and in that moment, he chose to correct himself instead of the slave. Even Socrates could not trust his own anger, so no one can boast that they are perfectly in control of their emotions.
