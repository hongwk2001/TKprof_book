[Chapter 14]
King Cambyses of Persia drank far too much. Among his closest friends, only Praxaspes advised him to drink a little less, pointing out how shameful drunkenness was for a king whose every move was watched and heard by everyone. Cambyses replied, "I will show you that I never lose control over myself. I will prove right now that my eyes and hands still work perfectly even after I have been drinking."

He then drank even more freely than usual using large cups. When he was heavily drunk and sluggish, he ordered the son of the friend who had criticized him to stand in the doorway with his left hand raised above his head. He then drew his bow and, exactly as he said he would, shot an arrow straight through the young man's heart. He then had the young man's chest cut open to show the arrow lodged perfectly in the heart. Looking at the boy's father, he asked if his hand was not steady enough.

The father replied that not even Apollo, the god of the sun, could have aimed better. May the gods curse a man who was a slave in his heart, even if not in his status! He actually praised the act he should not have been able to bear watching. He saw his son's chest torn open and the heart quivering from the wound, yet he saw it as an opportunity to offer a compliment. He should have argued with the king about whether it was a direct hit and demanded he shoot again, so the king could gladly try to prove his steady hand once more by using the father as the target.

What a savage king! What a perfectly fitting target for all the arrows his followers could shoot! But even if we curse the king for ending a banquet with cruelty and death, praising the archery was far worse than the shot itself. We will discuss later how a father should behave when standing next to the body of his son, whose murder he both caused and witnessed.

The point we are discussing now has been proven. It means that anger can be suppressed. The father did not curse the king, nor did he utter a single ominous word, even though his own heart had been struck just as deeply as his son's. You could say he did well to swallow his words. Even though he might have spoken as an angry man, he could never have fully expressed what he felt as a father. I repeat, I think his behavior in that situation was wiser than when he tried to control the drinking of a king who was better off drinking wine than drinking blood, and who gave people peace as long as he held a wine glass. Therefore, he simply added one more person to the crowd that bitterly proves just how little kings value the good advice of their friends.
