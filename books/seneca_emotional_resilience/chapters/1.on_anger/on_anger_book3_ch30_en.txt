[Chapter 30]

It is very helpful to notice how small and harmless the original triggers of anger usually are. You will see that people are driven by the exact same things as dumb animals. We are easily thrown off balance by useless, silly things. A bull is provoked by the color red. A snake raises its head at a passing shadow. A bear or a lion gets irritated if you simply wave a rag at them. All creatures that are naturally fierce and wild are easily alarmed by tiny things. The exact same thing happens to humans, whether they are naturally restless or normally calm. They are gripped by sudden suspicions, and they often mistake minor inconveniences for serious injuries.

These petty suspicions are the most common, and definitely the most bitter, triggers for anger. We get angry at our closest friends because they gave us less than we expected, or because they gave someone else more than they gave us. But there is a simple cure for both of these complaints. If our friend favors someone else over us, we should just enjoy what we have without constantly comparing ourselves to others. A person will never truly be happy if they are tortured every time they see someone else doing better than them. If I received less than I hoped for, then perhaps I simply hoped for more than I actually deserved.

This is what we must guard against the most. This kind of jealousy fuels the most destructive anger, the kind that spares nothing and ruins even the most sacred relationships. The Emperor Julius Caesar was not stabbed by his actual enemies. He was murdered by his own friends, simply because he could not satisfy their endless greed. He was more than willing to reward them. No one ever used a victory more generously than Caesar. He kept none of the spoils for himself; he only wanted the power to hand them out. But it was impossible to satisfy such unreasonable appetites, especially when every single person wanted as much as any one person could possibly hold.

That is why Caesar ended up surrounded by his own fellow soldiers with drawn swords. Among them was Tillius Cimber, who had previously been the most passionate defender of Caesar's party. There were others who only joined Pompey's side after Pompey was already dead. This endless greed is what turns the weapons of kings against themselves. It is what drives a king's most trusted followers to plot his death, even though they once would have gladly laid down their own lives to protect him.
