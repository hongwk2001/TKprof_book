[Chapter 1]

My first book covered a much richer topic, Novatus. The words flowed easily, like a carriage rolling downhill. Now, we must move on to drier matters. The question before us is whether anger comes from deliberate choice or from impulse. In other words, does anger act on its own, or does it spring up within us without our knowledge, like most passions? We must discuss these basic questions first before moving on to more important topics. Think about our bodies: the bones, muscles, and joints are formed first. They aren't pretty to look at, but they form the foundation of our bodies and are essential for life. Next come the parts that make up the beauty of the face and appearance. Finally, after the rest of the body is fully formed, the color that charms the eye is added last.

There is no doubt that anger is triggered by the thought that someone has harmed us. But the question is whether anger springs up immediately after that thought without the mind's consent, or whether it rises with the mind's approval. The Stoic view is that anger can do nothing on its own without the mind's approval. The mind must first think it has been wronged, long for revenge, and combine two ideas: "we should not have been harmed" and "it is our duty to avenge the harm." This process cannot be a mere impulse that happens without our consent. A simple impulse is a single act. Anger, on the other hand, is a complex act made up of several parts. A person understands that something happened, gets angry about it, condemns the action, and finally seeks revenge. None of this can happen without the mind agreeing to the issues affecting it.
