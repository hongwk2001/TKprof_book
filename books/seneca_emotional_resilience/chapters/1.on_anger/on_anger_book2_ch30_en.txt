[Chapter 30]

Sometimes, we witness offenses directly with our own eyes. In these cases, let us stop to look at the person's character and intentions. Perhaps the person who wronged you is a child. We should pardon their young age, because a child simply does not know any better. Perhaps the offender is your father. He has given you so much throughout his life that he has earned the right to occasionally wrong you. Or perhaps the very thing that offends you is actually something he did for your own good. Suppose it is a woman; well, she simply made a mistake. Suppose a man did the deed because he was strictly ordered to do it. It is incredibly unfair to get angry at someone who acted under force. Perhaps you hurt the person first. If so, you have no right to complain when you receive the exact pain that you caused in the first place.

Suppose your opponent is a judge. In that case, you should trust his judgment over your own. Suppose he is a king. If he punishes the guilty, you should yield to him because he is just, and if he punishes the innocent, you should yield to him because he is powerful. Suppose the offender is a dumb animal, or a person who acts just as stupidly. If you get angry at them, you only drag yourself down to their level. Suppose you are dealing with a disease or a misfortune. The suffering will have much less impact on you if you bear it quietly. Suppose your anger is directed at a god. Getting angry at a god is just as much a waste of your time as praying for the god to get angry at someone else. If a good person hurts you, do not believe that they actually meant to do it. If a bad person hurts you, do not be surprised. They will eventually face justice from someone else for what they did to you. In fact, by committing the sin in the first place, they have already punished themselves.
