[Chapter 19]

A hot-tempered personality has this major flaw: it absolutely hates being controlled. If the truth turns out to be different from what it wants, it gets angry at the truth itself. It yells, makes loud noises, and attacks its victims with wild gestures, insults, and curses.

Reason does not act like this. If necessary, reason will quietly and calmly wipe out an entire family that has become an enemy of the state, right down to the wife and children. Reason will tear down their houses to the ground and erase the very name of the enemies of freedom. But reason does all of this without grinding its teeth, wildly shaking its head, or doing anything unworthy of a judge. Especially when handing down a heavy sentence, a judge's expression must be incredibly calm and steady.

Hieronymus pointed out that just because you want to hit someone, you do not need to bite your own lips. He would have been shocked if he saw a governor jump down from the judge's seat, grab a guard's whip, and tear his own clothes in frustration just because a victim's clothes were not torn fast enough. You do not need to flip tables, smash wine glasses, bang your head against a pillar, pull your hair out, or beat your chest and thighs. Just imagine how violent the anger must be that explodes at itself simply because it cannot hurt someone else as quickly as it wants to. People who act like this can only be stopped if onlookers hold them back and beg them to calm down.

However, a person who is free from anger and hands down the proper punishment to each person does none of these things. If a person truly regrets what they have done and shows the potential to improve in the future, the judge will often admit that their bad actions are not deeply rooted but only surface-level, and let them go. A judge is willing to overlook someone if it harms neither the person being forgiven nor the person doing the forgiving.

Sometimes, the judge will punish a major crime more lightly than a minor one. This happens if the major crime was the result of a sudden impulse rather than cruelty, while the minor crime was secretly and sneakily planned. Even if two people committed exactly the same crime, he will never give them the same punishment if one did it out of carelessness and the other did it with a deliberate plan to hurt someone.

Whenever dealing with a crime, he will remember that one kind of punishment is meant to make bad people better, and the other is to isolate them permanently. In both cases, he will look to the future, not the past. As Plato said, a wise person punishes someone not because they have sinned, but so that they will not sin again. The past cannot be undone, but the future can be prevented.

If the judge wants to make someone a public example to show the consequences of bad actions, he will execute them publicly. This is not just to kill the criminal, but to use their death to stop others from doing the same thing.

You can see how free from emotional turmoil one must be to weigh all these factors. Handling the power of life and death is a matter that requires the ultimate care. It is truly terrible to place the sword of justice into the hands of an angry person.
