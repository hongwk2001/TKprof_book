[Chapter 21]

It is the greatest help to boys to raise them properly. However, it is very difficult to balance this education. We must be very careful not to let a habit of anger grow in them, but we also must not break their energetic spirit. This requires close attention, because the traits we want to encourage and the traits we need to control both grow from the exact same things. Even a careful observer can be easily tricked by how similar they look. A boy's spirit grows when he feels free, but it shrinks when he is treated like a slave. Praise lifts his spirit and makes him expect great things of himself. However, this same praise can also make him arrogant and hot-tempered. Therefore, we must guide him carefully between these two extremes, using control at times and encouragement at others. He must never face degrading or slave-like treatment. He should never have to beg miserably for anything, and he should not get what he wants just by begging. Instead, give him rewards based on his own good character, his past good behavior, or his promise to act right in the future.

In games with his friends, we must not let him pout or fly into a rage. Make sure he stays on good terms with his competitors. This teaches him that the goal of a contest is not to hurt his opponent, but to win fairly. When he wins or does something praise-worthy, let him enjoy his victory, but do not let him lose control in wild excitement. Joy quickly turns into boasting, and boasting leads to arrogance and too much pride. Give him some time to relax, but do not let him become lazy, and above all, keep him far away from luxury. A soft, pampered upbringing makes children more prone to anger than anything else. The more an only child is spoiled, or the more an orphan is allowed to do whatever they want, the more corrupt they become. A child who has never been told "no," whose anxious mother always wipes away his tears, and whose tutor is blamed for his mistakes, will grow up unable to handle any rejection or criticism.

You can easily see how a person's anger grows more violent as they rise in status. This is especially true for the rich, the noble, and those in power, because good fortune stirs up the emptiest and pettiest passions in their minds. Success feeds anger when a crowd of flatterers surrounds a proud person, whispering things like, "How dare that person talk back to you! You are lowering yourself and acting beneath your dignity." Prosperity showers them with flattery that even a healthy, well-principled mind struggles to resist.

Therefore, you must keep flattery far away from children. Let a child hear the truth, and sometimes, let him fear it. Teach him to always respect the truth. Make him stand up to show respect when older people are present. Do not let him get anything by throwing a tantrum. If he cries for something, refuse him, but give it to him later when he is quiet. Let him see his father's wealth, but do not let him use it whenever he pleases. Make sure he is corrected when he does something wrong. It is incredibly helpful to surround boys with calm, even-tempered teachers and tutors. A soft, unshaped mind clings to whatever is nearby and takes on its form. The habits of young people are just copies of the nurses and tutors who raised them. Once, a boy who grew up in the house of the great philosopher Plato went back to his parents. When he saw his father screaming in a fit of rage, the boy said, "I never saw anyone at Plato's house act like that." Without a doubt, that boy learned to copy his father's anger much faster than he learned to copy Plato's calm. Above all, keep the child's meals simple, dress him in plain clothes, and make sure he dresses like his peers. If you treat him as an equal to other children from the start, he will not get angry later when someone is compared to him.
