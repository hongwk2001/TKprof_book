[Chapter 28]

If we truly want to be fair judges of everything that happens, we must first convince ourselves of one basic truth: not a single one of us is completely without fault. Most of our anger comes from refusing to admit this. We say, "I haven't sinned! I haven't done anything wrong!" No, it would be more accurate to say that you refuse to admit you have done anything wrong. We explode with anger when someone scolds or punishes us, even though at that very moment, we are adding arrogance and stubbornness to our list of wrongdoings.

No one can honestly say they have never broken a rule. And even if someone could, what a cheap kind of innocence that is, just barely following the letter of the law. True duty goes far beyond what is written in rulebooks. Being a good family member, showing kindness, being generous, treating people fairly, and acting with honor—these are all required of us, even though they are not written in any law book. Yet, we cannot even guarantee that we pass the lowest, narrowest test of legal innocence. We have done wrong things, thought wrong things, wished for wrong things, and encouraged wrong things. Sometimes, the only reason we remained innocent was simply because our bad plans failed. When we keep this in mind, let us treat people who make mistakes more fairly, and let us believe that the people who scold us might actually be right. Whatever we do, let us not get angry with ourselves—because if we get angry at ourselves, we would have to be angry at everyone else too. Above all, we must not get angry at the gods. The suffering we face is not a punishment sent by the gods, but the natural result of living in a frail human body.

You might argue, "But sickness and pain attack us!" Well, if you live in a fragile, temporary house like the human body, you must find a way to deal with it falling apart. Maybe you hear that someone has been gossiping about you. Ask yourself if you gossiped about them first. Think about how many other people you have gossiped about. Instead of assuming that other people are wronging us, let us assume that they are just paying us back for what we have already done to them. Some people act with good intentions, some are forced to act, and others act out of ignorance. Even if someone hurts us deliberately, let us believe they did not do it just to hurt us. Maybe they gave into the temptation of making a clever joke, or maybe they just pushed us aside because they could not succeed any other way. Sometimes we even get annoyed by people flattering us, even as they are pouring it on. But if you stop and remember how often you have been unfairly suspected, how often your good intentions looked like bad behavior, and how many people you hated at first but eventually learned to love, you will stop yourself from getting angry. This is especially true if, whenever someone offends you, you quietly say to yourself, "I have done that exact same thing."

It is incredibly hard to find a truly fair judge. The man who chases after other men's wives—and thinks a woman is desirable simply because she belongs to someone else—is the same man who absolutely refuses to let anyone even look at his own wife. No one demands perfect loyalty more than a traitor. The liar is the first to seek revenge when someone breaks a promise to him. The dishonest lawyer gets the most furious when he is sued. The person who treats their own purity as a joke cannot stand it if someone makes a move on their servants. We keep other people's faults right in front of our eyes, but we hide our own behind our backs. This is why a father who is worse than his son will scold the boy for throwing wild parties. This is why a man who lived a life of limitless luxury will scold someone else for showing the slightest hint of it. This is why tyrants get angry at murderers, and church robbers punish petty thieves. A huge part of humanity is not actually angry at the sin itself, but at the person committing it. Taking a hard look at ourselves will make us much more forgiving. All we have to do is ask ourselves, "Have I ever done something like this? Have I ever made this exact mistake? Does condemning this person's behavior actually help me?"
