[Book 1]

[Chapter 11]

Some people might argue that anger is necessary against an enemy. However, in reality, there is no time when anger is needed less. Our attacks must not be wild and messy; they should be carefully coordinated and controlled. The anger that destroys itself is the exact same thing that brings down barbarians, who have much greater strength and endurance than we do. Gladiators defend themselves with skill, but when they fight with anger, they leave themselves wide open to being hurt.

Furthermore, anger is useless when reason can achieve exactly the same result. A hunter never gets angry at the animals he is hunting. He fights back if they attack, and he chases them if they run away. He does all of this with reason, not with anger.

When the massive tribes of the Cimbri and Teutones poured over the Alps, they were completely crushed. Not even a single messenger survived to carry the news of their defeat back home, leaving only rumors. This happened because they used anger instead of true courage. Anger sometimes crushes whatever it hits, but far more often, it destroys itself.

There are no people braver than the Germans. No one charges forward more boldly than they do. They are born and raised among weapons, and they care for nothing else. No one loves weapons more than they do. No one endures hardships better than they do, living in a harsh climate with hardly any clothes or shelter. Yet, before the regular Roman legions even came into full view, the Germans fell like autumn leaves to the Spaniards, the Gauls, and even the peaceful people of Asia and Syria. It was their own hot-tempered nature that drove them to their deaths. If we added a little intelligence to their minds and a bit of discipline to their bodies, we would certainly have to return to the rough and harsh Roman lifestyle of the past just to survive against them.

Fabius rebuilt the shattered Roman nation by knowing how to use delay tactics to buy time. Angry people do not know how to do that. If Fabius had acted as recklessly as anger urged him to, the empire would have collapsed while taking its last breath. Instead, he thought about the situation carefully. He counted his troops and realized that losing even a single soldier meant losing everything. He put away his grief and desire for revenge, focusing only on what was useful and making the most of his chances. In the end, he conquered his own anger before he conquered Hannibal.

The first Scipio bypassed Hannibal, the Carthaginian army, and everyone else he had a good reason to be angry at, and brought the war straight to Africa. He moved so slowly and carefully that his enemies thought he had become lazy and weak. The second Scipio waited patiently outside Numantia for a long time, quietly enduring his country's insults that taking Numantia was taking longer than taking Carthage. By simply surrounding the enemies and starving them, he made them so desperate that they killed themselves with their own swords.

Therefore, anger is useless even in war or battle. Anger tends to be reckless. It tries to put others in danger, but fails to protect itself from danger. The most reliable virtue is one that looks at itself closely for a long time, maintains control, and moves forward slowly and carefully.
