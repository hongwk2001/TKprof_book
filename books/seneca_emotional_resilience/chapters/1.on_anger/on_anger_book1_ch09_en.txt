[Chapter 9]

Next, anger is completely useless on its own, and it never truly inspires the mind to fight a battle. Virtue is completely perfect on its own and never needs the help of a vice. When virtue suddenly needs explosive energy, it never gets angry. It simply rises to face the situation. It pushes itself forward or calms itself down exactly as much as needed, just like a catapult operator adjusting the tension as much as he wants.

Aristotle said that anger is necessary because no battle can be won unless anger fills the mind and fires up the spirit. He added that anger should be used as a soldier, not a general.

This idea is completely wrong. If anger listens to reason and goes where reason leads, it is no longer anger. This is because stubbornness is the defining feature of anger. On the other hand, if anger does not listen, if it does not stay quiet when ordered, and if it is swept away by its own stubborn and forceful impulses, then it is useless to the mind. It is as useless to a general as a soldier who ignores the signal to retreat.

Therefore, if anger allows limits to be set on it, it must be called by another name. It is no longer the wild and unmanageable anger that I defined. On the other hand, if anger refuses to accept limits, it is harmful and not helpful at all. So, anger is either not true anger, or it is completely useless.

If someone insists on punishment not because they are anxious to punish, but because it is the right thing to do, that person is not angry. That person is a useful soldier who knows how to obey orders. Emotions, however, cannot obey orders, just as they cannot give orders.
