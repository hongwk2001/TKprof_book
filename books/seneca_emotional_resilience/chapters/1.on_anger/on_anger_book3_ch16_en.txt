[Chapter 16]
Unless we find something in our lives so unbearable that it drives us to suicide, let's keep anger far away from us, no matter what our position is. Anger brings ruin to the people who become slaves to it. All the madness of anger eventually turns back into their own misery, and the more stubbornly they resist, the more troublesome authority becomes. It is like a wild animal that only tightens the noose the more it struggles, or like birds that flap around trying to escape but end up getting birdlime all over their feathers. No yoke fails to hurt the person who fights against it more than the one who submits to it. The only way to ease a massive hardship is to endure it and go along with what it demands.

Controlling our emotions, especially this crazy and uncontrollable emotion called anger, is useful for subjects, but it is even more useful for kings. When a person's position allows them to do whatever their anger pushes them to do, everything is lost. Power built on hurting the majority cannot last long. The moment people who were suffering individually unite through a shared fear, that power is in danger. That is why many kings have been destroyed—some by individuals, and others by the anger of the general public, where the masses were forced to use one person as the agent of their collective anger.

However, there have been many kings, like Darius, who freely unleashed their anger as if it were a royal privilege. After overthrowing the fake Magus, he became the first ruler of Persia and most of the eastern empire. When he declared war on the Scythians, who bordered his eastern empire, an old nobleman named Oeobazus begged the king to let just one of his three sons stay home to comfort him, asking the king to be satisfied with the service of the other two. Darius promised him even more than he asked for. He said he would let all three sons stay home, and then threw their dead bodies in front of the father's eyes. It would have been less cruel if he had just taken them all to war.

How generous Xerxes was in comparison! When Pythius, the father of five sons, begged him to excuse one son from military service, the king allowed the father to choose whichever son he wanted. Then, he cut the chosen son's body in half, placed the pieces on either side of the road, and purified his army with this sacrifice. As a result, he met a fitting end—he was defeated, his army scattered in all directions, and he was completely ruined. Along the way, he walked among the corpses of his soldiers and saw the signs of his own destruction everywhere.
