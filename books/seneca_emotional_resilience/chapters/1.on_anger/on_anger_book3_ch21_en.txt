[Chapter 21]
That king got angry at a nation he didn't even know, and which hadn't done anything wrong, yet the nation still felt his wrath. Cyrus, however, got angry at a river. When he was rushing to lay siege to Babylon (because seizing the right moment is the most important thing in war), he tried to wade across the wide Gyndes River. This was barely safe even in the summer heat when the water level was at its lowest. During the crossing, one of the white horses pulling the king's chariot was swept away by the current, and this loss drove the king into a furious rage. He swore he would reduce the river that took his royal horse to such a low level that even women could wade across it and trample it under their feet.

He poured all of his army's resources into this goal. He dug 180 channels across the riverbed, dividing it into 360 streams. He kept working until the water flowed through the new channels, leaving the original riverbed completely dry. By doing this, he wasted crucial time in a major military operation, broke his soldiers' courage with useless labor, and missed his chance to attack his enemies while they were unprepared, all because he was busy fighting a war against a river.

This madness—what else could you call it?—happened to the Romans as well. Gaius Caesar destroyed a beautiful villa in Herculaneum simply because his mother had once been imprisoned there. By doing this, he made the place famous for its misfortune. When the villa was still standing, we used to sail past it without noticing it, but now people stop and ask why it is in ruins.
