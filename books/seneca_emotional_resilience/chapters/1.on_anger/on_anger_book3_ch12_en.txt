[Chapter 12]
A large part of humanity creates their own dissatisfaction by holding onto baseless suspicions or exaggerating minor issues. Anger often comes to us, but we frequently walk right into anger ourselves. We should not forcefully invite anger, and we should brush it aside even when it appears in our path. No one says to themselves, "I have done, or could have done, the exact same thing I am angry at someone else for doing." We only think about what happened, without considering the intentions of the person who did it. But we should think about the person—whether they did it on purpose or by accident, whether they were forced to do it or made a mistake, whether they did it because they hated us or to get something for themselves, whether they did it to please themselves or to help a friend.

In some cases, the offender's age or situation in life might make it the humane or practical thing to do to treat them generously and let their actions slide. Let's put ourselves in the shoes of the person we are angry with. Right now, our excessive pride in our own importance makes us quick to anger, and we are perfectly willing to do things to others that we could never stand having done to us.

No one wants to put off their anger, but delaying is the best remedy for anger. It lets the initial heat cool down, giving the clouds that darken the mind time to scatter, or at least become less dense. Some of these wrongs that make you frantic will seem much lighter after just one hour, not even a few days, and some will disappear entirely. Even if you gain nothing from delaying, whatever you do afterward will look like the result of careful thought rather than the result of anger. If you want to find out the truth about something, leave it to time. Nothing can be accurately understood in the middle of a commotion.

When Plato was angry at his slave, he could not force himself to wait. He immediately ordered the slave to take off his shirt and present his shoulders for the beating he intended to give with his own hands. But when he realized he was angry, he stopped with his hand raised in the air and stood there like a man about to strike. When a friend happened to walk in and asked what he was doing, he replied, "I am making an angry man pay for his sins." Like a man horrified by the fact that anger is a truly shameful thing for a philosopher, he froze in the posture of someone about to give in to emotion. He forgot about the slave because he had found another person who deserved to be punished.

From that moment on, he refused to exercise his authority over his own household. Once, quite angry over a mistake, he said, "Speusippus, please punish that slave with a beating for me, because I am filled with anger right now." He refused to strike the slave for the exact same reason another person would have struck him. "I am filled with anger," he said. "I would strike the slave more than he deserves, and I would feel more pleasure doing it than I should. Let's not let that slave fall into the power of a man who cannot control himself." Plato gave up his right to take revenge himself, and no one should ever want to give power to an angry person. When you are angry, you should not be allowed to do anything. The reason is that when you are angry, there is nothing you do not want to be allowed to do.
