[Chapter 20]

We must not believe that anger contributes anything to a truly great mind. What anger actually gives is not greatness, but empty arrogance. When a sick body swells up with fluid, it is not healthy growth; it is just a swollen lump of flesh. People who lose their sense of reality because of madness often mistakenly think they are inspired by high and noble thoughts. But there is no solid ground beneath them, and whatever is built without a foundation will eventually collapse.

Anger has no real ground to stand on. Anger does not rise up on a solid and lasting foundation. It is just an empty, windy trait. Anger is as far from true greatness of mind as recklessness is from courage, bragging is from confidence, gloom is from self-control, and cruelty is from strictness.

Let me say it again: there is a huge difference between a noble mind and an arrogant mind. Anger never creates anything grand or beautiful. On the contrary, getting constantly irritated looks like the action of a weak, unhappy mind that is highly aware of its own frailty. It is like people with bruised, sick bodies who scream at the lightest touch.

For this reason, anger is a vice that mostly appears in women and children.

Someone might say, "But it appears in men, too!" That is because many men also have the minds of women or children.

Our opponent might point out that angry men sometimes say things that seem to come from a truly great mind.

Yes, it might seem that way to people who do not know what true greatness is. For example, think of that nasty and hateful saying that was openly used during the time of Sulla: "Let them hate me, as long as they fear me." I do not know which of the two things he wished for is worse—being hated or being feared. He realizes that one day people will curse him, plot against him, and crush him. He adds a terrible prayer to this. May all the gods curse him for finding a cure for hatred that is just as hateful as the hatred itself!

He says, "Let them hate me," but he does not add the condition that they must obey or respect him. Instead, he says, "As long as they fear me!" Personally, I would not even want to be loved under those conditions.

This was by no means a brave statement. This is not greatness; it is monstrous behavior.

Do not believe the words of angry people. Their words are incredibly loud and threatening, but their inner minds are completely timid. When the great historian Titus Livius described someone as having "a great character rather than a good one," you should not quickly assume he was right. Those two things cannot be separated. A person must be good first, or else they can never be great.

I define greatness of mind as something unshakable, healthy to its core, solid to its foundation, and consistent. This kind of greatness cannot exist in an evil character. Evil people can be terrible, frantic, and destructive, but they can never have true greatness. Because greatness is based on goodness and gets all its power from it.

Someone might argue, "Even so, through their words, actions, and appearance, they will make people think they are great."

Yes. They might say things that sound like a great spirit. Think of Gaius Caesar. He got angry at the sky when the performance of his ballet dancers was interrupted by the weather. Ironically, he was someone who put more effort into copying their dances than watching them. When a thunderstorm ruined his party, and when lightning unfortunately missed him, he shouted a line from Homer and challenged Jupiter to a fight to the death. "Either you take me down, or I will take you down!"

How massive his madness was! He must have believed that not even Jupiter could hurt him, or that he was strong enough to hurt Jupiter. I think these words of his played a big part in finally giving the assassins the courage to kill him. After all, having to endure a man that even Jupiter could not put up with must have seemed like the ultimate test of patience.
