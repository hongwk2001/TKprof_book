[Book 1: Recalled to Life]

[Chapter 1]
The Period

It was the best of times. It was the worst of times. It was the age of wisdom, and the age of foolishness. It was the epoch of belief, and the epoch of incredulity. It was the season of light, and the season of darkness. It was the spring of hope, and the winter of despair. We had everything before us, and we had nothing before us. We were all going directly to Heaven, and we were all going directly the other way. In short, the period was so much like the present day, that some of its loudest voices insisted on judging it only in extremes, for good or for evil.

A king with a large jaw and a queen with a plain face sat on the throne of England. A king with a large jaw and a queen with a fair face sat on the throne of France. In both countries, it was crystal clear to the wealthy lords in power that things were settled forever.

It was the year one thousand seven hundred and seventy-five. Spiritual revelations were granted to England at that time, just as they are today. Mrs. Southcott had recently celebrated her twenty-fifth birthday. A prophetic soldier in the Life Guards had heralded her appearance by announcing that London and Westminster were about to be swallowed up. The famous Cock-lane ghost had been put to rest only twelve years earlier, after tapping out its messages, much like the equally unoriginal spirits of the past year. Meanwhile, ordinary messages in the earthly order of events had recently reached the English Crown and its people from a congress of British subjects in America. Strange as it may seem, those political messages have proven far more important to the human race than any communications ever received from spirits or ghosts.

France was less favored in spiritual matters than her sister nation across the channel. Instead, she rolled smoothly downhill, printing paper money and spending it rapidly. Under the guidance of her religious leaders, France entertained herself with supposedly humane achievements. For example, she sentenced a young man to have his hands cut off, his tongue torn out with pincers, and his body burned alive. His only crime was failing to kneel in the rain to honor a dirty procession of monks passing fifty yards away. It is highly likely that while this sufferer was being put to death, trees were growing in the woods of France and Norway that had already been marked by Fate, the Woodman. These trees were destined to be cut down and sawn into boards to build a terrible movable framework with a sack and a heavy dropping knife. It is also likely that in the rough barns of farmers near Paris, rough carts sat sheltered from the weather that very day. These carts, covered in mud and surrounded by pigs and poultry, had already been chosen by Death, the Farmer, to serve as the tumbrils of the Revolution. But that Woodman and that Farmer work silently, even though they work without pausing. No one heard them walking with their muffled steps. In fact, to even suspect that they were awake was considered traitorous and against religion.

In England, there was barely enough order and protection to justify any national pride. Daring burglaries by armed men and highway robberies took place in the capital every single night. Families were publicly warned not to leave town without first moving their furniture to secure warehouses. A highwayman in the dark could easily be a respectable city tradesman during the day. When one such highwayman was recognized by a fellow tradesman he had stopped, he simply shot the man through the head and rode away. The mail coach was ambushed by seven robbers. The guard shot three of them dead, but was then killed by the other four because he ran out of ammunition. After that, the mail was robbed in total peace. The powerful Lord Mayor of London was forced to hand over his valuables by a single highwayman, who robbed the important official in plain sight of his entire staff. Prisoners in London jails fought bloody battles with their guards, and the law responded by firing heavy shotguns directly into the crowds of inmates. Thieves secretly snipped diamond crosses from the necks of noble lords right inside the royal court. Soldiers went into the slums to search for illegal goods, and the mob fired on the soldiers while the soldiers fired back at the mob. Nobody considered any of these events to be unusual. In the midst of it all, the hangman was constantly busy, though his work was entirely useless. One moment he was hanging long rows of miscellaneous criminals. The next, he was hanging a burglar on Saturday who had just been caught on Tuesday. He burned the hands of dozens of prisoners at Newgate prison, and burned rebellious pamphlets at the door of Westminster Hall. Today he would take the life of a brutal murderer, and tomorrow he would hang a desperate thief who had stolen a mere sixpence from a farmer's boy.

All these things, and a thousand others just like them, happened in the year one thousand seven hundred and seventy-five. Surrounded by this chaos, the Woodman and the Farmer worked unnoticed. Meanwhile, the two kings with large jaws and the two queens with plain and fair faces went about their noisy business, enforcing their divine rights with a heavy hand. In this way, the year one thousand seven hundred and seventy-five led their royal highnesses, along with thousands of ordinary people, down the roads that lay before them. The characters of our story were among that ordinary crowd, traveling the very same paths.
