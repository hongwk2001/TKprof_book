CHAPTER 8. THE PALADIN OF THE THIRD

You'll remember that Monsieur Le Chevalier de Chabrillane was deeply involved in the unjust affair where Philippe de Vilmorin lost his life. We know enough to guess that he wasn't just La Tour d’Azyr’s second in the duel, but actually helped instigate the whole thing. André-Louis might have felt a justifiable satisfaction in offering the Chevalier’s life to the spirit of his murdered friend. He might have seen it as an act of simple justice, unobtainable by any other means. It's also worth remembering that Chabrillane went confidently to the meeting. He believed that he, a skilled swordsman, was dealing with a commoner completely unskilled with a blade. Morally, then, he was little better than a murderer. His fall into the very trap he thought he'd dug for André-Louis was a poetic retribution. Yet, despite all this, I would find André-Louis's cynical announcement of the outcome to the Assembly utterly detestable if I believed it was sincere. It would confirm Aline's opinion, shared by many others who knew him well, that André-Louis was completely heartless.

You've seen a similar heartlessness in his behavior when he discovered La Binet's betrayal, though his actions to avenge himself contradict that. I believe his subsequent contempt for the woman stemmed from the affection he once held for her. I don't believe this affection was as deep as he first imagined. But I also don't believe it was as shallow as he almost went out of his way to make it seem, by pretending to completely erase her from his mind once he discovered her worthlessness. As I've said, his actions don't support that belief either. Again, his callous cynicism in hoping he had killed Binet is also an affectation. Knowing that people like Binet are better out of the world, he felt no remorse. You must remember, he possessed that rare, balanced perspective that sees things in their true proportions, never exaggerating or diminishing them with sentiment. At the same time, it's quite incredible that he could contemplate taking a life with such complete and cynical composure, no matter the justification.

Similarly now, it's hard to believe that, coming straight from the Bois de Boulogne, straight from killing a man, he was genuinely expressing his true nature by referring to the event with such outrageous flippancy. He wasn't quite the full incarnation of Scaramouche to that extent. But he was enough of an actor to always mask his true feelings with a striking gesture, and his true thoughts with an impactful phrase. He was always the actor, constantly calculating the effect he would produce, always avoiding self-revelation, always keen to cover his real character with an assumed, completely fictitious one. There was something impish about this, and something else too.

Nobody laughed at his flippancy now. He didn't intend for anyone to. He intended to be terrifying. He knew that the more flippant and casual his tone, the more terrifying its effect would be. He achieved exactly the effect he wanted.

What happened next, in a place where emotions and customs had devolved so much, isn't hard to guess. When the session ended, a dozen hired swordsmen awaited him in the vestibule. This time, the men of his own party were less concerned about guarding him. He seemed entirely capable of guarding himself. Despite all his caution, he appeared to have so completely taken the fight to the enemy's camp, so thoroughly adopted their own methods, that his companions barely felt the need to protect him as they had yesterday.

As he emerged, he scanned the hostile line of men, whose demeanor and clothing clearly marked them for what they were. He paused, looking for the man he expected, the one he was most eager to confront. But Monsieur de La Tour d’Azyr was absent from those eager ranks. This struck him as odd. La Tour d’Azyr was Chabrillane’s cousin and closest friend. Surely he should have been among the first today. The truth was, La Tour d’Azyr was too deeply overwhelmed by amazement and grief at the utterly unexpected event. His vindictiveness was also curiously held in check. Perhaps he, too, remembered Chabrillane's role in the Gavrillac affair. He might have seen this obscure André-Louis Moreau, who had so persistently tormented him ever since, as a destined avenger. The reluctance he felt to confront him, especially after this ultimate provocation, was puzzling even to himself. But it existed, and it curbed him now.

To André-Louis, since La Tour wasn't among that waiting pack, it mattered little on that Tuesday morning who would be next. As it happened, the next was the young Vicomte de La Motte-Royau, one of the deadliest swordsmen in the group.

On Wednesday morning, André-Louis arrived at the Assembly an hour or so late. He announced, in much the same terms as he had announced Chabrillane's death, that Monsieur de La Motte-Royau probably wouldn't disturb the Assembly's harmony for some weeks to come. This was assuming, of course, that he was fortunate enough to eventually recover from the effects of an unpleasant accident he had quite unexpectedly met with that morning.

On Thursday, he made an identical announcement regarding the Vidame de Blavon. On Friday, he told them he had been delayed by Monsieur de Troiscantins. Then, turning to the members of the Cote Droit, he adopted an expression of sympathetic gravity:

“I'm glad to inform you, messieurs, that Monsieur des Troiscantins is in the hands of a very competent surgeon. The surgeon hopes, with careful treatment, to restore him to your councils in a few weeks' time.”

It was paralyzing, fantastic, unreal. Both friends and foes in that assembly sat equally stupefied by those bland daily announcements. Four of the most formidable master duelists put out of action for a time, one of them dead—and all this done with such an air of indifference, announced in such casual terms by a wretched little provincial lawyer!

He began to take on a romantic aura in their eyes. Even that group of philosophers from the Cote Gauche, who refused to worship any force but the force of reason, began to look upon him with a respect and consideration that no oratorical triumphs could ever have earned him.

And from the Assembly, his fame gradually spread throughout Paris. Desmoulins wrote a glowing tribute about him in his paper, “Les Revolutions.” In it, he dubbed him the “Paladin of the Third Estate,” a name that captured the people's imagination and stuck with him for some time. He was disdainfully mentioned in the “Actes des Apotres,” the mocking publication of the Privileged party. This was light-heartedly and provocatively edited by a group of gentlemen afflicted by a singular mental shortsightedness.

On the Friday of that very busy week, this young man, who even later would insist he was not a man of action, found the Manege vestibule empty of swordsmen. He made his leisurely and expectant exit between Le Chapelier and Kersain.

He was so surprised that he stopped in his tracks.

“Have they had enough?” he wondered, asking Le Chapelier.

“They've had enough of you, I should think,” came the answer. “They'll prefer to turn their attention to someone less able to take care of himself.”

Now, this was disappointing. André-Louis had committed himself to this business with a very definite goal in mind. The slaying of Chabrillane had been satisfactory, as far as it went. He had considered that an acceptable appetizer. But the three who had followed were no concern of his at all. He had met them with a certain reluctance, and dealt with each as lightly as his own safety allowed. Was the baiting of him now to stop while the man he was aiming for hadn't presented himself? In that case, it would be necessary to force the issue!

Out there under the awning, a group of gentlemen stood in earnest conversation. Scanning the group with a rapid glance, André-Louis spotted Monsieur de La Tour d’Azyr among them. He tightened his lips. He mustn't offer any provocation. They had to be the ones to pick a fight with him. Already that morning, the “Actes des Apotres” had torn the mask from his face. It had proclaimed him the fencing-master of the Rue du Hasard, successor to Bertrand des Amis. Engaging in single combat had been hazardous for a man of his status even before this. Now, with this exposure, presented to the public as an aristocratic apology, it was doubly so.

Still, things couldn't be left as they were, or all his efforts would have been for nothing. Carefully looking away from that group of gentlemen, he raised his voice so his words would carry to their ears.

“It's beginning to look as if my fears of having to spend the rest of my days in the Bois were groundless.”

Out of the corner of his eye, he caught the stir his words created in that group. Its members had turned to look at him, but for the moment, that was all. A little more was needed. Pacing slowly between his friends, he resumed:

“But isn't it remarkable that Lagron's assassin makes no move against Lagron’s successor? Or perhaps it's not remarkable. Perhaps there are good reasons. Perhaps the gentleman is prudent.”

He had passed the group by now. He let that last sentence of his trail behind him, and after it, he sent laughter—insolent and provoking.

He didn't have long to wait. A quick step came behind him, and a hand falling on his shoulder spun him violently around. He was brought face to face with Monsieur de La Tour d’Azyr. The Marquis's handsome face was calm and composed, but his eyes reflected something of the sudden blaze of passion stirring within him. Behind him, several members of the group were approaching more slowly. The others, like André-Louis’ two companions, remained watching.

“You spoke of me, I think,” the Marquis said quietly.

“I spoke of an assassin—yes. But to these, my friends.” André-Louis’s manner was no less quiet; in fact, he was the quieter of the two, for he was the more experienced actor.

“You spoke loudly enough to be overheard,” the Marquis said, answering the insinuation that he had been eavesdropping.

"People who want to eavesdrop usually find a way."

"I see you're trying to be offensive."

"Oh, but you're mistaken, Monsieur le Marquis. I have no desire to be offensive. However, I resent being violently touched, especially by hands I consider unclean. Given the circumstances, you can hardly expect me to be polite."

The older man's eyelids flickered. He almost found himself admiring André-Louis's composure. Instead, he worried that his own composure suffered by comparison. This thought completely enraged him, and he lost control.

"You called me Lagron's assassin. I won't pretend to misunderstand you. You explained your views to me once before, and I remember them."

"What flattery, monsieur!"

"You called me an assassin then, because I used my skill to get rid of a hot-headed troublemaker who made the world unsafe for me. But how much better are you, Monsieur the fencing-master, when you face men whose skill is naturally inferior to yours!"

Monsieur de La Tour d'Azyr's friends looked serious and troubled. It was truly incredible to see this great gentleman forget himself so much as to argue with a common lawyer-swordsman. What was worse, he was being made to look ridiculous in the argument.

"I face them?" André-Louis said with amused protest. "Ah, pardon me, Monsieur le Marquis; it is they who chose to face me, and so stupidly. They push me, they slap my face, they step on my toes, they call me unpleasant names. What if I am a fencing-master? Must I, because of that, submit to every kind of mistreatment from your ill-mannered friends? Perhaps if they had found out sooner that I am a fencing-master, their manners would have been better. But to blame me for that! What injustice!"

"Comedian!" the Marquis contemptuously called him. "Does that change anything? Are these men who have faced you people who live by the sword, like yourself?"

"On the contrary, Monsieur le Marquis, I have found them to be men who died by the sword with astonishing ease. I cannot imagine you wish to join their ranks."

"And why, if you please?" La Tour d'Azyr's face had turned scarlet at that sneer.

"Oh," André-Louis raised his eyebrows and pursed his lips, like a man deep in thought. He spoke slowly. "Because, monsieur, you prefer the easy victim, the Lagrons and Vilmorins of this world, mere sheep for your butchering. That is why."

And then the Marquis struck him.

André-Louis stepped back. His eyes gleamed for a moment; the next, they were smiling up into the face of his tall enemy.

"No better than the others, after all! Well, well! Notice, I beg you, how history repeats itself, with certain differences. Poor Vilmorin struck you because he couldn't bear a vile lie you used to provoke him. You strike me because you cannot bear an equally vile truth I have spoken. But the vileness is always yours. And now, as then, for the one who strikes, there is..." He broke off. "But why name it? You will remember what there is. You yourself wrote it that day with the point of your too-ready sword. But there. I will meet you if you desire it, monsieur."

"What else do you suppose I desire? To talk?"

André-Louis turned to his friends and sighed. "So, I'm to go on another trip to the Bois. Isaac, perhaps you would kindly speak with one of Monsieur le Marquis's friends and arrange for nine o'clock tomorrow, as usual."

"Not tomorrow," the Marquis said curtly to Le Chapelier. "I have an engagement in the country that I cannot postpone."

Le Chapelier looked at André-Louis.

"Then, for Monsieur le Marquis's convenience, we will say Sunday at the same hour."

"I do not fight on Sunday. I am not a pagan to break the holy day."

"But surely the good God would not presume to damn a gentleman of Monsieur le Marquis's standing on that account? Ah, well, Isaac, please arrange for Monday, if it is not a feast-day or if monsieur does not have some other pressing engagement. I leave it in your hands."

He bowed with the air of a man wearied by these details, and, threading his arm through Kersain's, he withdrew.

"Ah, good heavens! But what a way you have with words," said the Breton deputy, who was entirely unsophisticated in these matters.

"Indeed I do. I've learned from them." He laughed. He was in excellent spirits. And Kersain was now among those who considered André-Louis a man without heart or conscience.

But in his "Confessions," he tells us, and this is one of the glimpses that reveal the true man beneath all that pretense, that on that night he went down on his knees to commune with his dead friend Philippe. He called Philippe's spirit to witness that he was about to take the final step in fulfilling the oath sworn upon his body at Gavrillac two years ago.
