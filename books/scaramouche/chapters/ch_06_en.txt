[Book 1 - Chapter 6: THE WINDMILL]

Chapter 6: THE WINDMILL

A regular stagecoach service ran three times a week between Nantes and Rennes in both directions. For twenty-four livres, which was about the same as an English guinea, it would carry a passenger the seventy-odd miles in about fourteen hours. Once a week, one of the coaches would detour from the main road to stop at Gavrillac. It delivered mail and newspapers, and it occasionally picked up passengers. André-Louis usually took this coach when he needed to travel. But right now, he was in too much of a hurry to wait a whole day for it. Instead, he hired a horse from the local Breton inn and set out the next morning. He rode briskly for an hour under a gray winter sky. The half-ruined road took him through ten miles of flat, uninteresting country until he finally reached the city of Rennes.

He rode across the main bridge over the Vilaine River. This brought him into the upper and principal part of that important city. Rennes had about thirty thousand residents. Judging by the loud and restless crowds blocking his way everywhere, André-Louis guessed that almost all of them had taken to the streets today. Clearly, Philippe had not exaggerated the excitement sweeping through the area.

He pushed forward as best he could and finally reached the Place Royale. The crowd was incredibly dense here. A pale young man stood on the base of the equestrian statue of King Louis 15 and excitedly addressed the multitude. His youth and clothing showed that he was a student. A group of his classmates acted as an honor guard and kept the immediate area around the statue clear.

Over the heads of the crowd, André-Louis caught a few phrases shouted by the eager speaker.

"It was the promise of the King! It is the King's authority they ignore! They claim total power over Brittany for themselves. The King has dissolved them! These insolent nobles are defying their sovereign and the people!"

Philippe had already told him about the events pushing the Third Estate toward active revolt. But even if he had not known, those few phrases would have explained everything. André-Louis thought this public anger was perfect for his own needs. He hoped the unrest might make the King's Lieutenant more reasonable. With this in mind, he pushed on up the wide, paved Rue Royale, where the crowd began to thin out. He left his hired horse at the Corne de Cerf inn and set out on foot toward the Palais de Justice.

A noisy mob was fighting near the wooden poles and scaffolding around the cathedral. Construction on the building had started a year ago, but André-Louis did not stop to figure out why the crowd had gathered there. He kept walking and soon arrived at the handsome Italian-style palace. It was one of the few public buildings that had survived the terrible fire sixty years ago.

He struggled through the doors and finally reached the great hall, which was known as the Hall of Lost Steps. There, he managed to find an usher who agreed to pass a message to the powerful official in charge of this shrine of Justice. The usher announced that a lawyer from Gavrillac humbly requested an audience regarding a very serious matter. After that, André-Louis was left waiting for a full half-hour.

The powerful official only agreed to see him because of the tense political climate. Finally, André-Louis was escorted up the broad stone staircase. He was led into a large, barely furnished waiting room. He joined a crowd of other waiting clients, who were mostly men.

He spent another half-hour there. He used the time to plan exactly what he would say. As he thought about it, he realized the weakness of his case. He was about to present his argument to a man whose views on law and morality were entirely shaped by his high social rank.

At last, he was guided through a narrow but massive, highly decorated door. He entered a beautiful, bright room. It was filled with enough gold trim and satin to furnish a fashionable lady's private bedroom.

It was a silly setting for a King's Lieutenant, but there was nothing silly about the King's Lieutenant himself, at least to normal people. That exalted man sat at the far end of the room, to the right of a tall window looking over the inner courtyard. He worked at an ornate desk decorated with paintings and heavy gold bronze. He wore a scarlet coat with a bright medal on its chest. A cloud of lace spilled from his collar, holding diamonds that sparkled like drops of water. Above all this sat the massive, powdered head of Monsieur de Lesdiguières. He leaned back and scowled at his visitor. He looked so arrogant that André-Louis wondered if the man expected him to drop to his knees.

The official saw a lean young man with a sharp jaw and straight, limp black hair. André-Louis wore a brown riding coat with a cape, yellow buckskin pants, and knee-boots splashed with mud. Seeing this, the scowl on the mighty official's face deepened until his thick black eyebrows met above his large hooked nose.

"You announce yourself as a lawyer of Gavrillac with an important communication," he growled. His tone was an absolute command. He expected André-Louis to speak quickly and not waste the valuable time of a King's Lieutenant. Monsieur de Lesdiguières considered himself a terrifying figure, and he had good reason to think so. Over the years, he had seen many poor men scared out of their wits by his thunderous voice.

He waited to see the same fear strike this young lawyer from Gavrillac. But he waited in vain.

André-Louis found him completely ridiculous. He knew that showing off was just a mask for weakness and a lack of real worth. And right here, he was looking at arrogance in the flesh. It was obvious in the haughty tilt of the man's head, his dark scowl, and the deep boom of his voice. It is hard for a man to look like a hero to his own servant, who sees him put on his impressive clothes piece by piece. It is even harder for a man to look like a hero to someone who studies human nature and sees through those tricks.

André-Louis stepped forward boldly. Monsieur de Lesdiguières thought it was downright disrespectful.

"You are His Majesty's Lieutenant here in Brittany," André-Louis said. To the mighty lord of life and death, it seemed this young man had the incredible nerve to speak to him as an equal. "You are the one who hands out the King's highest justice in this province."

Surprise spread across the official's handsome, pale face beneath his heavy white wig.

"Is your business about this terrible rebellion of the common people?" he asked.

"It is not, sir."

The black eyebrows rose. "Then why the devil are you bothering me now? All my attention is needed for this obvious and disgraceful emergency!"

"The matter that brings me here is just as disgraceful and just as urgent."

"It will have to wait!" the great man thundered in a rage. He tossed back the lace from his wrist and reached for the small silver bell on his desk.

"Wait a moment, sir!" André-Louis spoke with a commanding tone. Monsieur de Lesdiguières stopped, absolutely amazed by the young man's boldness. "I can explain it very quickly."

"Haven't I already told you..."

"And when you have heard it," André-Louis continued relentlessly, cutting off the official's interruption, "you will agree with me about how serious it is."

Monsieur de Lesdiguières stared at him with stern anger.

"What is your name?" he asked.

"André-Louis Moreau."

"Very well, André-Louis Moreau. If you can make your case quickly, I will listen. But I warn you, I will be extremely angry if you cannot justify your rude persistence at such a bad time."

"You shall be the judge of that, sir," André-Louis said. He started explaining his case immediately. He began with the shooting of Mabey, and then moved on to the killing of Monsieur de Vilmorin. But he held back the name of the powerful gentleman he was accusing until the very end. He was sure that if he said the name too early, he would not be allowed to finish.

He had a natural talent for public speaking. He did not fully realize his own power yet, though he was destined to discover it very soon. He told his story well. He avoided exaggeration, but his simple words had an irresistible force. Gradually, the great man's forbidding face relaxed. A look of interest, and almost sympathy, appeared on his features.

"And who, sir, is the man you accuse of this?"

"The Marquis de La Tour d'Azyr."

That terrifying name had an immediate effect. The official's face shifted from sympathy to shocked anger, followed by an arrogance even worse than before.

"Who?" he shouted. Without waiting for an answer, he stormed on. "What incredible nerve! To come to me with a charge like this against a man as important as Monsieur de La Tour d'Azyr! How dare you call him a coward."

"I call him a murderer," the young man corrected him. "And I demand justice against him."

"You demand it, do you? My God, what next?"

"That is for you to say, sir."

The bold reply surprised the great gentleman so much that he made a successful effort to control himself.

"Let me warn you," he said bitterly. "It is not wise to make wild accusations against a nobleman. Doing that is a punishable crime, as you may soon learn. Now listen to me. Let us assume your story about Mabey is exact. The gamekeeper might have gone slightly past his duty, but it is such a minor thing it is hardly worth mentioning. Consider, too, that this is not a matter for the King's Lieutenant. It belongs entirely to the local court controlled by Monsieur de La Tour d'Azyr himself. His own appointed judges must handle it, since it involves his own land and power. Since you are a lawyer, you shouldn't need me to tell you that."

"As a lawyer, I am prepared to argue that point. But as a lawyer, I also realize that if we pushed the case, it would only end in the unfair punishment of a wretched gamekeeper. He was just following orders, but they would use him as a scapegoat if they needed one. I am not interested in hanging Benet on the gallows that Monsieur de La Tour d'Azyr deserves."

Monsieur de Lesdiguières slammed his hand violently onto the table. "My God!" he cried out. Then he added more quietly, with a threatening edge to his voice, "You are remarkably insolent, my man."

"I assure you, sir, that is not my intention. I am a lawyer arguing a case. It is the case of Monsieur de Vilmorin. I have come to beg the King's justice for his murder."

"But you just said it was a duel!" cried the Lieutenant, caught between anger and utter confusion.

"I said that it was made to look like a duel. There is a huge difference, as I will show you, if you will kindly hear me out."

"Take your time, sir!" said Monsieur de Lesdiguières sarcastically. In all his time in office, he had never experienced anything remotely like this.

André-Louis took the invitation literally. "I thank you, sir," he answered seriously, and presented his argument. "We can prove that Monsieur de Vilmorin never practiced fencing a single day in his life. At the same time, everyone knows that Monsieur de La Tour d'Azyr is an exceptional swordsman. Sir, can you call it a duel when only one of the fighters is truly armed? When you compare their levels of skill, it amounts to exactly that."

"You could make the same cheap argument about almost every duel ever fought."

"But it wouldn't always be as fair or true. And in at least one case, that exact argument was used successfully in court."

"Successfully? When was that?"

"Ten years ago, in the province of Dauphiny. I am talking about the case of Monsieur de Gesvres, a gentleman there who forced Monsieur de la Roche Jeannine into a duel and killed him. Monsieur de Jeannine belonged to a powerful family, and they worked hard to get justice. They used the exact same arguments that apply now to Monsieur de La Tour d'Azyr. As you might remember, the judges decided that Monsieur de Gesvres intentionally provoked the fight. They found him guilty of premeditated murder, and he was hanged."

Monsieur de Lesdiguières exploded in anger once again. "By my life!" he cried. "Do you have the absolute nerve to suggest that Monsieur de La Tour d'Azyr should be hanged? Do you?"

"But why not, sir? If it is the law, and if there is a legal precedent for it, as I just showed you. We can easily prove that what I am saying is the truth."

"You ask me why not? Do you really have the boldness to ask me that?"

"I do, sir. Can you answer me? If you cannot, sir, then I will understand how things really work. I will understand that the law can be put into motion for a powerful family like La Roche Jeannine. But it must remain completely paralyzed for obscure, ordinary people, no matter how brutally they have been wronged by a great nobleman."

Monsieur de Lesdiguières realized that arguing would accomplish nothing against this calm and determined young man. His tone became much more threatening.

"I advise you to leave at once," he warned. "You should be thankful that I am letting you walk out of here unharmed."

"Then I am supposed to understand, sir, that there will be no investigation into this case? That nothing I say will change your mind?"

"You are to understand that if you are still standing there in two minutes, things will go very badly for you." Monsieur de Lesdiguières rang the silver handbell on his desk.

"I have informed you, sir, that a supposed duel has been fought and a man was killed," André-Louis said. "It seems I must remind you, the administrator of the King's justice, that duels are strictly against the law. It is your official duty to hold an inquiry. I stand here as the legal representative of Monsieur de Vilmorin's grieving mother. I demand the investigation that she is owed."

The door behind André-Louis opened softly. Monsieur de Lesdiguières was pale with anger, barely able to control himself.

"You seek to force our hand, do you, you impudent rascal?" he growled. "You think the King's justice can be pushed around by the voice of any loudmouthed commoner? I am amazed at my own patience with you. But I give you one last warning, master lawyer. Keep a much closer guard over that disrespectful tongue of yours, or you will have very bitter cause to regret how quickly it moves." He waved a jeweled hand in contempt and spoke to the usher standing behind André-Louis. "Show him to the door!" he ordered shortly.

André-Louis hesitated for a second. Then, with a shrug, he turned around. This was indeed a windmill, and he was nothing but a poor, sad-faced knight. To attack it up close would just mean being smashed to pieces. Yet, as he reached the doorway, he turned back one last time.

"Monsieur de Lesdiguières," he said. "May I tell you an interesting fact about natural history? The tiger is a great lord in the jungle. For centuries, he was the terror of lesser beasts, including the wolf. But the wolf, who is also a hunter, eventually grew tired of being hunted. He began to associate with other wolves. Soon, they were driven to form packs to protect themselves. These wolves discovered the power of the pack. They began hunting the tiger, and it ended disastrously for him. You should really study the naturalist Buffon, Monsieur de Lesdiguières."

"I think I have studied a buffoon this morning," Monsieur de Lesdiguières sneered, making a cruel joke. If he hadn't thought he was being so clever, he probably wouldn't have bothered to answer at all. "I don't understand you," he added.

"But you will, Monsieur de Lesdiguières. You will," said André-Louis, and with that, he departed.
