Chapter 6. THE WINDMILL

Between Nantes and Rennes, three stage-coaches ran weekly in each direction. For twenty-four livres, roughly the cost of an English guinea, they would carry you the seventy-odd miles in about fourteen hours. Once a week, one of these diligences, traveling in each direction, would turn off the main road to stop at Gavrillac. It would deliver and pick up letters, newspapers, and occasionally passengers. André-Louis usually traveled by this coach when he had the chance. However, he was in too much of a hurry now to wait a whole day for that diligence. So, the next morning, he set out on a horse hired from the Breton arme. An hour's brisk ride under a gray, wintry sky, along a half-ruined road through ten miles of flat, uninteresting country, brought him to the city of Rennes.

He rode across the main bridge over the Vilaine River, entering the upper and main part of that important city. With about thirty thousand inhabitants, Rennes was bustling. From the seething, clamoring crowds blocking his way everywhere, he guessed that most of the population must have taken to the streets that day. Clearly, Philippe had not exaggerated the excitement in the city.

He pushed on as best he could, finally reaching the Place Royale, where the crowd was densest. From the base of the equestrian statue of Louis XV, a pale-faced young man was excitedly addressing the crowd. His youth and clothing marked him as a student. A group of his peers, acting as an honor guard, kept the immediate area around the statue clear.

Over the heads of the crowd, André-Louis caught a few phrases from that eager voice.

“It was the King’s promise... They defy the King’s authority... They claim all sovereignty in Brittany for themselves. The King has dissolved them... These arrogant nobles are defying their sovereign and the people...”

Even if he hadn't already known from Philippe what had driven the Tiers État to active revolt, those few phrases would have fully informed him. This public display of anger was very timely for his purpose, he thought. Hoping it might make the King’s Lieutenant more reasonable, he pushed on up the wide, well-paved Rue Royale. There, the crowd of people began to thin out. He stabled his hired horse at the Corne de Cerf, then set out again on foot for the Palais de Justice.

A brawling mob stood by the poles and scaffolding around the cathedral, where construction had begun a year ago. But he didn't stop to find out the specific reason for that gathering. He strode on, soon arriving at the handsome Italianate palace. It was one of the few public buildings that had survived the devastating fire sixty years earlier.

He made his way with difficulty to the great hall, known as the Salle des Pas Perdus. There, he was left to cool his heels for a full half-hour. This was after he finally found an usher condescending enough to inform the "god" who presided over that shrine of Justice that a lawyer from Gavrillac humbly requested an audience on a serious matter.

That the "god" deigned to see him at all was probably due to the serious nature of the times. Finally, he was escorted up the broad stone staircase and ushered into a spacious, sparsely furnished anteroom. He joined a waiting crowd of clients, most of whom were men.

He spent another half-hour there, using the time to consider exactly what he should say. This reflection made him realize the weakness of the case he intended to present to a man whose views on law and morality were colored by his social rank.

At last, he was ushered through a narrow but very massive and richly decorated door. It led into a fine, well-lit room, furnished with enough gilt and satin to supply a fashionable lady’s boudoir.

It was a trivial setting for a King’s Lieutenant. However, about the King’s Lieutenant himself, there was nothing trivial, at least to ordinary eyes. At the far end of the chamber, to the right of one of the tall windows overlooking the inner court, sat that exalted being. He was seated before a goat-legged writing table with Watteau panels, heavily encrusted with ormolu. Above a scarlet coat, with an order blazing on its breast and a billow of lace where diamonds sparkled like drops of water, rose the massive, powdered head of M. de Lesdiguieres. His head was thrown back, scowling at this visitor with an expectant arrogance. It made André-Louis almost wonder if a genuflection was expected from him.

He saw a lean, lantern-jawed young man with straight, lank black hair. The man wore a caped riding coat of brown cloth and yellow buckskin breeches, his knee-boots splashed with mud. The scowl on that august face deepened, drawing together the thick black eyebrows above the great hooked nose.

He growled, “You announce yourself as a lawyer from Gavrillac with an important communication.” It was a peremptory command to deliver this message without wasting the valuable time of a King’s Lieutenant. The tone conveyed more than a hint of his immense importance. M. de Lesdiguieres considered himself an imposing personality, and he had every reason to do so. In his time, he had seen many a poor soul terrified out of their wits by the thunder of his voice.

He waited now to see the same thing happen to this young lawyer from Gavrillac. But he waited in vain.

André-Louis found him ridiculous. He knew pretentiousness was a mask for worthlessness and weakness. Here, he saw pretentiousness personified. It was evident in the arrogant tilt of his head, his scowling brow, and the inflection of his booming voice. It is difficult for a man to be a hero to his valet, who has witnessed the dispersal of the parts that make up the imposing whole. It is even more difficult for a man to be a hero to a student of human nature, who has witnessed the same in a different sense.

André-Louis stepped forward boldly. M. de Lesdiguieres thought it was impudently.

André-Louis said, “You are His Majesty’s Lieutenant here in Brittany.” To the august lord of life and death, it almost seemed that this fellow had the incredible audacity to address him as one man speaking to another. He continued, “You are the dispenser of the King’s high justice in this province.”

Surprise spread across that handsome, sallow face beneath the heavily powdered wig.

He asked, “Is your business concerned with this infernal insubordination of the canaille?”

“It is not, monsieur.”

The black eyebrows rose. “Then what the devil do you mean by intruding upon me,” he asked, “at a time when all my attention is claimed by the obvious urgency of this disgraceful affair?”

“The affair that brings me is no less disgraceful and no less urgent.”

“It will have to wait!” the great man thundered in a passion. Tossing back a cloud of lace from his hand, he reached for the little silver bell on his table.

“A moment, monsieur!” André-Louis’ tone was peremptory. M. de Lesdiguieres stopped, in sheer amazement at his impudence. “I can state it very briefly...”

“Haven’t I said already...”

André-Louis went on relentlessly, interrupting the interruption. “And when you have heard it,” he said, “you will agree with me as to its character.”

M. de Lesdiguieres considered him very sternly.

"What is your name?" he asked.

"André-Louis Moreau."

"Well, André-Louis Moreau, I will hear you if you can state your plea briefly. But I warn you, I will be very angry if you fail to justify the impertinence of this insistence at such an inopportune moment."

"You shall be the judge of that, monsieur," André-Louis said. He immediately began to state his case. He started with the shooting of Mabey, then moved on to the killing of Monsieur de Vilmorin. He held back the name of the great gentleman against whom he demanded justice until the very end. He was convinced that if he introduced it earlier, he would not be allowed to continue.

He possessed a gift for public speaking, though he was hardly aware of its full power yet. He was destined to become very conscious of it soon. He told his story well, without exaggeration, but with an irresistible force of simple appeal. Gradually, the great man's face relaxed from its forbidding severity. Interest, warming almost to sympathy, began to show on it.

"And who, sir, is the man you are charging with this?"

"The Marquis de La Tour d'Azyr."

The effect of that formidable name was immediate. Dismayed anger, and an arrogance more complete than before, replaced the sympathy he had inadvertently shown.

"Who?" he shouted. Without waiting for an answer, he stormed on, "Why, this is impudence! To come before me with such a charge against a gentleman of Monsieur de La Tour d'Azyr's eminence! How dare you speak of him as a coward..."

"I speak of him as a murderer," the young man corrected. "And I demand justice against him."

"You demand it, do you? My God, what next?"

"That is for you to say, monsieur."

This surprised the great gentleman, prompting him into a more or less successful effort of self-control.

"Let me warn you," he said acidly, "it is not wise to make wild accusations against a nobleman. That, in itself, is a punishable offense, as you may learn. Now listen to me. In this matter of Mabey—assuming your statement of it is exact—the gamekeeper may have exceeded his duty. But it was by so little that it's hardly worth comment. Consider, however, that in any case, it is not a matter for the King's Lieutenant, or for any court other than Monsieur de La Tour d'Azyr's own seigneurial court. Such a matter must be brought before the magistrates he himself appoints, since it strictly concerns his own seigneurial jurisdiction. As a lawyer, you should not need to be told so much."

"As a lawyer, I am prepared to argue the point. But as a lawyer, I also realize that if that case were prosecuted, it could only end in the unjust punishment of a wretched gamekeeper. He did no more than carry out his orders, but he would nonetheless be made a scapegoat if one were necessary. I am not concerned with hanging Benet on the gallows earned by Monsieur de La Tour d'Azyr."

Monsieur de Lesdiguieres struck the table violently. "My God!" he cried out. Then he added more quietly, with a note of menace, "You are singularly insolent, my man."

"That is not my intention, sir, I assure you. I am a lawyer, pleading a case, the case of Monsieur de Vilmorin. It is for his assassination that I have come to beg the King's justice."

"But you yourself said it was a duel!" cried the Lieutenant, caught between anger and bewilderment.

"I said that it was made to appear a duel. There is a distinction, as I will show, if you will condescend to hear me out."

"Take your own time, sir!" said the ironical Monsieur de Lesdiguieres. His tenure of office had never yet involved anything that remotely resembled this experience.

André-Louis took him literally. "I thank you, sir," he answered solemnly, and then submitted his argument. "It can be shown that Monsieur de Vilmorin never practiced fencing in his entire life. It is also notorious that Monsieur de La Tour d'Azyr is an exceptional swordsman. Is it truly a duel, monsieur, when only one of the combatants is armed? Because that is what it amounts to when comparing their respective skill levels."

"There has scarcely been a duel fought where the same trumpery argument might not be advanced."

"But not always with equal justice. And in one case, at least, it was advanced successfully."

"Successfully? When was that?"

"Ten years ago, in Dauphiny. I refer to the case of Monsieur de Gesvres, a gentleman of that province, who forced a duel upon Monsieur de la Roche Jeannine and killed him. Monsieur de Jeannine was a member of a powerful family, which worked hard to obtain justice. It presented precisely the arguments that now apply against Monsieur de La Tour d'Azyr. As you will remember, the judges ruled that the provocation had intentionally come from Monsieur de Gesvres. They found him guilty of premeditated murder, and he was hanged."

Monsieur de Lesdiguieres exploded yet again. "Death of my life!" he cried. "Do you have the effrontery to suggest that Monsieur de La Tour d'Azyr should be hanged? Do you?"

"But why not, monsieur? It is the law, and there is precedent for it, as I have shown you. And it can be established without difficulty that what I state is the truth."

"Do you ask me, why not? Do you have the temerity to ask me that?"

"I have, monsieur. Can you answer me? If you cannot, monsieur, I will understand that while it is possible for a powerful family like that of La Roche Jeannine to set the law in motion, the law must remain inactive for the obscure and uninfluential, no matter how brutally wronged by a great nobleman."

Monsieur de Lesdiguieres realized that in an argument, he would accomplish nothing against this impassive, resolute young man. The menace in his demeanor grew more fierce.

“I advise you to leave immediately and be grateful for the chance to go unharmed.”

“Am I to understand, then, monsieur, that there will be no investigation into this case? That nothing I say will change your mind?”

“You are to understand that if you are still here in two minutes, things will go very badly for you.” M. de Lesdiguieres then tinkled the silver hand-bell on his table.

“I have informed you, monsieur, that a so-called duel was fought, and a man was killed. It seems I must remind you, the administrator of the King's justice, that duels are against the law. It is your duty to hold an investigation. I come as the legal representative of M. de Vilmorin's grieving mother to demand the investigation that is her right.”

The door behind André-Louis opened softly. M. de Lesdiguieres, pale with anger, struggled to control himself.

“You're trying to force us, are you, you insolent rascal?” he growled. “You think the King's justice should be rushed headlong by any arrogant commoner? I'm amazed at my own patience with you. But I'm giving you a final warning, master lawyer: control that insolent tongue of yours, or you'll have bitter cause to regret how glib it is.” He waved a jeweled, contemptuous hand and spoke to the usher standing behind André. “To the door!” he said sharply.

André-Louis hesitated for a second. Then, with a shrug, he turned. This was indeed a windmill, and he was a poor knight with a sorrowful face. To attack it head-on would mean being shattered. Yet, on the threshold, he turned again.

“M. de Lesdiguieres,” he said, “may I share an interesting fact from natural history with you? The tiger is a great lord in the jungle. For centuries, it terrorized lesser beasts, including the wolf. The wolf, a hunter himself, grew tired of being hunted. He began associating with other wolves. Then, driven to form packs for self-protection, the wolves discovered the power of the pack. They started hunting the tiger, with disastrous results for him. You should study Buffon, M. de Lesdiguieres.”

“I think I've studied a buffoon this morning,” M. de Lesdiguieres replied with a punning sneer. He probably wouldn't have bothered to reply at all if he hadn't thought himself so witty. “I don't understand you,” he added.

“But you will, M. de Lesdiguieres. You will,” André-Louis said, and then he left.
