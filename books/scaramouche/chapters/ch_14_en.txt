CHAPTER 5. ENTER SCARAMOUCHE

Dressed in a tight-fitting, all-black suit from a bygone era, from his flat velvet cap to his rosetted shoes, Scaramouche looked at himself in the mirror. His face was whitened, a slight, upturned mustache was glued to his upper lip, a small sword hung at his side, and a guitar was slung behind him. He felt a sardonic mood coming on, which was exactly right for the part.

He reflected that his life, which had been quiet and contemplative until recently, had suddenly become incredibly busy. In just one week, he had been a lawyer, a mob orator, an outlaw, a property manager, and now, finally, a buffoon. Last Wednesday, he had been stirring an audience in Rennes to anger. This Wednesday, he was to make an audience in Guichen laugh. Back then, his job was to draw tears. Today, his business was to provoke laughter. There was a difference, yet also a parallel. Then, as now, he had been a comedian. The part he played back then was, when you thought about it, similar to the part he would play this evening. What had he been in Rennes, after all, but a kind of Scaramouche? A little skirmisher, an astute schemer, slyly sowing seeds of trouble. The only difference was that today he performed under the name that truly fit his character, while last week he had been disguised as a respectable young provincial attorney.

He bowed to his reflection in the mirror.

"Buffoon!" he said to his reflection. "At last, you've found yourself. At last, you've found your true calling. You should be a great success."

Hearing M. Binet call his new name, he went downstairs. He found the company assembled and waiting in the inn's entrance corridor.

Of course, he was an object of great interest to everyone in the company. M. Binet and mademoiselle examined him most critically. M. Binet's eyes were gravely searching, while mademoiselle's lip curled scornfully.

"You'll do," M. Binet said, commending his make-up. "At least you look the part."

"Unfortunately, men are not always what they look like," Climene said acidly.

"That's a truth that doesn't apply to me right now," André-Louis said. "Because it's the first time in my life that I look like what I truly am."

Mademoiselle curled her lip a little further and turned her shoulder to him. But the others thought him very witty, probably because he was new. Columbine encouraged him with a friendly smile that showed her large white teeth. M. Binet declared once again that he would be a great success, seeing how much spirit he put into the undertaking. Then, in a voice that for the moment seemed borrowed from the roaring captain, M. Binet gathered them for the short parade across to the market hall.

The new Scaramouche took his place beside Rhodomont. The old Scaramouche, hobbling on a crutch, had left an hour ago to become the doorkeeper, a position necessarily left vacant by André-Louis. So, the exchange between the two was complete.

Led by Polichinelle banging his great drum and Pierrot blowing his trumpet, they set out. They were watched by rows of ragamuffins, who had lined up to enjoy as much of the free spectacle as they could.

Ten minutes later, three knocks sounded. The curtains drew aside to reveal a battered set, part garden and part forest, where Climene feverishly searched for Leander's arrival. In the wings stood the beautiful, melancholy lover, awaiting his cue. Immediately behind him was the inexperienced Scaramouche, who was soon to follow.

André-Louis was hit with nausea in that terrifying moment. He tried to do a quick mental review of the first act of this play, which he himself had largely written, but his mind was a complete blank. Sweat breaking out on his skin, he stepped back to the wall. Above a dim lantern there, a sheet was pasted with a brief outline of the play. He was still studying it when his arm was clutched, and he was pulled violently towards the wings. He caught a glimpse of Pantaloon's grotesque face, its eyes blazing, and heard a raucous growl:

"Climene has spoken your cue three times already."

Before he realized it, he had been shoved onto the stage. He stood there foolishly, blinking in the glare of the footlights with their tin reflectors. He looked so utterly foolish and bewildered that waves of laughter welcomed him from the audience, which packed the hall from end to end that evening. Trembling a little, his bewilderment at first increasing, he stood there, receiving that continuous laughter at his absurdity. Climene eyed him with expectant mockery, savoring his upcoming humiliation. Leander looked at him in dismay, while behind the scenes, M. Binet was fuming with rage.

"Name of a name," he groaned to the rather scared members of the company assembled there. "What will happen when they discover that he isn't acting?"

But they never did discover it. Scaramouche's paralysis of bewilderment lasted only a few seconds. He realized he was being laughed at and remembered that his Scaramouche was a character to be laughed *with*, not *at*. He had to save the situation, twisting it to his own advantage as best he could. His real bewilderment and terror were now replaced by *acted* bewilderment and terror, far more pronounced but not quite as funny. He managed to make it clearly appear that his terror was of someone off the stage. He took cover behind a painted shrub. From there, as the laughter finally began to subside, he addressed Climene and Leander.

"Forgive me, beautiful lady, if my abrupt entrance startled you. The truth is, I've never been the same since that last incident with Almaviva. My heart isn't what it used to be. Down there at the end of the lane, I came face to face with an elderly gentleman carrying a heavy cudgel. The horrible thought entered my mind that it might be your father. And that our little plan to get you safely married might already have been betrayed to him. I think it was the cudgel that put that idea in my head. Not that I'm afraid. I'm not really afraid of anything. But I couldn't help thinking that if it really *had* been your father, and he had broken my head with his cudgel, your hopes would have died with me. For without me, what would you have done, my poor children?"

A ripple of laughter from the audience had been steadily encouraging him, helping him recover his natural impudence. It was clear they found him comical. They were to find him far more comical than he had ever intended, and this was largely due to a lucky coincidence he hadn't fully considered. The fear of recognition by someone from Gavrillac or Rennes had weighed heavily on him. His face was made up enough to prevent recognition, but his voice remained. To disguise this, he had used the fact that Figaro was a Spaniard. He had known a Spaniard at Louis le Grand who spoke fluent but most extraordinary French, with a comically excessive amount of sibilant sounds. It was an accent he had often imitated, as young people will imitate characteristics that make them laugh. Opportunely, he had remembered that Spanish student. Tonight, he modeled his own speech on that student's. The audience of Guichen found it as laughable on his lips as he and his friends had once found it on the lips of that mocked Spaniard.

Meanwhile, behind the scenes, Binet, who was listening to that smooth improvisation that wasn't in the script, had recovered from his fears.

"Dieu de Dieu!" he whispered, grinning. "Did he do it on purpose, then?"

It seemed impossible to him that a man who had been as terrified as he had imagined André-Louis to be could have recovered his wits so quickly and completely. Yet, the doubt remained.

The curtain fell on a first act that had gone with an energy unmatched in the company's history until this moment, carried almost entirely by the new Scaramouche. To resolve his doubt, M. Binet bluntly questioned him afterwards.

They were standing in the space that served as a green room, the company all assembled there, showering congratulations upon their new recruit. Scaramouche, a little elated by his success at that moment, however trivial he might consider it tomorrow, then took full revenge on Climene for the malicious satisfaction she had shown at his momentary blank terror.

"I don't wonder that you ask," he said. "Honestly, I should have warned you that I intended to do my best from the start to get the audience to like me. Mademoiselle very nearly ruined everything by refusing to show any of my terror. She wasn't even startled. Another time, mademoiselle, I shall give you full warning of my every intention."

She blushed beneath her greasepaint. But before she could find a sufficiently venomous answer, her father was scolding her severely for her stupidity. He did so even more severely because he himself had been deceived by Scaramouche's supreme acting.

Scaramouche's success in the first act was more than confirmed as the performance proceeded. Completely master of himself by now, and stimulated as only success can stimulate, he warmed to his work. Impudent, alert, sly, and graceful, he embodied the very ideal of Scaramouche. He enhanced his natural wit with many a remembered line from Beaumarchais, thus convincing the more knowledgeable audience members that this was indeed something of the real Figaro, and connecting them, in a way, with the great world of the capital.

When the curtain finally fell for the last time, Scaramouche and Climene shared the evening's honors. Their names were called together as the audience summoned them back before the curtains.

As they stepped back, and the curtains once again hid them from the departing audience, Monsieur Binet approached. He rubbed his fat hands softly together. This wandering young lawyer, whom chance had brought into his company, had clearly been sent by Fate to make Binet's fortune. The sudden success at Guichen, unmatched until now, should be repeated and even greater elsewhere. There would be no more sleeping under hedges or tightening belts. Hardship was behind him. He placed a hand on Scaramouche's shoulder and looked at him with a smile so oily that even his red paint and colossal false nose couldn't hide it.

“And what do you have to say to me now?” he asked. “Was I wrong when I promised you would succeed? Do you think I've spent a lifetime in the theater without recognizing a born actor when I see one? You are my discovery, Scaramouche. I've helped you discover yourself. I've set your feet on the road to fame and fortune. I'm waiting for your thanks.”

Scaramouche laughed at him, and his laugh wasn't entirely pleasant.

“Always Pantaloon!” he said.

Binet's large face clouded over. “I see you still haven't forgiven me for the little trick I used to make you realize your own talent. Ungrateful dog! As if my only goal wasn't to make you a star; and I've done it. Continue as you've started, and you'll end up in Paris. You might even tread the stage of the Comedie Francaise, a rival to Talma, Fleury, and Dugazon. When that happens, perhaps you'll feel the gratitude due to old Binet, because you'll owe it all to this soft-hearted old fool.”

“If you were as good an actor on stage as you are in private,” Scaramouche said, “you would have reached the Comedie Francaise long ago. But I hold no grudge, Monsieur Binet.” He laughed and held out his hand.

Binet seized his hand and wrung it heartily.

“That, at least, is something,” he declared. “My boy, I have great plans for you—for us. Tomorrow we go to Maure; there's a fair there until the end of this week. Then on Monday we'll try our luck at Pipriac, and after that, we'll have to think. I might be about to realize the dream of my life. We must have taken in over fifteen louis tonight. Where the devil is that rascal Cordemais?”

Cordemais was the name of the original Scaramouche, who had so unfortunately twisted his ankle. Binet referring to him by his real name was a sign that, within Binet's company at least, he had forever fallen from the lofty position of Scaramouche.

“Let's go and find him. Then we'll head to the inn and open a bottle of the best Burgundy, perhaps two bottles.”

But Cordemais wasn't easy to find. None of the company had seen him since the performance ended. Monsieur Binet went around to the entrance. Cordemais wasn't there. At first, Binet was annoyed. Then, as he kept shouting the man's name in vain, he started to grow uneasy. Finally, when Polichinelle, who was with them, found Cordemais' crutch discarded behind the door, Monsieur Binet became alarmed. A dreadful suspicion entered his mind. He grew visibly pale under his stage paint.

“But this evening he couldn't walk without the crutch!” he exclaimed. “How could he leave it there and just take off?”

“Perhaps he's gone on to the inn,” someone suggested.

“But he couldn't walk without his crutch,” Monsieur Binet insisted.

Nevertheless, since he clearly wasn't anywhere around the market-hall, they all trooped to the inn. They deafened the landlady with their questions.

“Oh, yes, Monsieur Cordemais came in some time ago.”

“Where is he now?”

“He went away again at once. He just came for his bag.”

“For his bag!” Binet was on the verge of an apoplectic fit. “How long ago was that?”

She glanced at the clock on the overmantel. “It would have been about half an hour ago. It was a few minutes before the Rennes diligence passed through.”

“The Rennes diligence!” Monsieur Binet was almost speechless. “Could he... could he walk?” he asked, his voice filled with terrible anxiety.

“Walk? He ran like a hare when he left the inn. I myself thought his agility was suspicious, considering how lame he'd been since he fell downstairs yesterday. Is anything wrong?”

Monsieur Binet collapsed into a chair. He buried his head in his hands and groaned.

“The scoundrel was faking it the whole time!” Climene exclaimed. “His fall downstairs was a trick. He was planning this. He has swindled us.”

“Fifteen louis at least—perhaps sixteen!” Monsieur Binet said. “Oh, the heartless blackguard! To swindle me, who have been like a father to him—and to swindle me at such a moment!”

From the silent, awe-stricken company, each member wondering how much of the loss would be deducted from their own meager pay, came a splutter of laughter.

Monsieur Binet glared, his eyes bloodshot.

“Who laughs?” he roared. “What heartless wretch has the audacity to laugh at my misfortune?”

André-Louis, still in the dark, magnificent costume of Scaramouche, stepped forward. He was still laughing.

"So it's you, is it? You might not be laughing for long, my friend, if I decide to get my money back my own way."

"Dullard!" Scaramouche scorned him. "You rabbit-brained elephant! So what if Cordemais ran off with fifteen louis? Didn't he leave you something worth twenty times that?"

Monsieur Binet just stared, completely confused.

He concluded, "I think you're drunk. You've been drinking."

"Yes, I have. I've been drinking at the fountain of Thalia. Oh, don't you see? Don't you see the treasure Cordemais left behind?"

"What has he left?"

"A unique idea for a play's plot. It's all unfolding before me. I'll borrow part of the title from Molière. We'll call it 'Les Fourberies de Scaramouche.' And if we don't have the audiences in Maure and Pipriac aching with laughter, I'll play the dullard Pantaloon from now on."

Polichinelle smacked his fist into his palm. "Superb!" he said fiercely. "To turn bad luck into good fortune, to turn loss into profit—that shows true genius."

Scaramouche made a leg. "Polichinelle, you are a man after my own heart. I love a man who can recognize my worth. If Pantaloon had half your wit, we'd be drinking Burgundy tonight, despite Cordemais running off."

"Burgundy?" roared Monsieur Binet. Before he could say anything more, Harlequin clapped his hands together.

"That's the spirit, Monsieur Binet! You heard him, landlady! He called for Burgundy!"

"I called for nothing of the kind."

"But you heard him, dear madame. We all heard him."

The others chimed in, while Scaramouche smiled at him and patted his shoulder.

"Come on, man, a little courage! Didn't you say fortune awaits us? And don't we now have the means to make fortune obey us? Burgundy, then, to... to toast 'Les Fourberies de Scaramouche!'"

And Monsieur Binet, who understood the power of the idea, gave in, took heart, and got drunk with the rest.
