CHAPTER 2. QUOS DEUS VULT PERDERE

Just as he had done when he joined the Binet troupe, André-Louis now fully committed himself to his new profession. Necessity had driven him to it, and it offered effective concealment from those who wished him harm. Though it didn't, this profession might have finally made him consider himself a man of action. However, he hadn't stopped being a man of thought. The events of spring and summer 1789 in Paris gave him plenty to reflect on. He witnessed firsthand what was perhaps the most astonishing chapter in human history. In the end, he had to conclude that all his earlier assumptions were wrong. It was passionate, idealistic enthusiasts like Vilmorin who had been right all along.

I suspect he actually took pride in being mistaken. He complacently attributed his error to the fact that his own mind was too sane and logical to grasp the depths of human madness now being revealed.

He observed the growing hunger, poverty, and distress in Paris that spring. He correctly identified its cause, along with the remarkable patience with which the people endured it. France was in a state of hushed, paralyzed anticipation. Everyone waited for the States-General to assemble and for centuries of tyranny to end. Because of this anticipation, industry had ground to a halt. The flow of trade had dwindled to a trickle. People wouldn't buy or sell until they clearly understood how the brilliant Swiss banker, Monsieur Necker, would rescue them from this quagmire. This paralysis of affairs meant ordinary people were thrown out of work. They were left to starve with their wives and children.

André-Louis watched, smiling grimly. So far, he was right. The suffering always fell on the proletariat. The men pushing for this revolution—the electors, both in Paris and elsewhere—were people of means. They were prominent bourgeois, wealthy traders. These men despised the *canaille* and envied the privileged. They spoke grandly of equality, by which they meant an upward equality that would elevate them to the gentry's level. Meanwhile, the proletariat perished from want in their squalid homes.

Finally, in May, the deputies arrived. André-Louis’ friend Le Chapelier was prominent among them. The States-General were inaugurated at Versailles. That's when things started to get interesting. That's when André-Louis began to seriously doubt the validity of the views he had held until then.

When the royal proclamation decreed that the deputies of the Third Estate should number twice as many as those of the other two orders combined, André-Louis had believed this would guarantee the Third Estate a majority of votes. He thought it would make the reforms they had promised inevitable.

But he hadn't accounted for the power of the privileged orders over the proud Austrian queen. Nor had he accounted for her power over the obese, sluggish, indecisive monarch. André-Louis could understand why the privileged orders would fight to defend their privileges. Human nature, cursed with acquisitiveness, means people will never willingly give up what they possess, whether they acquired it fairly or not. But what surprised André-Louis was the utterly crass methods the Privileged used to prepare for battle. They countered reason and philosophy with brute force. They met ideas with battalions of foreign mercenaries. As if ideas could be impaled on bayonets!

The war had begun: the Privileged and the Court on one side, the Assembly and the People on the other.

The Third Estate held its ground and waited. They waited with the patience of nature. They waited a month while business remained completely paralyzed, and the skeletal hand of famine tightened its grip on Paris. They waited another month while the Privileged gradually assembled an army in Versailles to intimidate them. This army consisted of fifteen regiments, nine of which were Swiss and German. They also set up an artillery park in front of the building where the deputies met. But the deputies refused to be intimidated. They refused to acknowledge the guns and foreign uniforms. They refused to see anything but the purpose for which the royal proclamation had brought them together.

This continued until June 10th, when the great thinker and metaphysician, Abbé Sieyes, gave the signal. He said, "It is time to cut the cable."

The opportunity came soon, at the very beginning of July. Monsieur du Chatelet, a harsh, haughty disciplinarian, proposed transferring eleven French Guards, who were under arrest, from the military jail of the Abbaye to the filthy Bicêtre prison. Bicêtre was reserved for the lowest class of thieves and felons. When word of this intention spread, the people finally met violence with violence. A mob of four thousand broke into the Abbaye. They freed not only the eleven guardsmen but all the other prisoners as well. The only exception was one man they discovered to be a thief, whom they promptly put back.

That was open revolt at last, and Privilege knew exactly how to handle a revolt. They would strangle this rebellious Paris in the iron grip of foreign regiments. Measures were quickly coordinated. Old Maréchal de Broglie, a veteran of the Seven Years’ War, took control. He had a soldier's contempt for civilians and believed the sight of a uniform would be enough to restore peace and order. Besenval served as his second-in-command. The foreign regiments were stationed around Paris. Their very names irritated Parisians: regiments of Reisbach, Diesbach, Nassau, Esterhazy, and Roehmer. Swiss reinforcements were sent to the Bastille. Since June 30th, the menacing mouths of loaded cannons had already been visible between its battlements.

On July 10th, the electors once again appealed to the King, requesting the withdrawal of the troops. The next day, they were told the troops were there to defend the Assembly's liberties! The day after that, a Sunday, Dr. Guillotin arrived. He was a philanthropist and a member of the Assembly. His philanthropic machine for painless death would soon find plenty of work. He came to assure the electors of Paris that, despite appearances, all was well. He claimed Necker was more firmly in power than ever. He didn't know that at the very moment he spoke so confidently, Monsieur Necker, often dismissed and often recalled, had just been dismissed yet again by the hostile cabal surrounding the Queen. Privilege wanted decisive measures, and decisive measures it would get—decisive for its own benefit.

At the same time, another philanthropist, also a doctor, was writing. This was Jean-Paul Mara, of Italian descent, better known as Marat—the French version of his name he adopted. He was also a man of letters who had spent years in England and published several works on sociology there. He wrote, "Be careful! Consider the fatal effect of a rebellious movement. If you have the misfortune to give in to it, you will be treated as people in revolt, and blood will flow."

That Sunday morning, André-Louis was in the gardens of the Palais Royal. It was a universal meeting place, full of shops, puppet shows, circuses, cafes, gaming houses, and brothels. News of Necker’s dismissal spread there, bringing dismay and fury. The people interpreted Necker’s dismissal as a triumph for the party hostile to them. It sounded the death knell for any hope of righting their wrongs.

He saw a slight young man with a pock-marked face, whose magnificent eyes saved him from being utterly ugly. The man leaped onto a table outside the Café de Foy, a drawn sword in his hand, shouting, "To arms!" His cry imposed a silence of astonishment. Then, the young man unleashed a torrent of inflammatory eloquence. His voice was marred at times by a stutter. He told the people that the Germans on the Champ de Mars would enter Paris that night to butcher its inhabitants. He cried, "Let us wear a cockade!" He tore a leaf from a tree to serve his purpose—the green cockade of hope.

Enthusiasm swept through the crowd. It was a motley mix of men and women from every class, from vagabond to nobleman, from harlot to lady of fashion. Trees were stripped of their leaves, and the green cockade was proudly displayed on almost every head.

The incendiary's stuttering voice raved on. "You are caught between two fires! Between the Germans on the Champ de Mars and the Swiss in the Bastille. To arms, then! To arms!"

Excitement boiled over. From a neighboring waxworks show came a bust of Necker. Soon after, a bust of that comedian, the Duke of Orleans, appeared. The Duke had his own political faction and, like other budding opportunists of the day, was ready to exploit the moment for his own gain. The bust of Necker was draped with crepe.

André-Louis watched, and he grew afraid. Marat’s pamphlet had impressed him. It had expressed the very same sentiment he had voiced more than half a year ago to the mob at Rennes. This crowd, he felt, had to be restrained. That hot-headed, irresponsible stutterer would have the town ablaze by night unless someone did something. The young man, a lawyer from the Palais named Camille Desmoulins, later to become famous, leaped down from his table. He was still waving his sword, still shouting, "To arms! Follow me!" André-Louis advanced to occupy the improvised platform the stutterer had just vacated. He intended to try and counteract that inflammatory performance. He pushed through the crowd and suddenly came face to face with a tall, beautifully dressed man. His handsome face was sternly set, and his large, dark eyes smoldered as if with suppressed anger.

They stood face to face for a long moment, each looking into the other's eyes. The jostling crowd streamed past them, unnoticed. Then André-Louis laughed.

He said, "That fellow also has a very dangerous gift of eloquence, Monsieur le Marquis. In fact, there are many such people in France today. They grow from the soil, which you and your kind have irrigated with the blood of liberty's martyrs. Soon, it may be your blood instead. The soil is parched and thirsty for it."

The Marquis replied, "Gallows-bird! The police will take care of you. I'll tell the Lieutenant-General that you can be found in Paris."

André-Louis cried, "My God, man, will you never learn? Will you talk about Lieutenant-Generals like that when Paris itself is likely to collapse around your ears or catch fire under your feet? Raise your voice, Monsieur le Marquis. Denounce me here, to these people. You'll make a hero of me in an hour like this. Or shall I denounce you? I think I will. I think it's high time you received your just deserts. Hey! You others, listen to me! Let me present you to..."

A rush of men hurtled against him, sweeping him along despite his efforts. This separated him from Monsieur de La Tour d’Azyr, whom he had met so strangely. He tried to push against the human torrent. The Marquis, caught in an eddy, remained where he was. André-Louis’ last glimpse of him was of a man smiling with tight, ugly lips.

Meanwhile, the gardens were emptying in the wake of that stuttering firebrand who had started the green cockade trend. The human torrent poured out into the Rue de Richelieu. André-Louis was forced to let himself be carried along by it, at least as far as the Rue du Hasard. There, he managed to slip out of the crowd. He had no wish to be crushed to death or to take further part in the unfolding madness. He slipped down the street and made his way home to the deserted academy. There were no pupils that day. Even Monsieur des Amis, like André-Louis, had gone out to find news of what was happening at Versailles.

This was not a normal state of affairs at Bertrand des Amis's Academy. Whatever else in Paris might have been at a standstill lately, the fencing academy had flourished like never before. Usually, both the master and his assistant were busy from morning until dusk. André-Louis was already being paid for the lessons he gave. The master allowed him half of the fee for each lesson, an arrangement André-Louis found profitable. On Sundays, the academy usually had a half-holiday. But on this particular Sunday, the city was in such a state of suspense and ferment that no one had appeared by eleven o'clock. So, both des Amis and André-Louis had gone out. They were very good friends by now. As they casually said goodbye, they had no idea they would never meet again in this world.

Bloodshed erupted in Paris that day. On the Place Vendome, a detachment of dragoons waited for the crowd André-Louis had slipped away from. The horsemen charged into the mob, scattering it. They smashed the waxen effigy of M. Necker and killed one man on the spot. He was an unfortunate French Guard who had stood his ground.

That was a beginning. As a consequence, Besenval brought his Swiss troops from the Champ de Mars. He arranged them in battle formation on the Champs Elysees, complete with four pieces of artillery. He stationed his dragoons in the Place Louis XV. That evening, an enormous crowd streamed along the Champs Elysees and the Tuileries Gardens. They watched the military preparations with alarm. Some people hurled insults at the foreign mercenaries, and others threw stones. Besenval, either losing his head or acting under orders, sent for his dragoons. He ordered them to disperse the crowd. But the crowd was too dense to be dispersed that way. It was so dense that the horsemen couldn't move without crushing someone.

Several people were crushed. As a result, when the dragoons, led by the Prince de Lambesc, advanced into the Tuileries Gardens, the outraged crowd met them with a fusillade of stones and bottles. Lambesc gave the order to fire. There was a stampede. The indignant people poured out of the Tuileries and through the city. They spread their story of German cavalry trampling women and children. Now, in grim earnest, they echoed the call to arms that Desmoulins had raised at noon in the Palais Royal.

The victims were picked up and carried away. Among them was Bertrand des Amis. Like all who lived by the sword, he was an ardent supporter of the noblesse. Yet, he had been trampled to death under the hooves of foreign horsemen, launched by the noblesse and led by a nobleman.

That evening, André-Louis was waiting on the second floor of No. 13 Rue du Hasard for his friend and master to return. Four men of the people brought him a broken body. It was one of the Revolution's earliest victims, for the uprising had now truly begun.
