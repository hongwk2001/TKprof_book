Book 1: The Robe
Chapter 1: The Republican

He was born with a gift of laughter and a sense that the world was mad. That was all his inheritance. His parentage was a mystery, though the village of Gavrillac had long since cleared up any confusion. The simple folk of Brittany weren't so naive as to be fooled by a supposed relationship that wasn't even original. Sometimes a nobleman, for no clear reason, declares himself the godfather of a baby whose origins no one knows. If he then takes care of the boy's upbringing and education, even the most unsophisticated country people understand what's really going on. So the good people of Gavrillac had no illusions about the true relationship between the boy, who had been named André-Louis Moreau, and Quintin de Kercadiou, Lord of Gavrillac. M. de Kercadiou lived in the big grey house that stood high above the village clustered below.

André-Louis had learned to read and write at the village school. During that time, he lived with old Rabouillet, the attorney, who managed M. de Kercadiou's financial affairs as his fiscal intendant. Later, at fifteen, he was sent to Paris, to the Lycée of Louis Le Grand, to study law. He had now returned to practice law with Rabouillet. All of this was paid for by his godfather, M. de Kercadiou. By placing André-Louis back under Rabouillet's guidance, it seemed quite clear he was securing the young man's future.

André-Louis, for his part, had made the most of his opportunities. At twenty-four, he was brimming with enough knowledge to give an ordinary person intellectual indigestion. His enthusiastic study of humanity, from Thucydides to the Encyclopaedists, from Seneca to Rousseau, had solidified his earliest impressions: that his own species was generally insane. This became an unshakeable conviction. And I can't find anything in his eventful life that ever made him change that opinion.

Physically, he was a slender, wiry man, just above average height. He had a lean, shrewd face with a prominent nose and cheekbones, and long, lank black hair that almost reached his shoulders. His mouth was long, thin-lipped, and humorous. He was saved from being ugly only by his magnificent, luminous eyes. They were so dark they were almost black, and always seemed to be searching. His writings—unfortunately too few—especially his Confessions, provide ample evidence of his whimsical mind and his rare gift for graceful expression. He wasn't fully aware of his talent for public speaking yet, though he had already gained some fame for it in the Literary Chamber of Rennes. These clubs were now common across France, where the intellectual youth gathered to study and discuss the new philosophies spreading through society. But the fame he'd gained there was hardly enviable. He was too impish, too caustic, and—his colleagues thought—too inclined to ridicule their grand theories for the regeneration of mankind. He himself protested that he merely held their ideas up to the mirror of truth, and it wasn't his fault if they looked ridiculous when reflected there.

All this did was exasperate people. He would have been expelled from a society that had grown distrustful of him, if not for his friend, Philippe de Vilmorin. Philippe, a divinity student from Rennes, was one of the Literary Chamber's most popular members.

Philippe arrived in Gavrillac on a November morning, bringing news of the political storms gathering over France. In that sleepy Breton village, he found something that quickly fueled his already strong indignation. A peasant from Gavrillac, named Mabey, had been shot dead that morning in the woods of Meupont, across the river. He was killed by a gamekeeper belonging to the Marquis de La Tour d’Azyr. The poor man had been caught taking a pheasant from a snare. The gamekeeper had acted under explicit orders from his master.

Infuriated by such an absolute and merciless act of tyranny, M. de Vilmorin decided to present the matter to M. de Kercadiou. Mabey was a vassal of Gavrillac. Vilmorin hoped to persuade the Lord of Gavrillac to demand at least some compensation for the widow and the three orphans left by that brutal deed.

But André-Louis was Philippe’s dearest friend—almost a brother, in fact. So the young seminarian sought him out first. He found André-Louis eating breakfast alone in Rabouillet’s long, low-ceilinged, white-paneled dining room—the only home André-Louis had ever known. After embracing him, Philippe deafened him with his denunciation of M. de La Tour d’Azyr.

André-Louis said, "I have heard of it already."

His friend reproached him, "You speak as if this hasn't surprised you."

André-Louis replied, "Nothing beastly can surprise me when a beast does it. And La Tour d’Azyr is a beast, as everyone knows. Mabey was a fool for stealing his pheasants. He should have stolen somebody else’s."

Philippe asked, "Is that all you have to say about it?"

André-Louis said, "What more is there to say? I hope I have a practical mind."

Philippe declared, "What more there is to say, I intend to say to your godfather, M. de Kercadiou. I shall appeal to him for justice."

André-Louis raised his eyebrows. "Against M. de La Tour d’Azyr?"

Philippe asked, "Why not?"

André-Louis said, "My dear, naive Philippe, dog doesn’t eat dog."

Philippe insisted, "You are unjust to your godfather. He is a humane man."

André-Louis replied, "Oh, as humane as you please. But this isn’t a question of humanity. It’s a question of game laws."

M. de Vilmorin threw his long arms up to heaven in disgust. He was a tall, slender young gentleman, a year or two younger than André-Louis. He was dressed very soberly in black, as befitted a seminarian, with white bands at his wrists and throat and silver buckles on his shoes. His neatly tied brown hair was free of powder.

He exploded, "You talk like a lawyer!"

André-Louis said, "Naturally. But don’t waste your anger on me for that. Tell me what you want me to do."

Philippe said, "I want you to come with me to M. de Kercadiou and use your influence to get justice. I suppose I’m asking too much."

André-Louis replied, "My dear Philippe, I exist to serve you. I warn you that it’s a futile quest. But give me a moment to finish my breakfast, and I am at your orders."

M. de Vilmorin dropped into a winged armchair by the well-swept hearth, where a cheerful fire of pine logs was burning brightly. While he waited, he gave his friend the latest news from Rennes. Young, ardent, enthusiastic, and inspired by utopian ideals, he passionately denounced the rebellious attitude of the privileged classes.

André-Louis was already fully aware of the prevailing sentiment among the nobility, whose discussions he participated in as a nobleman's representative. So he wasn't at all surprised by what he heard. M. de Vilmorin found it exasperating that his friend seemed unwilling to share his indignation.

He cried, "Don’t you see what this means? By disobeying the King, the nobles are striking at the very foundations of the throne. Don’t they realize their very existence depends on it? If the throne falls, they are the ones standing closest to it who will be crushed! Don’t they see that?"

André-Louis replied, "Evidently not. They are simply governing classes, and I’ve never heard of governing classes that cared about anything but their own profit."

"That's our complaint. That's what we're going to change."

"You're going to abolish governing classes? That's an interesting experiment. I believe it was the original plan for creation, and it might have succeeded if not for Cain."

M. de Vilmorin curbed his exasperation. He said, "What we're going to do is transfer the government to other hands."

"And you think that will make a difference?"

"I know it will."

"Ah! I assume that since you're now in minor orders, you already have the Almighty's confidence. He must have confided in you His intention to change the pattern of mankind."

M. de Vilmorin's fine, ascetic face grew overcast. He reproved his friend. "Andre, you are profane."

"I assure you, I'm quite serious. To do what you imply would require nothing short of divine intervention. You must change man, not systems. Can you and our boastful friends from the Literary Chamber of Rennes, or any other learned society in France, devise a system of government that has never been tried? Surely not. And can they say of any system that *has* been tried that it proved to be anything other than a failure in the end? My dear Philippe, the future can only be read with certainty in the past. *Ab actu ad posse valet consecutio.* Man never changes. He is always greedy, always acquisitive, always vile. I'm speaking of humanity as a whole."

M. de Vilmorin challenged him. "Do you pretend it's impossible to improve the lives of the people?"

"When you say 'the people,' you mean, of course, the populace. Will you abolish it? That's the only way to improve its lot, because as long as it remains the populace, its lot will be damnation."

M. de Vilmorin spoke with a mix of sorrow and indignation. "You argue, of course, for the side that employs you. That's natural, I suppose."

"On the contrary, I try to argue with absolute detachment. Let's test these ideas of yours. What form of government do you aspire to? A republic, I gather from what you've said. Well, you already have it. France is, in reality, a republic today."

Philippe stared at him. "You're being paradoxical, I think. What about the King?"

"The King? Everyone knows there's been no king in France since Louis XIV. There's an obese gentleman at Versailles who wears the crown, but the very news you bring shows how little he really counts. It's the nobles and clergy who sit in high places, with the people of France harnessed under their feet, who are the real rulers. That's why I say France is a republic. She's a republic built on the best pattern—the Roman pattern. Then, as now, there were great patrician families living in luxury, preserving power and wealth for themselves, and everything else considered worth possessing. And there was the populace, crushed and groaning, sweating, bleeding, starving, and perishing in the Roman kennels. That was a republic; the mightiest we've ever seen."

Philippe struggled with his impatience. "At least you'll admit—you have, in fact, admitted it—that we couldn't be worse governed than we are?"

"That's not the point. The point is, would we be better governed if we replaced the present ruling class with another? Without some guarantee of that, I'd be the last to lift a finger to make a change. And what guarantees can you give? What class aims at government? I'll tell you: the bourgeoisie."

"What?"

"That startles you, doesn't it? Truth is often so disconcerting. You hadn't thought of it? Well, think of it now. Look closely at this Nantes manifesto. Who are its authors?"

"I can tell you who compelled the municipality of Nantes to send it to the King. About ten thousand workmen—shipwrights, weavers, laborers, and artisans of every kind."

André-Louis replied, "They were stimulated and driven by their employers, the wealthy traders and shipowners of that city. I have a habit of observing things up close, which is why our colleagues in the Literary Chamber dislike me so heartily in debate. Where I delve, they merely skim. Behind those laborers and artisans of Nantes, advising them, urging on these poor, stupid, ignorant toilers to shed their blood in pursuit of the will-o'-the-wisp of freedom, are the sail-makers, the spinners, the ship-owners, and the slave-traders. The slave-traders! The men who live and grow rich by trafficking in human flesh and blood in the colonies are conducting a campaign at home in the sacred name of liberty! Don't you see that the whole movement is one of hucksters and traders and peddling vassals, swollen by wealth into envy of the power that comes from birth alone? The money-changers in Paris, who hold the bonds of the national debt, see the State's perilous financial condition. They tremble at the thought that a single man might have the power to cancel the debt through bankruptcy. To secure themselves, they are burrowing underground to overthrow a state and build a new one on its ruins, in which they shall be the masters. And to accomplish this, they inflame the people. Already in Dauphiny, we've seen blood run like water—always the blood of the populace. Now in Brittany, we may see the same. And if, in the end, the new ideas prevail? If the seigneurial rule is overthrown, what then? You will have exchanged an aristocracy for a plutocracy. Is that worthwhile? Do you think that under money-changers and slave-traders and men who have grown rich in other ways through the ignoble arts of buying and selling, the people's lot will be any better than under their priests and nobles? Has it ever occurred to you, Philippe, what makes the rule of the nobles so intolerable? Acquisitiveness. Acquisitiveness is the curse of mankind. And should you expect less acquisitiveness in men who have built themselves up through acquisitiveness? Oh, I'm ready to admit that the present government is execrable, unjust, tyrannical—whatever you want to call it. But I beg you to look ahead and see that the government they aim to exchange it for may be infinitely worse."

Philippe sat thoughtful for a moment. Then he returned to the attack.

"You don't speak of the abuses, the horrible, intolerable abuses of power under which we labor at present."

"Where there's power, there will always be its abuse."

"Not if holding power depends on its fair administration."

"Holding power *is* power. We can't dictate to those who hold it."

"The people can—the people in their might."

"Again I ask you, when you say 'the people,' do you mean the populace? You do. What power can the populace wield? It can run wild. It can burn and slay for a time. But it cannot wield enduring power, because power demands qualities the populace doesn't possess, or it wouldn't *be* the populace. The inevitable, tragic consequence of civilization is populace. For the rest, abuses can be corrected by fairness; and fairness, if it's not found in the enlightened, isn't to be found at all. Monsieur Necker is going to correct abuses and limit privileges. That's decided. To that end, the States General are to assemble."

"And we've made a promising beginning in Brittany, as Heaven hears me!" cried Philippe.

"Pooh! That's nothing. Naturally, the nobles won't yield without a struggle. It's a futile and ridiculous struggle—but then... I suppose it's human nature to be futile and ridiculous."

M. de Vilmorin became scathingly sarcastic. "You'll probably also describe the shooting of Mabey as futile and ridiculous. I'd even be prepared to hear you argue in defense of the Marquis de La Tour d'Azyr that his gamekeeper was merciful in shooting Mabey, since the alternative would have been a life sentence to the galleys."

André-Louis finished his chocolate, set down his cup, and pushed back his chair. His breakfast was done.

"I confess I don't have your great compassion, my dear Philippe. I'm touched by Mabey's fate. But once I get past the initial shock of this news, I can't forget that Mabey was, after all, stealing when he died."

Monsieur de Vilmorin stood up, bristling with indignation.

"That's the viewpoint I'd expect from someone who serves as a nobleman's assistant fiscal intendant and his delegate to the States of Brittany."

"Philippe, is that fair? You're angry with me!" André-Louis cried, genuinely concerned.

"I'm hurt," Vilmorin admitted. "Your attitude deeply hurts me. And I'm not the only one who resents your reactionary views. Do you know the Literary Chamber is seriously considering expelling you?"

André-Louis shrugged. "That neither surprises nor troubles me."

Monsieur de Vilmorin continued passionately, "Sometimes I think you have no heart. For you, it's always the law, never what's fair. It occurs to me, André, that I made a mistake coming to you. You're not likely to help me in my interview with Monsieur de Kercadiou." He picked up his hat, clearly intending to leave.

André-Louis sprang up and caught his arm.

He vowed, "This is the last time I'll ever agree to talk law or politics with you, Philippe. I love you too much to quarrel over other people's business."

"But I make them my own," Philippe insisted passionately.

"Of course you do, and I love you for it. It's right that you should. You're going to be a priest, and everyone's business is a priest's business. I, on the other hand, am a lawyer—a nobleman's fiscal intendant, as you say. A lawyer's business is his client's business. That's the difference between us. Still, you're not going to get rid of me so easily."

"But I'll tell you frankly, now that I think about it, I'd prefer you didn't see Monsieur de Kercadiou with me. Your duty to your client can't possibly help me."

His anger had passed, but his determination remained firm, based on the reason he'd given.

"Very well," André-Louis said. "It will be as you please. But nothing will stop me from at least walking with you as far as the chateau and waiting for you while you make your appeal to Monsieur de Kercadiou."

And so they left the house as good friends, for Monsieur de Vilmorin's sweet nature didn't allow for bitterness. Together, they walked up the steep main street of Gavrillac.
