CHAPTER 4. THE HERITAGE

Monsieur de Vilmorin wanted the matter settled immediately. He felt both objective and subjective about it. He was struggling with emotions that conflicted with his priestly calling. Above all, he was in a hurry to finish so he could return to a more suitable state of mind. He also feared himself a little. By that, I mean his sense of honor was afraid of his true nature. His education and the goal he had pursued for years had stripped him of much of the spirited brutality that is a man's birthright. He had become as timid and gentle as a woman. Knowing this, he feared that once his initial passion faded, he might show a shameful weakness during the ordeal.

The Marquis, for his part, was equally eager for an immediate settlement. They had Monsieur de Chabrillane to represent his cousin, and André-Louis to serve as a witness for Monsieur de Vilmorin. There was nothing to delay them.

So, within a few minutes, all arrangements were complete. You can imagine that small, sinister group of four gathered in the afternoon sunshine on the bowling-green behind the inn. They were completely private, mostly hidden from the house windows by a cluster of trees. Even though the trees were leafless, they were dense enough to form an effective screen.

There were no formalities about measuring blades or choosing the ground. The Marquis removed his sword-belt and scabbard. However, he refused to take off his shoes or coat, not considering it worthwhile for such a negligible opponent. Tall, agile, and athletic, he stood to face Monsieur de Vilmorin, who was also tall but very delicate and frail. Monsieur de Vilmorin likewise scorned the usual preparations. He knew that stripping down would gain him nothing. So, he took his guard fully dressed, with two feverish red spots burning above his cheekbones on his otherwise pale face.

Monsieur de Chabrillane leaned on a cane, having given his sword to Monsieur de Vilmorin. He watched with quiet interest. Facing him, on the other side of the combatants, stood André-Louis. He was the palest of the four, staring with fevered eyes, twisting and untwisting his clammy hands.

Every instinct told him to throw himself between the two men, to protest and stop this duel. However, the realization of its futility curbed that sensible impulse. To calm himself, he clung to the belief that the outcome couldn't be very serious. Philippe's honor forced him to cross swords with the man he had struck. But the Marquis de La Tour d'Azyr's noble birth equally compelled him not to seriously harm the inexperienced young man he had so severely provoked. The Marquis, after all, was a man of honor. He could only intend to teach a lesson, perhaps a harsh one, but one his opponent would live to learn from. André-Louis stubbornly held onto that thought for comfort.

Steel clashed on steel, and the men began to fight. The Marquis presented the narrow edge of his upright body to his opponent, his knees slightly bent and ready to spring. Meanwhile, Monsieur de Vilmorin stood squarely, a full target, his knees stiff. Both honor and the spirit of fair play cried out against such an uneven match.

The encounter was very short, of course. In his youth, Philippe had received the sword-fighting lessons given to every boy of his social standing. So, he knew at least the basics of what was now expected of him. But what good were basics here? Three disengages completed the exchanges. Then, without any haste, the Marquis slid his right foot along the moist turf. His long, graceful body extended into a lunge that went under Monsieur de Vilmorin's clumsy guard. With the utmost deliberation, he drove his blade through the young man's vital organs.

André-Louis sprang forward just in time to catch his friend's body under the armpits as it collapsed. His own legs buckled under the weight. He went down with his burden until he was kneeling on the damp turf. Philippe's limp head rested against André-Louis' left shoulder. Philippe's relaxed arms hung at his sides. Blood welled and bubbled from the ghastly wound, soaking the poor young man's clothes.

With a pale face and twitching lips, André-Louis looked up at the Marquis de La Tour d'Azyr. The Marquis stood surveying his work with a serious but merciless expression.

"You have killed him!" André-Louis cried.

"Of course."

The Marquis ran a lace handkerchief along his blade to wipe it clean. As he let the delicate fabric fall, he explained himself. "He had, as I told him, a dangerously powerful gift of eloquence."

He turned away, leaving André-Louis with a complete understanding. Still supporting the limp, bleeding body, the young man called out to him.

"Come back, you cowardly murderer! Make yourself completely safe by killing me too!"

The Marquis half-turned, his face dark with anger. Then Monsieur de Chabrillane placed a restraining hand on his arm. The Chevalier had been involved in the entire affair, but he was a little appalled now that it was done. He didn't have the strong resolve of the Marquis de La Tour d'Azyr, and he was much younger.

"Come away," he said. "The lad is raving. They were friends."

"Did you hear what he said?" the Marquis asked.

"Neither he, nor you, nor any man can deny it!" André-Louis shot back. "You yourself, monsieur, confessed it when you just gave me the reason why you killed him. You did it because you feared him."

"If that were true, then what?" the great gentleman asked.

"Do you even ask? Do you understand nothing of life and humanity except how to wear a coat and style your hair? Oh, yes, and how to wield weapons against boys and priests? Do you have no mind to think, no soul to guide your vision? Must I tell you that it's a coward's act to kill what he fears? And it's doubly a coward's act to kill in this manner? If you had stabbed him in the back with a knife, you would have shown the courage of your wickedness. It would have been undisguised wickedness. But you feared the consequences of that, powerful as you are. So, you hide your cowardice under the pretense of a duel."

The Marquis shook off his cousin's hand and took a step forward, now holding his sword like a whip. But again, the Chevalier caught and held him.

"No, no, Gervais! Stop, for God's sake!"

"Let him come, monsieur!" André-Louis raved, his voice thick and intense. "Let him finish his coward's work on me and make himself safe from a coward's just deserts."

Monsieur de Chabrillane let his cousin go. The Marquis's lips were white, his eyes glaring at the young man who had insulted him so recklessly. Then he stopped himself. Perhaps he suddenly remembered the popular belief about this young man's relationship with the Seigneur de Gavrillac, and the well-known affection the Seigneur held for him. So, he might have realized that if he pursued this matter further, he could find himself in a difficult situation. He would face two choices: shed more blood and thus get entangled with the Lord of Gavrillac, whose friendship was extremely important to him at that time, or withdraw with such a blow to his dignity that it would damage his authority in the countryside from then on.

Whether that was the reason or not, he stopped short. Then, with an angry and contemptuous exclamation, he threw up his arms, turned on his heel, and quickly strode off with his cousin.

When the landlord and his people arrived, they found André-Louis. His arms were around the body of his dead friend, and he was murmuring passionately into the deaf ear that rested almost against his lips:

"Philippe! Speak to me, Philippe! Philippe... Don't you hear me? Oh, God in Heaven! Philippe!"

They saw at a glance that neither a priest nor a doctor could help him now. The cheek resting against André-Louis's was ashen gray. Philippe's half-open eyes were glazed, and a little froth of blood appeared on his parted lips.

Half-blinded by tears, André-Louis stumbled after them as they carried the body into the inn. Upstairs, in the small room where they took him, André-Louis knelt by the bed. He held the dead man's hand in both of his own. In his helpless rage, he swore that Monsieur de La Tour d'Azyr would pay dearly for this.

He said, "It was your eloquence he feared, Philippe. So, if I can't get justice for this deed, at least it won't benefit him. The thing he feared in you, he shall fear in me. He feared your eloquence might sway men to overthrow people like him. Men shall be swayed by it still. Your eloquence and your arguments will be my legacy from you. I will make them my own. It doesn't matter that I don't believe in your gospel of freedom. I know it—every word of it. That's all that matters for our purpose, yours and mine. If all else fails, your thoughts will find expression through my living voice. That way, at least, we will have frustrated his vile aim to silence the voice he feared. It will do him no good to have your blood on his soul. That voice in you would never have hounded him and his half as relentlessly as it will in me—if all else fails."

It was an exhilarating thought. It calmed him and soothed his grief. He began to pray very softly. Then his heart trembled. He considered that Philippe, a man of peace, almost a priest, an apostle of Christianity, had gone to his Maker with the sin of anger on his soul. It was horrible. Yet God would surely see the righteousness of that anger. And in no case, no matter how humanity interpreted Divinity, could that one sin outweigh the loving good Philippe had always practiced, or the noble purity of his great heart. God, André-Louis reflected, was not a grand-seigneur after all.
