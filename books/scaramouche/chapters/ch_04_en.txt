[Book 1 - Chapter 4: THE HERITAGE]

Chapter 4. The Heritage

M. de Vilmorin wanted to settle the matter immediately. He was driven by both practical and personal reasons. He was struggling with emotions that conflicted deeply with his priestly calling, and he was eager to get it over with so he could return to a more appropriate state of mind. He was also a little afraid of himself. By this, I mean his sense of honor feared his own natural instincts. His education and the goals he had pursued for years had stripped away much of the aggressive toughness usually expected of men. He had grown as timid and gentle as a woman. Because he knew this, he was afraid that once his initial flash of anger faded, he might show a shameful weakness during the duel.

The Marquis, on his side, was equally eager for an immediate fight. Since they had M. de Chabrillane to act as a second for his cousin, and André-Louis to serve as a witness for M. de Vilmorin, there was no reason to delay.

Within a few minutes, all the arrangements were made. The sinister little group of four gathered in the afternoon sunshine on the bowling green behind the inn. They were completely isolated, hidden from the windows of the house by a thick screen of trees. Even without leaves, the branches were dense enough to provide effective cover.

There were no formal measurements of swords or careful selection of the ground. The Marquis took off his sword belt and scabbard. However, he refused to take off his shoes or coat, because he did not consider his opponent significant enough to justify the effort. He was tall, athletic, and flexible, and he stood facing the equally tall, but very delicate and fragile M. de Vilmorin. M. de Vilmorin also refused to make any of the usual preparations. He knew that taking off his coat would not help him, so he stepped into his fighting stance fully dressed. Two bright red spots burned on his cheekbones against his otherwise pale gray face.

M. de Chabrillane leaned on a cane because he had given his sword to M. de Vilmorin. He watched the scene with quiet interest. André-Louis stood facing him on the other side of the fighters. He was the palest of the four. He stared with feverish eyes and constantly twisted his sweaty hands together.

Every instinct in his body screamed at him to throw himself between the fighters and stop the duel. However, he held back that sensible urge because he knew it would be useless. To calm himself, he clung to the belief that the outcome would not be truly serious. Philippe’s honor forced him to fight the man who had struck him. At the same time, the Marquis's noble birth surely prevented him from seriously hurting the inexperienced young man he had provoked. After all, the Marquis was a man of honor. He probably just wanted to teach Philippe a lesson. It might be a harsh lesson, but Philippe would certainly live to learn from it. André-Louis clung stubbornly to that comforting thought.

Steel clashed against steel, and the men engaged in combat. The Marquis turned his body sideways to present a narrow target. His knees were slightly bent, acting like living springs. In contrast, M. de Vilmorin stood squarely facing his opponent, offering a full target with his knees locked straight. Both honor and the spirit of fair play protested against such an unequal match.

The fight was very short, as expected. During his youth, Philippe had received the basic fencing lessons given to every boy of his social class. He knew the basic movements expected of him. However, those simple skills were useless against the Marquis. They exchanged three quick sword movements. Then, without any rushing, the Marquis slid his right foot forward along the wet grass. His tall, graceful body extended in a long lunge that slipped easily under M. de Vilmorin’s clumsy defense. With absolute calm and deliberate focus, the Marquis drove his blade straight into the young man's stomach.

André-Louis sprang forward just in time to catch his friend under the arms as he collapsed. The weight of his friend made his own legs buckle, and he sank down until he was kneeling on the wet grass. Philippe’s limp head rested against his left shoulder. Philippe’s relaxed arms hung uselessly by his sides. Blood welled up and bubbled from the terrible wound, quickly soaking the poor young man's clothes.

With a pale face and trembling lips, André-Louis looked up at the Marquis. The nobleman stood there, watching his own work with an expression of serious but completely remorseless interest.

"You have killed him!" André-Louis cried out.

"Of course," the Marquis replied.

The Marquis carefully wiped his blade with a lace handkerchief. As he let the delicate cloth drop to the ground, he explained his actions. "I told him he had a very dangerous gift for speaking."

He then turned away, leaving André-Louis in complete understanding. While still holding his friend's bleeding, lifeless body, the young man shouted after him.

"Come back, you cowardly murderer, and make yourself completely safe by killing me too!"

The Marquis stopped and turned halfway around, his face dark with anger. Then M. de Chabrillane placed a restraining hand on his cousin's arm. Even though the Chevalier had helped arrange the duel, he was slightly horrified now that the deed was actually done. He did not share the cold, ruthless nature of the Marquis, and he was much younger.

"Let's go," M. de Chabrillane said. "The boy is raving mad. They were close friends."

"Did you hear what he said to me?" the Marquis demanded.

"Neither you nor any other man can deny it," André-Louis shouted back. "You confessed it yourself, sir, when you just told me why you killed him. You killed him because you were afraid of him."

"Even if that were true, what of it?" the wealthy nobleman asked coldly.

"Do you really have to ask? Do you understand nothing about life or humanity except how to wear a nice coat and style your hair? Oh yes, and how to use weapons against boys and priests. Do you have no mind to think with, and no soul to examine? Do I really have to tell you that it is a coward's choice to kill the thing he fears? It is doubly cowardly to kill someone in this manner. If you had stabbed him in the back with a knife in a dark alley, you would have at least shown the true courage of your own wickedness. It would have been an honest, undisguised crime. But you were afraid of the consequences of an assassination, even with all your power. So you hid your cowardice behind the excuse of a formal duel."

The Marquis angrily shook off his cousin's hand and took a step forward. He now held his sword as if it were a riding whip. But the Chevalier grabbed him again and held him back.

"No, no, Gervais! Let it go, in God’s name!"

"Let him come, sir," André-Louis raged, his voice thick with intense hatred. "Let him finish his coward's work on me. Then he can make himself completely safe from a coward's punishment."

M. de Chabrillane let go of his cousin. The Marquis was pale with fury, and he glared at the young man who was insulting him so recklessly. But then he stopped himself. Perhaps he suddenly remembered the rumor about who this young man's father really was. Everyone believed he was related to the Lord of Gavrillac, and it was well known that the Lord loved him dearly. The Marquis probably realized that if he pushed the situation any further, he would be trapped in a terrible dilemma. He would have to choose between spilling more blood and making an enemy of the Lord of Gavrillac, just when he desperately needed that gentleman's friendship. His other choice was to walk away and swallow the insult, which would damage his pride and weaken his authority in the local area.

Whatever the reason, the fact remains that he stopped abruptly. He let out an angry, incoherent sound that mixed rage with contempt. Then he threw up his arms in disgust, turned on his heel, and walked away quickly with his cousin.

When the innkeeper and his staff finally arrived, they found André-Louis holding the body of his dead friend. He was murmuring passionately into the deaf ear that rested almost against his lips.

"Philippe! Speak to me, Philippe! Philippe, can't you hear me? God in Heaven, Philippe!"

The innkeeper and his people saw at a single glance that no doctor or priest could help him now. The cheek resting against André-Louis's face was a terrible leaden gray color. His half-open eyes were completely glazed over, and a small trail of bloody foam trickled from his slightly parted lips.

André-Louis stumbled blindly after the men as they carried the body into the inn, his eyes completely blurred with tears. They placed the body on a bed in a small upstairs room. He knelt down beside the bed and grabbed the dead man’s hand in both of his own. Filled with helpless rage, he swore to his friend that the Marquis would pay a bitter price for this murder.

"He was afraid of your voice, Philippe," André-Louis whispered. "If I cannot get justice for this terrible crime, then his murder will at least be completely useless to him. The exact thing he feared in you, he will soon fear in me. He was terrified that you would use your powerful words to turn people against monsters like him. Well, people will still be swayed by your words. I am taking your eloquence and your arguments as my inheritance from you. I will make them my own. It does not matter that I do not believe in your gospel of freedom. I know every single word of it, and that is all that matters for our purpose now. If all else fails, your thoughts will find a voice in my living tongue. By doing this, we will at least frustrate his vile goal of silencing the voice he feared. Having your blood on his soul will do him absolutely no good. Your voice would never have hunted him down as relentlessly as my voice will, if everything else fails."

It was a dark, triumphant thought. The idea calmed him down and soothed his immediate grief, and he began to pray very softly. But then his heart trembled as a terrible thought struck him. Philippe was a man of peace. He was almost a priest, and a true apostle of Christianity. Yet he had died and gone to face his Maker with the sin of burning anger on his soul. It was a horrible thought. However, God would surely understand that his anger was righteous and justified. No matter how men interpreted the divine rules, André-Louis firmly believed that one single sin of anger could never outweigh all the loving goodness Philippe had practiced throughout his life. His great heart had always been completely pure. After all, André-Louis thought to himself, God was not a cruel, arrogant nobleman.
