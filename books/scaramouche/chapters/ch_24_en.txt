Chapter 4. AT MEUDON

Later in the week, just before noon, he received a visit from Le Chapelier.

“I have news for you, André. Your godfather is at Meudon. He arrived there two days ago. Had you heard?”

“But no. How should I hear? Why is he at Meudon?” He felt a faint excitement, though he couldn't quite explain why.

“I don’t know. There have been fresh disturbances in Brittany. It may be due to that.”

“And so he has come for shelter to his brother?” asked André-Louis.

“To his brother’s house, yes, but not to his brother. Where do you live, André? Do you never hear any of the news? Etienne de Gavrillac emigrated years ago. He was part of Monsieur d'Artois's household. He crossed the border with him. By now, he's probably in Germany with him, conspiring against France. That's what the émigrés are doing. That Austrian woman at the Tuileries will eventually destroy the monarchy.”

“Yes, yes,” said André-Louis impatiently. Politics didn't interest him at all this morning. “But about Gavrillac?”

“Why, haven’t I told you that Gavrillac is at Meudon, living in the house his brother left? Good heavens! Do I not speak French, or do you not understand the language? I believe Rabouillet, his intendant, is in charge of Gavrillac. I brought you the news the moment I received it. I thought you would probably wish to go out to Meudon.”

“Of course. I'll go at once, as soon as I can. I can't today, nor tomorrow. I am too busy here.” He waved a hand towards the inner room, from which came the click-click of blades, the quick movement of feet, and the voice of the instructor, Le Duc.

“Well, well, that's your business. You are busy. I leave you now. Let us dine this evening at the Café de Foy. Kersain will join us.”

“A moment!” André-Louis's voice stopped him at the threshold. “Is Mademoiselle de Kercadiou with her uncle?”

“How the devil should I know? Go and find out.”

He was gone, and André-Louis stood there a moment, lost in thought. Then he turned and went back to resume his interrupted explanation of Danet's demi-contre with his pupil, the Vicomte de Villeniort. He used a small-sword to illustrate the advantages of adopting it.

After that, he fenced with the Vicomte, who was perhaps his most skilled pupil at the time. All the while, André-Louis's thoughts were on the heights of Meudon. His mind calculated the lessons he had to give that afternoon and the next day, wondering which ones he could postpone without disrupting the academy. After touching the Vicomte three times in a row, he paused. He pulled himself back to the present, marveling at the precision achieved through purely mechanical action. Without giving a single thought to what he was doing, his wrist, arm, and knees had automatically performed their work. They moved like an accurate fighting machine, a result of more than a year of constant practice.

André-Louis couldn't satisfy his wish until Sunday. The impatience of the preceding days had turned it into a deep yearning. He dressed with more than ordinary care. His head was elegantly coiffed by one of those hairdressers to the nobility, many of whom were losing their jobs due to the steady flow of emigration. André-Louis then got into his hired carriage and drove out to Meudon.

The younger Kercadiou's house was as different from his elder brother's as their personalities were. The younger Kercadiou was a man of the Court, while his brother was essentially a man of the land. As an officer in the household of Monsieur le Comte d'Artois, he had built an imposing villa for himself and his family on the heights of Meudon. It stood in a miniature park, conveniently located midway between Versailles and Paris, and easily accessible from either city. Monsieur d'Artois, the royal tennis player, had been among the very first to emigrate. He had left France immediately after the fall of the Bastille. Others who left with him included the Condes, the Contis, the Polignacs, and other members of the Queen's inner circle, along with old Marshal de Broglie and Prince de Lambesc. They all realized their very names had become hateful to the people. He had gone to play tennis beyond the border. There, he intended to complete the work of ruining the French monarchy, a task he and the others had already begun in France. Etienne de Kercadiou went with him, along with several other members of his household. Etienne de Kercadiou also brought his family: a wife and four children. That's how the Seigneur de Gavrillac came to occupy his brother's handsome villa at Meudon during his absence. He was glad to escape Brittany, a province that was particularly disturbed. The nobles there had proven to be the most unyielding in all of France.

It's unlikely he was very happy there. He was a man of almost Spartan habits, used to simple food and doing things himself. He felt a little uneasy in this luxurious abode, with its soft carpets, abundant gilding, and a battalion of sleek, silent-footed servants. The younger Kercadiou had left his entire household behind. At Gavrillac, he had kept himself busy with farming matters. Here, time hung heavily on his hands. To cope, he slept a great deal. If not for Aline, who made no attempt to hide her delight at being so close to Paris and the center of everything, he probably would have retreated almost immediately from surroundings that clashed so badly with his habits. Perhaps later he would get used to this luxurious inactivity and grow resigned to it. Meanwhile, the novelty of it bothered him. So, it was a peevish and rather sleepy Monsieur de Kercadiou into whose presence André-Louis was shown that Sunday afternoon in June. He arrived unannounced, as was always the custom at Gavrillac. This was because Benoit, Monsieur de Kercadiou's old seneschal, had accompanied his lord on this easy adventure. Benoit was now installed as his maître d'hôtel here at Meudon, much to the constant and barely hidden amusement of the impertinent servants Monsieur Etienne had left behind.

Benoit welcomed Monsieur André with incoherent cries of delight. He almost gamboled around him like a faithful dog as he led him to the salon and to the Lord of Gavrillac. Benoit claimed his master would be thrilled to see Monsieur André again.

“Monseigneur! Monseigneur!” he cried in a quavering voice, entering a step or two ahead of the visitor. “It's Monsieur André... Monsieur André, your godson, who has come to kiss your hand. He's here, and so well-dressed you'd hardly recognize him. Here he is, monseigneur! Isn't he handsome?”

And the old servant rubbed his hands, convinced that he was conveying great delight to his master.

André-Louis crossed the threshold of that great room, softly carpeted and dazzling to the eye. It was immensely high. Its festooned ceiling rested on fluted pillars with gilded capitals. The door he entered through, and the windows opening onto the garden, were enormously tall. They were almost the full height of the room itself. The room was overwhelmingly gilded, with abundant ormolu decorations on the furniture. In this, it was no different from what was customary in the homes of the wealthy and well-born. Indeed, never had so much gold been used decoratively as in this era. Coined gold was almost impossible to find, and paper money had been put into circulation to make up for the shortage. André-Louis often said that if these people could only have been persuaded to put the paper on their walls and the gold into their pockets, the kingdom's finances might soon have been in better shape.

The Seigneur, dressed and ruffled to match his surroundings, had risen. He was startled by Benoit's exuberant entrance. Benoit had been almost as lonely as the Seigneur himself since their arrival at Meudon.

“What is it? Eh?” His pale, short-sighted eyes peered at the visitor. “André!” he said, a mix of surprise and sternness in his voice. The color deepened in his large, pink face.

Benoit, with his back to his master, deliberately winked and grinned at André-Louis to encourage him not to be deterred by his godfather's apparent hostility. Once that was done, the intelligent old man discreetly slipped away.

“What do you want here?” growled Monsieur de Kercadiou.

“No more than to kiss your hand, as Benoit has told you, Monsieur my godfather,” said André-Louis submissively, bowing his smooth black head.

“You've managed without kissing it for two years.”

“Don't, monsieur, blame me for my misfortune.”

The little man stood very stiffly, his disproportionately large head tilted back, his pale, prominent eyes looking very stern.

“Did you think you could make your outrageous offense any better by vanishing so heartlessly, by leaving us with no idea whether you were alive or dead?”

“At first, it was dangerous, even life-threatening, to reveal where I was. Then, for a time, I was in need, almost destitute. My pride forbade me from asking you for help, especially after what I had done and how you must view it. Later…”

“Destitute?” the Seigneur interrupted. For a moment, his lip trembled. Then he steadied himself. His frown deepened as he surveyed his very changed and elegant godson. He noted the quiet richness of André-Louis's clothing, the paste buckles and red heels on his shoes, the sword with its mother-of-pearl and silver hilt, and the carefully dressed hair that he had always seen hanging in wisps about his face. “At least you don’t look destitute now,” he sneered.

“I am not. I have prospered since. In that, monsieur, I differ from the ordinary prodigal, who returns only when he needs assistance. I return solely because I love you, monsieur, to tell you so. I came the very first moment after hearing of your presence here.” He advanced. “Monsieur my godfather!” he said, and held out his hand.

But Monsieur de Kercadiou remained unbending, wrapped in his cold dignity and resentment.

“Whatever tribulations you may have suffered, or believe you have suffered, they are far less than your disgraceful conduct deserved. I observe that they have done nothing to lessen your impudence. You think you only have to come here and say, ‘Monsieur my godfather!’ and everything will be forgiven and forgotten. That is your error. You have committed too great a wrong. You have offended against everything I stand for, and against me personally, by betraying my trust in you. You are one of those unspeakable scoundrels responsible for this revolution.”

“Alas, monsieur, I see that you share the common delusion. These unspeakable scoundrels merely demanded a constitution, as was promised them from the throne. They couldn’t have known that the promise was insincere, or that its fulfillment would be blocked by the privileged orders. The men who have truly started this revolution, monsieur, are the nobles and the prelates.”

“You dare, at such a time as this, to stand there and tell me such abominable lies! You dare to say that the nobles caused the revolution, when scores of them, following the example of Monsieur le Duc d’Aiguillon, have flung their privileges, even their title-deeds, into the lap of the people! Or perhaps you deny it?”

“Oh, no. Having recklessly set fire to their own house, they now try to put it out by throwing water on it. And where they fail, they put the entire blame on the flames.”

“I see you’ve come here to talk politics.”

“Far from it. I’ve come, if possible, to explain myself. To understand is always to forgive. That’s a great saying of Montaigne’s. If I could make you understand…”

“You can’t. You’ll never make me understand how you became so odiously notorious in Brittany.”

“Ah, not odiously, monsieur!”

“Certainly, odiously, among those that matter. It’s even said that you were Omnes Omnibus, though I cannot and will not believe that.”

“Yet it’s true.”

Monsieur de Kercadiou choked. “And you confess it? You dare to confess it?”

“What a man dares to do, he should dare to confess, unless he is a coward.”

“Oh, and to be sure, you were very brave, running away each time after you had caused trouble. You turned comedian to hide yourself, then caused more trouble as a comedian, provoked a riot in Nantes, and then ran away again to become God knows what, something dishonest judging by your affluent look. My God, man, I tell you that in these past two years, I have hoped you were dead, and you profoundly disappoint me that you are not!” He beat his hands together and raised his shrill voice to call, “Benoit!” He strode away towards the fireplace, scarlet in the face, shaking with the passion he had worked himself into. “Dead, I might have forgiven you, as one who had paid for his evil and his folly. Living, I never can forgive you. You have gone too far. God alone knows where this will end.

“Benoit, the door. Monsieur André-Louis Moreau to the door!” The tone showed an irrevocable determination. Pale and self-contained, but with a strange pain at his heart, André-Louis heard that dismissal. He saw Benoit’s white, scared face and shaking hands half-raised, as if he were about to argue with his master. And then another voice, a crisp, boyish voice, cut in.

“Uncle!” it cried, with a world of indignation and surprise in its pitch. Then: “André!” This time, a note almost of gladness, certainly of welcome, blended with the surprise that still remained.

Both turned. Half the room separated them at that moment. They saw Aline in one of the long, open windows, paused there as she was entering from the garden. Aline wore a milkmaid bonnet of the latest fashion, though without any of the tricolor embellishments so commonly seen on them.

André’s thin, long mouth twisted into a strange smile. The memory of their last parting flashed into his mind. He saw himself again, burning with indignation on the pavement of Nantes, watching her carriage as it receded down the Avenue de Gigan.

She was coming towards him now with outstretched hands, a heightened color in her cheeks, and a smile of welcome on her lips. He bowed low and kissed her hand in silence.

Then, with a glance and a gesture, she dismissed Benoit. In her imperious fashion, she appointed herself André’s advocate against the harsh dismissal she had overheard.

“Uncle,” she said, leaving André and crossing to Monsieur de Kercadiou, “you make me ashamed of you! To allow a feeling of peevishness to overwhelm all your affection for André!”

“I have no affection for him. I had once. He chose to extinguish it. He can go to the devil, and please observe that I don’t permit you to interfere.”

“But if he confesses that he has done wrong…”

“He confesses nothing of the kind. He comes here to argue with me about these infernal Rights of Man. He proclaims himself unrepentant. He proudly announces himself to have been, as all Brittany says, the scoundrel who hid himself under the sobriquet of Omnes Omnibus. Is that to be condoned?”

She turned to look at André across the wide space that now separated them.

“Is this truly how you feel? Don’t you regret it, André, now that you see all the harm that has come?”

It was a clear invitation, a plea for him to say he regretted his actions and make peace with his godfather. For a moment, he almost gave in. But then, finding such deception unworthy, he answered truthfully, though the pain he was suffering was clear in his voice.

He said slowly, “To confess regret would be to confess to a monstrous crime. Don’t you see that? Oh, monsieur, please be patient with me. Let me explain myself a little. You say I am partly responsible for some of what has happened. My speeches to the people at Rennes, and twice later at Nantes, are said to have played a part in what followed there. That might be true. It would be beyond my power to deny it completely. Revolution and bloodshed followed. More may still come. To regret implies recognizing that I have done wrong. How can I say I have done wrong, and thus take a share of responsibility for all that blood upon my soul? I will be quite frank with you to show you just how far I am from regret. What I did, I actually did against all my beliefs at the time. There was no justice in France to act against the murderer of Philippe de Vilmorin. So, I acted in the only way I imagined could make the evil done rebound upon the hand that did it, and those other hands that had the power but not the will to punish. Since then, I have come to see that I was wrong, and that Philippe de Vilmorin and those who thought with him were right.”

“You must realize, monsieur, that I am sincerely thankful to find I have done nothing that calls for regret. On the contrary, when France is given the invaluable gift of a constitution, as will happen soon, I may take pride in having played my part in creating the conditions that made this possible.”

There was a pause. Monsieur de Kercadiou’s face turned from pink to purple.

“Are you quite finished?” he asked harshly.

“If you have understood me, monsieur.”

“Oh, I have understood you. And I beg that you will go.”

André-Louis shrugged his shoulders and hung his head. He had come there so joyously, with such longing, only to receive a final dismissal. He looked at Aline. Her face was pale and troubled, but her quick mind couldn't find a way to help him. His excessive honesty had burned all his bridges.

“Very well, monsieur. Yet I would ask you to remember this after I am gone. I have not come to you as someone seeking assistance, or as someone driven to you by need. I am no returning prodigal, as I have said. I am someone who needs nothing, asks nothing, and is master of his own destiny. I have come to you driven only by affection, urged by the love and gratitude I bear you and will continue to bear you.”

“Ah, yes!” Aline cried, turning to her uncle. She thought, *Here at least was an argument in André’s favor.* “That is true. Surely that…”

Exasperated, he hissed her into silence, unable to form words.

“Perhaps this will help you think of me more kindly hereafter, monsieur.”

“I see no reason, sir, to think of you at all. Again, I beg that you will go.”

André-Louis looked at Aline for an instant, as if still hesitating.

She answered him with a glance at her furious uncle, a faint shrug, and a lift of her eyebrows. Dejection was clear on her face.

It was as if she said, “You see his mood. There is nothing to be done.”

He bowed with the singular grace the fencing-room had given him and went out the door.

“Oh, it is cruel!” Aline cried in a stifled voice, her hands clenched. She sprang to the window.

“Aline!” Her uncle’s voice stopped her. “Where are you going?”

“But we don’t know where he can be found.”

“Who wants to find that scoundrel?”

“We may never see him again.”

“That is most earnestly to be desired.”

Aline said, “Ouf!” and went out the window.

He called after her, imperiously commanding her return. But Aline, a dutiful child, closed her ears so she wouldn't have to disobey him. She sped light-footed across the lawn to the avenue, intending to intercept the departing André-Louis.

As he came out, wrapped in gloom, she stepped from the trees bordering the path into his way.

“Aline!” he cried, almost joyously.

“I didn’t want you to go like this. I couldn’t let you,” she explained. “I know him better than you do. I know that his great soft heart will soon melt. He will be filled with regret. He will want to send for you, and he won’t know where to send.”

“You think that?”

"Oh, I know it! You've arrived at a bad time. He's been irritable and difficult, poor man, ever since he got here. These comfortable surroundings are so strange to him. He's miserable away from his beloved Gavrillac, his hunting and farming. The truth is, he largely blames you in his mind for what's happened, for the necessity, or at least the good sense, of this move. Brittany, you must understand, was becoming too dangerous. The Chateau of La Tour d'Azyr, among others, was burned to the ground some months ago. At any moment, with a new spark, it could be Gavrillac's turn. And for all this, and his current discomfort, he blames you and your friends. But he'll come around soon. He'll be sorry he sent you away like this, because I know he loves you, Andre, despite everything. I'll talk some sense into him when the time is right. And then we'll want to know where to find you."

"At number 13, Rue du Hasard. The number is unlucky, and the street name is appropriate. So, both are easy to remember."

She nodded. "I will walk with you to the gates." Side by side, they walked at a leisurely pace down the long avenue, the June sunshine dappling through the shadows of the bordering trees. "You are looking well, Andre. And do you know, you've changed a lot? I'm glad you've prospered." Then, abruptly changing the subject before he had time to answer her, she brought up the matter that was most on her mind.

"I have wanted to see you so much these past months, Andre. You were the only one who could help me, the only one who could tell me the truth. And I was angry with you for never having written to say where you could be found."

"Of course, you encouraged me to do so when we last met in Nantes."

"What? Still resentful?"

"I am never resentful. You should know that." He was expressing one of his vanities; he loved to think himself a Stoic. "But I still bear the scar of a wound that would feel much better with the balm of your retraction."

"Well then, I retract, Andre. Now tell me."

"Yes, a self-seeking retraction," he said. "You're giving me something so you can get something in return." He laughed quite pleasantly. "Well, well; command me."

"Tell me, Andre." She paused, as if in some difficulty, then continued, her eyes on the ground. "Tell me the truth of that event at the Feydau."

The request made him frown. He immediately suspected the thought that prompted it. Quite simply and briefly, he gave her his version of the affair.

She listened very attentively. When he finished, she sighed; her face was very thoughtful.

"That's much like what I was told," she said. "But it was added that Monsieur de La Tour d'Azyr had gone to the theater expressly to finally break with La Binet. Do you know if that was true?"

"I don't; nor do I know any reason why it should be true. La Binet provided him the sort of amusement that he and his kind are forever craving..."

"Oh, there was a reason," she interrupted him. "I was the reason. I spoke to Madame de Sautron. I told her that I would not continue to receive someone who came to me contaminated in that way." She spoke of it with obvious difficulty, her face flushing as he watched her half-averted face.

"Had you listened to me..." he began, when she interrupted him again.

"Monsieur de Sautron conveyed my decision to him. Afterwards, he presented him to me as a man in despair, repentant, ready to give proofs, any proofs, of his sincerity and devotion to me. He told me that Monsieur de La Tour d'Azyr had sworn to him that he would cut short that affair, that he would see La Binet no more. And then, the very next day, I heard of him having almost lost his life in that riot at the theater. He had gone straight from that interview with Monsieur de Sautron, straight from those promises of future good behavior, to La Binet. I was indignant. I made my final decision. I stated definitely that I would not, under any circumstances, receive Monsieur de La Tour d'Azyr again! And then they urged this explanation upon me. For a long time, I wouldn't believe it."

"So you believe it now," Andre said quickly. "Why?"

"I have not said that I believe it now. But... but I can't disbelieve it either. Since we came to Meudon, Monsieur de La Tour d'Azyr has been here, and he himself has sworn to me that it was true."

"Oh, if Monsieur de La Tour d'Azyr has sworn..." Andre-Louis laughed with a bitter note of sarcasm.

"Have you ever known him lie?" she cut in sharply. That stopped him. "Monsieur de La Tour d'Azyr is, after all, a man of honor, and men of honor never deal in falsehood. Have you ever known him do so, that you should sneer as you have done?"

"No," he confessed. Common justice demanded that he admit that virtue, at least, in his enemy. "I have not known him lie, it's true. His kind is too arrogant, too self-confident to resort to untruth. But I have known him do things just as vile..."

"Nothing is as vile," she interrupted, speaking from the code by which she had been raised. "There is no hope only for liars, who are first cousins to thieves. It is only in falsehood that there is a real loss of honor."

"You are defending that satyr, I think," he said coldly.

"I desire to be just."

"Justice may seem like a different matter to you when you finally decide to become Marquise de La Tour d'Azyr." He spoke bitterly.

"I don't think I shall ever make that decision."

"But you are still not sure, in spite of everything."

"Can one ever be sure of anything in this world?"

"Yes. One can be sure of being foolish."

She either didn't hear him or chose to ignore him.

“You don't personally know that M. de La Tour d’Azyr is wrong, do you? You don't know for sure that he *didn't* go to the Feydau that night, as he claims?”

“No, I don't,” he admitted. “It's certainly possible. But does it really matter?”

“It might matter. Tell me, what happened to La Binet in the end?”

“I don't know.”

“You don't know?” She turned to look at him. “And you can say that so indifferently! I thought... I thought you loved her, André.”

“So did I, for a little while. I was mistaken. It took a La Tour d’Azyr to reveal the truth to me. These gentlemen do have their uses. They help stupid fellows like me understand important truths. I was lucky that this realization came before marriage. Now, I can look back on that whole episode calmly and with gratitude for my narrow escape. What I felt was nothing more than a fleeting infatuation, something often confused with love. As you can see, the experience was very educational.”

She looked at him, openly surprised.

“You know, André,” she said, “I sometimes think you have no heart.”

“Presumably because I sometimes show intelligence. And what about you, Aline? What about your attitude from the very beginning when it comes to M. de La Tour d’Azyr? Does that show heart? If I told you what it really shows, we'd just end up quarreling again. And heaven knows, I can't afford to quarrel with you now. I... I'll find another way.”

“What do you mean?”

“Oh, nothing at the moment. You're not in any danger of marrying that animal.”

“And if I were?”

“Ah! In that case, my affection for you would help me find a way to prevent it... unless...” He paused.

“Unless what?” she demanded, challenging him. She stood to her full, short height, her eyes commanding.

“Unless you could also tell me that you loved him,” he said simply. She softened suddenly and very strangely at that. Then he added, shaking his head, “But that, of course, is impossible.”

“Why?” she asked him, quite gently now.

“Because you are who you are, Aline: utterly good, pure, and adorable. Angels don't mate with devils. You might become his wife, but never his true partner, Aline—never.”

They had reached the wrought-iron gates at the end of the avenue. Through them, they saw the waiting yellow chaise that had brought André-Louis. From nearby, they heard the creak of other wheels and the beat of other hooves. Soon, another vehicle came into view and stopped beside the yellow chaise. It was a handsome equipage with polished mahogany panels, its gold and azure armorial bearings flashing brilliantly in the sunlight. A footman jumped down to open the gates wide. But at that moment, the lady inside the carriage saw Aline, waved to her, and gave a command.
