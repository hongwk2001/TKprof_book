CHAPTER 15. SAFE-CONDUCT

Over the convulsively sobbing woman, who was the mother of one and the mistress of the other, the eyes of those mortal enemies met. They stared at each other with a startled, appalled interest that left them speechless.

Aline stood beyond the table, as if turned to stone by this ultimate, horrifying revelation.

Monsieur de La Tour d’Azyr was the first to move. A memory surfaced in his bewildered mind: something Madame de Plougastel had said about a letter on the table. He walked forward, unopposed. With the announcement made, Madame de Plougastel no longer feared what would follow, so she let him pass. He walked unsteadily past his newly discovered son and picked up the sheet lying beside the candelabra. He stood reading it for a long moment, unnoticed by anyone. Aline’s eyes were fixed on André-Louis, full of wonder and sympathy, while André-Louis stared down at his mother, utterly fascinated and stunned.

Monsieur de La Tour d’Azyr read the letter slowly from beginning to end. Then, very quietly, he put it back. His immediate concern, as a man from an artificial age strictly trained in suppressing emotion, was to compose himself. He then stepped back to Madame de Plougastel’s side and stooped to help her up.

“Thérèse,” he said.

Instinctively obeying the unspoken command, she made an effort to rise and regain her composure. The Marquis partly guided, partly carried her to the armchair by the table.

André-Louis watched. Still numb and bewildered, he made no attempt to help. He saw the Marquis bending over Madame de Plougastel as if in a dream. As if in a dream, he heard him ask:

“How long have you known this, Thérèse?”

“I… I have always known it… always. I entrusted him to Kercadiou. I saw him once as a child… Oh, but what does that matter now?”

“Why was I never told? Why did you deceive me? Why did you tell me that this child had died a few days after birth? Why, Thérèse? Why?”

“I was afraid. I… I thought it better that way—that nobody, nobody, not even you, should know. And nobody has known, except Quintin, until last night. He was forced to tell him then, to persuade him to come here and save me.”

“But what about me, Thérèse?” the Marquis insisted. “It was my right to know.”

“Your right? What could you have done? Acknowledge him? And then what? Ha!” It was a strange, desperate sound of laughter. “There was Plougastel; there was my family. And there was you… you, yourself, who had stopped caring, in whom the fear of discovery had stifled any love. So why should I have told you then? Why? I wouldn’t have told you now if there had been any other way to… to save you both. Once before, I felt just such dreadful fear when you and he fought in the Bois. I was on my way to prevent it when you met me. I would have revealed the truth, as a last resort, to stop that horror. But mercifully, God spared me the necessity then.”

It had not occurred to any of them to doubt her statement, incredible though it might seem. If anyone had, her current words would have resolved all doubt. They explained much that had been unclear to each of her listeners until this moment.

Overcome, Monsieur de La Tour d’Azyr reeled to a chair and sat down heavily. Losing control of himself for a moment, he buried his haggard face in his hands.

From the distance, through the open garden windows, came the faint throbbing of a drum. It was a reminder of what was happening around them. But the sound went unnoticed. To each of them, it must have seemed that they were facing a horror greater than any that might be tormenting Paris. At last, André-Louis began to speak, his voice level and impossibly cold.

“Monsieur de La Tour d’Azyr,” he said, “I trust you’ll agree that this disclosure, which can hardly be more distasteful and horrible to you than it is to me, changes nothing. It erases nothing of all that lies between us. Or, if it changes anything, it merely adds to that score. And yet… Oh, but what good is talking! Here, monsieur, take this safe-conduct. It’s made out for Madame de Plougastel’s footman. Use it to escape as best you can. In return, I will ask you a favor: never allow me to see you or hear of you again.”

“André!” His mother cried, turning sharply to him. And again, she asked that question. “Have you no heart? What has he ever done to you that you should harbor such bitter hatred for him?”

“You shall hear, madame. Once, two years ago, in this very room, I told you about a man who had brutally killed my dearest friend and ruined the girl I was to have married. Monsieur de La Tour d’Azyr is that man.”

A moan was her only answer. She covered her face with her hands.

The Marquis slowly rose to his feet again. He came slowly forward, his smoldering eyes scanning his son’s face.

“You are hard,” he said grimly. “But I recognize that hardness. It comes from the blood you carry.”

“Spare me that,” André-Louis said.

The Marquis inclined his head. “I will not mention it again. But I want you to at least understand me, and you too, Thérèse. You accuse me, sir, of murdering your dearest friend. I will admit that the methods I used were perhaps unworthy. But what other methods were available to me to address an urgency that every day since then proves to have existed? Monsieur de Vilmorin was a revolutionary, a man with new ideas that aimed to overthrow society and rebuild it to suit the desires of people like himself. I belonged to the order that, just as justifiably, wanted society to remain as it was. Not only was it better that way for me and my family, but I also argue—and you have yet to prove me wrong—that it is better that way for the entire world. Indeed, no other conceivable society is possible. Every human society must, by necessity, be made up of several layers. You may temporarily disrupt it into an amorphous whole with a revolution like this, but only temporarily. Soon, out of the chaos that is all you and your kind can ever produce, order must be restored, or life will perish. And with the restoration of order comes the restoration of the various layers necessary for an organized society. Those who were at the top yesterday may find themselves dispossessed in the new order of things, without any benefit to society as a whole. I resisted that change. I fought its spirit with whatever weapons were available, whenever and wherever I encountered it. Monsieur de Vilmorin was an incendiary of the worst kind, an eloquent man full of false ideals that misled poor, ignorant men into believing the proposed change could make the world a better place for them. You are an intelligent man, and I challenge you to answer me from your heart and conscience that such a thing was true or possible. You know that it is untrue; you know that it is a harmful doctrine. And what made it worse on Monsieur de Vilmorin’s lips was that he was sincere and eloquent. His voice was a danger that had to be removed—silenced. So much was necessary for self-defense. I did it in self-defense. I held no grudge against Monsieur de Vilmorin. He was a man of my own class; a gentleman of pleasant manners, amiable, estimable, and capable.”

“You imagine me killing him for the sheer lust of it, like some jungle beast throwing itself upon its natural prey. That has been your mistake from the start. I did what I did with the heaviest heart—oh, spare me your sneer! I do not lie; I have never lied. And I swear to you here and now, by my every hope of Heaven, that what I say is true. I loathed the thing I did. Yet for my own sake and the sake of my order, I had to do it. Ask yourself whether Monsieur de Vilmorin would have hesitated for a moment if, by causing my death, he could have brought the Utopia of his dreams a moment closer to reality.”

“After that, you decided that the sweetest vengeance would be to frustrate my goals by reviving in yourself the voice I had silenced. You chose to carry forward the fantastic apostleship of equality that was Monsieur de Vilmorin’s. You lacked the vision that would have shown you that God did not create men as equals. Well, tonight you are in a position to judge which of us was right, and which was wrong. You see what is happening here in Paris. You see the foul specter of Anarchy stalking through a land that has fallen into confusion. You probably have enough imagination to conceive something of what must follow. And do you deceive yourself that an ideal form of society will rise out of this filth and ruin? Don’t you understand that society must soon re-order itself out of all this?”

“But why say more? I must have said enough to make you understand the only thing that really matters: that I killed Monsieur de Vilmorin as a matter of duty to my order. And the truth, which though it may offend you should also convince you, is that tonight I can look back on the deed with equanimity, without a single regret, apart from what lies between you and me.”

“When you knelt beside the body of your friend that day at Gavrillac and insulted and provoked me, if I had been the tiger you imagined me to be, I would have killed you too. I am, as you may know, a man of quick passions. Yet I curbed the natural anger you aroused in me, because I could forgive an insult to myself where I could not overlook a calculated attack upon my order.”

He paused for a moment. André-Louis stood still, listening and wondering. The others did too. Then the Marquis continued, sounding less confident. "Regarding Mademoiselle Binet, I was unfortunate. I wronged you unintentionally. I didn't know about your relationship."

Finally, André-Louis interrupted him sharply with a question. "Would it have made a difference if you had known?"

"No," the Marquis answered frankly. "I have the flaws of my class. I cannot pretend that any scruple like the one you suggest would have mattered to me. But can you blame me much for that, if you're capable of any impartial judgment?"

"Considering everything, monsieur, I'm quickly coming to the conclusion that it's impossible to blame anyone for anything in this world. We are all just playthings of destiny. Consider, monsieur, this gathering, this family gathering, here tonight. While out there... Oh, my God, let's end this! Let's go our separate ways and close this horrible chapter of our lives."

The Marquis de La Tour considered him gravely and sadly, in silence for a moment.

"Perhaps it is best," he said at last, in a small voice. He turned to Madame de Plougastel. "If there is one wrong I must admit in my life, one I bitterly regret, it is the wrong I have done to you, my dear..."

"Not now, Gervais! Not now!" she faltered, interrupting him.

"Now, for the first and last time. I am going. It's not likely we'll ever meet again. I'll probably never see any of you again, you who should have been the nearest and dearest to me. He says we are all playthings of destiny. Ah, but not entirely. Destiny is an intelligent force, moving with purpose. In life, we pay for the evil that we do. That is the lesson I have learned tonight. By an act of betrayal, I unknowingly fathered a son. He was as ignorant as I was of our relationship, but he has become the evil genius of my life. He has crossed and thwarted me, and finally helped to pull me down into ruin. It is just, poetically just. My full and resigned acceptance of that fact is the only atonement I can offer you."

He stooped and took one of Madame's hands that lay limply in her lap.

"Goodbye, Therese!" His voice broke. He had reached the end of his iron self-control.

She rose and clung to him for a moment, unashamed in front of them. The ashes of that dead romance had been deeply stirred tonight. Deep down, some lingering embers had been found, and they glowed brightly now before their final extinction. Yet she made no attempt to detain him. She understood that their son had pointed out the only wise, the only possible course, and was thankful that the Marquis de La Tour d'Azyr accepted it.

"God keep you, Gervais," she murmured. "You will take the safe-conduct, and you will let me know when you are safe?"

He held her face between his hands for an instant. Then he very gently kissed her and put her from him. Standing erect and outwardly calm again, he looked across at André-Louis, who was offering him a sheet of paper.

"It is the safe-conduct. Take it, monsieur. It is my first and last gift to you, and certainly the last gift I should ever have thought of making you: the gift of life. In a sense, it makes us quits. The irony, sir, is not mine, but Fate's. Take it, monsieur, and go in peace."

The Marquis de La Tour d'Azyr took it. His eyes looked hungrily into the lean, sternly set face confronting him. He thrust the paper into his jacket, and then abruptly and convulsively held out his hand. His son's eyes asked a question.

"Let there be peace between us, in God's name," the Marquis said thickly.

Pity stirred at last in André-Louis. Some of the sternness left his face. He sighed. "Goodbye, monsieur," he said.

"You are hard," his father told him, speaking wistfully. "But perhaps you are right to be so. In other circumstances, I should have been proud to have acknowledged you as my son. As it is..." He broke off abruptly, then just as abruptly added, "Goodbye."

He released his son's hand and stepped back. They bowed formally to each other. Then the Marquis de La Tour d'Azyr bowed to Mademoiselle de Kercadiou in utter silence, a bow that contained a sense of utter renunciation, of finality.

After that, he turned and walked stiffly out of the room, and so out of all their lives. Months later, they were to hear of him in the service of the Emperor of Austria.
