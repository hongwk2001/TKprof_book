Book 2: The Buskin
Chapter 1: The Trespassers

Andre-Louis soon came upon the Redon road. He obeyed his instinct rather than his reason, turned his face to the south, and walked wearily forward. He had no clear idea of where he was going or where he should go. The only important thing at the moment was to put as much distance as possible between Gavrillac and himself.

He had a vague idea of returning to Nantes. He thought he might use his new talent for public speaking to excite the people there. They could shelter him as the first victim of the persecution he had warned them about and urged them to fight against. But he only considered this as a vague possibility. He felt no real urge to act on it.

He chuckled at the memory of Fresnel with his muffled face and glaring eyeballs. He later wrote in his confessions that he felt he had done quite well for someone who was not a man of action. This idea comes up often in his sketchy writings. He constantly reminds the reader that his activities are mental, not physical, and he apologizes when strict necessity forces him into violence. I suspect this insistence on his philosophical detachment shows his deep-seated vanity, even though he had plenty of justification for it.

His fatigue brought depression and self-criticism. He realized he had foolishly gone too far by insulting and denouncing Monsieur de Lesdiguieres. He wrote somewhere that it is much better to be wicked than to be stupid. He believed that most of the world's misery comes from stupidity, not wickedness, as priests tell us. He considered anger the most deplorable of all stupidities. Yet he had allowed himself to get angry with a creature like Monsieur de Lesdiguieres. The King's Lieutenant was a lackey and a nobody, despite his power to do evil. Andre-Louis could easily have completed his self-imposed mission without making the King's Lieutenant so vengeful.

He saw himself launched into the world with only the riding suit he wore. His total wealth consisted of a single gold louis and a few silver coins. His knowledge of the law had not been enough to protect him from the consequences of breaking it.

He had some other things that would eventually save him, though he did not count them at the time. He had a gift for laughter, which he had repressed lately. He also had a philosophical outlook and a lively temperament. These are the standard tools of adventurers in all ages.

He walked mechanically through the night until he could walk no more. He skirted the little town of Guichen. He was within a half mile of Guignen, and Gavrillac was a good seven miles behind him. His legs refused to carry him any further.

He stopped when he was halfway across the vast common north of Guignen. He had left the road and carelessly taken a footpath across the poor pasture, which was dotted with clumps of gorse. A thorn hedge bordered the common a short distance to his right. A tall building stood beyond the hedge. He could see it was an open barn on the edge of a large meadow. That dark shadow might have caused him to stop, suggesting shelter to his subconscious mind. He hesitated for a moment and then walked toward a gap in the hedge that was closed by a wooden gate. He pushed the gate open, went through the gap, and stood in front of the barn. It was as big as a house but consisted only of a roof supported by six tall brick pillars. A huge stack of hay was packed tightly under the roof, promising a warm bed on such a cold night. Stout wooden beams were built into the brick pillars. Their ends stuck out, serving as ladders so workers could climb up to stack or remove hay. Andre-Louis used his remaining strength to climb one of these pillars. He reached the top safely. He had to kneel because there was no room to stand up. He took off his coat, his neckcloth, and his wet boots and stockings. He made a hollow space in the hay and lay down in it. He covered himself up to his neck with the hay he had moved. Within five minutes, he forgot all his worldly troubles and fell sound asleep.

The sun was high in the sky when he woke up. He realized it was late morning even before he remembered where he was or how he got there. He heard the drone of voices close by, but he ignored them at first. He felt wonderfully refreshed, luxuriously sleepy, and very warm.

His mind cleared, and he remembered his situation. He raised his head out of the hay so he could listen better. His pulse beat a little faster because he feared the voices might mean danger. Then he heard the comforting sound of a woman's voice. It was musical and silvery, although it sounded alarmed.

"Oh my God, Leander, we must separate at once. What if my father..."

A man's voice interrupted her, sounding calm and reassuring.

"No, Climene, you are wrong. No one is coming. We are completely safe. Why are you frightened by shadows?"

"Oh, Leander, I tremble just thinking about what would happen if he found us here together."

Andre-Louis did not need to hear any more. He knew he was listening to a pair of lovers. They had less to fear from life than he did, but they were much more timid. His curiosity made him crawl out of his warm bed to the edge of the hay. He lay on his stomach, stuck his head out, and looked down.

A young man and a young woman stood in the short grass between the barn and the hedge. The man was well-built and handsome. He had fine chestnut hair tied back with a wide black satin ribbon. His clothes had some cheap and showy decorations that made a bad first impression. He wore a fashionable coat made of faded plum-colored velvet trimmed with silver lace. The lace had lost its shine a long time ago. He wore ruffles, but they lacked starch and hung limply over his fine, delicate hands. He wore plain black cloth breeches and black cotton stockings. These plain clothes clashed with his fancy coat. His strong shoes had cheap, dull paste buckles. Andre-Louis would have assumed the man was a dishonest trickster if he did not have such an innocent and friendly face. Andre-Louis decided to wait before judging the man and looked closely at the girl. He found her incredibly attractive right from the start. This was surprising because he was a studious man who rarely paid attention to women.

She was very young, perhaps twenty at the most. Her face and figure were nearly perfect. She also had a sparkling energy and a graceful way of moving. Andre-Louis had never seen so many wonderful qualities in one person. Her silvery voice had woken him up, and it sounded exquisite. He thought her voice alone could make the ugliest woman irresistible. She wore a green cloth cloak with the hood pushed back. Her beautiful head was fully visible. The morning sun shone on her light brown hair, which hung in curls around her oval face. Her skin was as delicate as a rose petal. He could not see the color of her eyes from that distance, but he guessed they were blue because they sparkled under her dark eyebrows.

He felt annoyed to see her so close to this pretty young man in his worn-out noble clothes. He could not guess her social class, but she spoke clearly and used cultured words. He strained to listen to them.

"I will never have peace until we are safely married, Leander," she said. "I will not feel safe from him until then. But if we marry without my father's consent, we will only make trouble for ourselves. I am almost losing hope of ever getting his permission."

Andre-Louis thought her father must be a sensible man. He could see past Leander's shabby clothes and cheap buckles.

Leander stood directly in front of her and held both her hands. "My dear Climene, you are wrong to lose hope," he answered. "I have not told you all the details of my plan to win over your cruel father. I want you to enjoy the surprise. But trust me, and trust the clever friend I told you about. He should be here any minute."

Andre-Louis thought Leander was a pompous fool. He wondered if the man had memorized that speech or if he naturally spoke in such a stiff and formal way. How could such a sweet girl waste her time on such a snob? And what a ridiculous name he had.

Meanwhile, the girl was speaking again.

"My heart wants to trust you, Leander, but I am afraid your plan will be too late. I am supposed to marry the horrible Marquis of Sbrufadelli today. He will arrive by noon to sign the marriage contract. He will make me the Marchioness of Sbrufadelli. Oh!" The cry of pain came from her tender heart. "The name burns my lips. I could never say it if it belonged to me. The man is completely awful. Save me, Leander. Please save me. You are my only hope."

Andre-Louis felt a sharp pang of disappointment. She was not as wonderful as he had first thought. She seemed to be copying her lover's stiff way of speaking. Her words sounded incredibly fake. They made sense to his brain, but they did not touch his heart. Perhaps this was just because he disliked Leander and the whole situation.

So her father was making her marry a marquis. That meant she must come from a good family. Yet she wanted to run away with this boring adventurer in his faded lace. Andre-Louis supposed this was typical behavior for women. His philosophy had taught him that women were the craziest part of a crazy human race.

"It will never happen," Leander shouted passionately. "Never. I swear it." He shook his weak fist at the sky like a hero defying the gods. "Ah, here is our clever friend." Andre-Louis missed the friend's name because Leander turned toward the gap in the hedge. "I am sure he brings us news."

Andre-Louis also looked toward the gap. A thin man walked through it. He wore a rusty cloak and a three-cornered hat pulled down low over his nose to hide his face. He took off his hat and bowed deeply to the young lovers. Andre-Louis thought to himself that he would also hide his face if he had such an ugly, suspicious-looking face. Leander looked like he was wearing a nobleman's old clothes, and the newcomer looked like he was wearing Leander's old clothes. The man's face was covered in a three-day beard, but he still walked with confidence. He strutted forward and bowed in a polite, practiced way.

"Monsieur," the newcomer said, acting like a secret agent. "The time for action is here, and the Marquis is here too. That is the situation."

The young lovers jumped apart in shock. Climene clasped her hands, opened her mouth, and breathed heavily. Leander stood there with his mouth hanging open, looking completely foolish and terrified.

The newcomer kept talking. "I was at the inn an hour ago when he arrived. I watched him closely while he ate breakfast. Now I am absolutely certain our plan will work. I could talk for hours about how ugly and fat he is, but that does not matter. We only care about how smart he is. I can confidently say he is so dull and stupid that he will fall right into every trap I have set for him."

"Tell me, tell me. Speak," Climene begged him. She held out her hands in a way that would melt any man's heart. Then she gasped and screamed softly. "My father," she cried, looking wildly at the two men. "He is coming. We are ruined."

"You must run away, Climene," Leander said.

"It is too late," she cried. "Too late. He is already here."

"Stay calm, mademoiselle, stay calm," the clever friend told her. "Keep calm and trust me. I promise you everything will be fine."

"Oh," Leander cried weakly. "You can say whatever you want, my friend, but we are ruined. This is the end of all our hopes. Your clever plans will never get us out of this. Never."

A huge man walked through the gap in the hedge. He had a red, round face and a large nose. He wore decent clothes, like a respectable middle-class man. He was clearly angry, but he expressed his anger in a way that amazed Andre-Louis.

"Leander, you are an idiot. You have no energy. You have no passion. A farm boy would not believe you. Have you even thought about what those words mean?" The fat man threw his round hat on the ground and stood next to Leander. He then repeated the exact words Leander had just said, while the other three watched him closely.

"Oh, you can say whatever you want, my friend, but we are ruined. This is the end of all our hopes. Your clever plans will never get us out of this. Never."

His voice shook with desperate emotion. He turned back to Leander. "Do it like that," he ordered with contempt. "Make sure the audience hears the passion and hopelessness in your voice. Remember, you are not asking Scaramouche if he patched your pants. You are a desperate lover expressing your pain."

He stopped talking suddenly and looked startled. Andre-Louis finally understood what was happening. He realized he had been tricked by the actors, and he started laughing out loud. His loud laughter echoed strangely under the large barn roof and startled the people below.

The fat man recovered first. He used one of his usual sarcastic remarks.

"Listen," he cried. "Even the gods are laughing at you, Leander." Then he yelled up at the roof of the barn. "Hey, you up there."

Andre-Louis stuck his messy head further over the edge.

"Good morning," he said politely. He stood up on his knees and looked past the hedge. He saw a huge, battered travel carriage on the wide common. There was also a cart piled with wood and covered by an oiled canvas sheet. Next to it was a wagon shaped like a house, complete with a tin chimney that was slowly puffing smoke. Three heavy horses and two donkeys were eating grass nearby. Their legs were tied so they could not wander off. If Andre-Louis had seen all this earlier, he would have understood the strange scene he was watching. Other people were moving around behind the hedge. Three more people walked through the gap. One was a girl with a cute, turned-up nose, who was probably playing Columbine, the maid. The second was a thin, energetic young man who had to be the servant Harlequin. The third was a clumsy young man who might be a fool or a doctor in the play.

Andre-Louis saw all of this in a quick glance while he was saying good morning. Pantaloon yelled back at him.

"What the devil are you doing up there?"

"Exactly what you are doing down there," Andre-Louis answered. "I am trespassing."

"What?" Pantaloon asked. He looked at his companions, losing some of his confidence. The actors trespassed all the time, but it was still uncomfortable to hear someone say the word out loud.

"Whose land is this?" he asked, sounding less sure of himself.

Andre-Louis answered while he pulled on his stockings. "I believe it belongs to the Marquis de La Tour d'Azyr."

"That sounds like an important name. Is the gentleman strict?"

"The gentleman is the devil," Andre-Louis said. "Actually, I take that back. The devil is a gentleman compared to him."

"And yet," said the ugly man playing Scaramouche, "you admit you are trespassing on his property yourself."

"Ah, but I am a lawyer. Everyone knows lawyers cannot follow the law, just like actors cannot act. Besides, nature forces us to do things, and nature is stronger than the law. Nature conquered me last night when I reached this spot. So I slept here without worrying about the very powerful Marquis de La Tour d'Azyr. But I must point out, Monsieur Scaramouche, that I did not trespass as openly as you and your friends."

Andre-Louis put on his boots and quickly climbed down to the ground. He was in his shirtsleeves and carried his riding coat over his arm. The fat manager looked at him closely while Andre-Louis put his coat on. Pantaloon noticed that Andre-Louis wore plain but stylish clothes. His shirt was made of fine cotton, and he spoke like an educated man. Pantaloon decided to be polite.

"I appreciate your warning, sir," he began.

"You should act on it, my friend. The Marquis's guards have orders to shoot trespassers. You should pack up and leave, just like I am doing."

They immediately followed him through the gap in the hedge back to their camp on the common. Andre-Louis said goodbye to them there. As he turned to leave, he saw a young actor washing his face in a bucket on the steps of the house-wagon. Andre-Louis hesitated for a moment and then spoke directly to Pantaloon, who was standing nearby.

"Monsieur, I do not want to take advantage of your kindness," he said. "But I would love to wash my face like that excellent young man before I leave."

"My dear sir, it is no trouble at all," the fat manager said, glowing with good nature. "Please go right ahead. Rhodomont will give you everything you need. He plays the tough guy on stage, but he is the best-dressed man in our group in real life. Hey, Rhodomont."

The young actor stopped bending over the bucket. He stood up straight and looked through a face full of soap bubbles. Pantaloon gave him an order. Rhodomont was just as gentle offstage as he was terrifying on it. He friendly offered the stranger the use of his bucket.

Andre-Louis took off his neckcloth and his coat again. He rolled up the sleeves of his fine shirt. Rhodomont gave him soap, a towel, a broken comb, and even a greasy hair ribbon in case he had lost his own. Andre-Louis politely refused the ribbon but gladly took the comb. He washed himself and stood with the towel over his shoulder. He combed his messy hair while looking in a broken mirror attached to the door of the wagon.

He was standing there while the gentle Rhodomont chatted beside him. Then Andre-Louis heard the sound of horse hooves. He looked over his shoulder casually and froze in place. His mouth hung open, and he held the comb in the air. A group of seven policemen on horseback rode across the road next to the common. They wore blue coats with red trim.

He knew immediately who these police officers were looking for. He felt as if the shadow of the gallows had suddenly fallen over him.

The group of riders stopped next to the actors. The sergeant in charge yelled across the common.

"Hey, you there. Hey." His voice sounded threatening.

All twelve members of the acting company stopped and stared. Pantaloon stepped forward proudly. He threw his head back and acted like a high-ranking official.

"Now, what the devil is this?" he asked aloud, though it was unclear who he was talking to.

The riders spoke briefly among themselves. Then they trotted straight across the common toward the actors' camp.

Andre-Louis stayed near the back of the wagon. He kept running the comb through his hair, but he did it without thinking. His mind was focused entirely on the approaching soldiers. He gathered his wits, ready to run in any direction if he had to.

The sergeant shouted a question while he was still a distance away. He sounded impatient.

"Who gave you permission to camp here?"

This question did not make Andre-Louis feel any better. He knew these men were not just looking for vagrants and trespassers. That was not their main job. They were probably just checking the camp to see if they could demand some bribe money. It was highly likely they were from Rennes and were really hunting for a young lawyer accused of treason. Meanwhile, Pantaloon shouted back.

"Who gave us permission? What permission? This is public land, free for everyone."

The sergeant laughed a nasty laugh and rode closer, with his men behind him.

"There is no such thing as public land on the Marquis de La Tour d'Azyr's estate," a voice said next to Pantaloon. "This land requires rent. His managers collect money from anyone who lets their animals eat the grass here."

Pantaloon turned and saw Andre-Louis standing beside him. Andre-Louis was in his shirtsleeves without a neckcloth. The towel was still draped over his shoulder, and he held a comb in his hand. His hair was only half-combed.

"Good God," Pantaloon swore. "This Marquis de La Tour d'Azyr is a monster."

"I already told you what I think of him," Andre-Louis said. "You should let me handle these men. I know how to deal with them." Andre-Louis did not wait for Pantaloon to agree. He stepped forward to meet the approaching police officers. He knew that only bold action could save him now.

A moment later, the sergeant stopped his horse next to the half-dressed young man. Andre-Louis kept combing his hair. He looked up with a small smile that was meant to look friendly, innocent, and harmless.

Despite the smile, the sergeant spoke to him roughly. "Are you the leader of this group of beggars?"

"Yes, well, my father over there is the real leader." Andre-Louis pointed his thumb at Pantaloon, who was standing far enough away that he could not hear them. "How can we help you, captain?"

"I am here to tell you that you will probably all go to jail for this." His voice was loud and bullying. Everyone in the acting company heard him and stood completely still in fear. The life of a traveling actor was hard enough without going to jail.

"But why, captain? This is public land, free for anyone to use."

"No, it is not."

"Where are the fences?" Andre-Louis asked. He waved the hand holding the comb to show how open the area was.

"Fences," the sergeant sneered. "Fences have nothing to do with it. This land requires payment. You cannot let your animals graze here unless you pay the Marquis de La Tour d'Azyr."

"But we are not grazing," Andre-Louis said innocently.

"Go to the devil, you fool. You are not eating grass, but your animals are."

"They eat very little," Andre-Louis apologized, offering his charming smile again.

The sergeant looked angrier than ever. "That is not the point. The point is that you are basically stealing, and thieves go to jail."

"I suppose you are right according to the strict rules of the law," Andre-Louis sighed. He started combing his hair again and kept looking up at the sergeant. "But we made a mistake because we did not know the rules. We appreciate your warning." He moved the comb to his left hand and reached into his right pocket. The sound of jingling coins could be heard. "We are very sorry to have wasted your time. Perhaps your men would do us the honor of stopping at the next inn. You could drink a toast to Monsieur de La Tour d'Azyr, or anyone else you prefer."

The sergeant looked a little less angry, but he was still stern.

"Fine," he said roughly. "But you have to pack up and leave. Do you understand?" He leaned down from his saddle and held out his hand. Andre-Louis placed a silver three-livre coin in it.

"We will leave in half an hour," Andre-Louis said.

"Why half an hour? Why not right now?"

"Oh, we need time to eat breakfast."

They stared at each other. The sergeant looked at the large silver coin in his hand. Finally, his face softened.

"After all," he said, "it is not our job to act as tax collectors for the Marquis de La Tour d'Azyr. We are the police force from Rennes." Andre-Louis's eyes twitched nervously, betraying his fear for a split second. "But if you stay too long, watch out for the Marquis's guards. They are not friendly at all. Well, enjoy your breakfast, sir," he said as a goodbye.

"Have a pleasant ride, captain," Andre-Louis answered.

The sergeant turned his horse around, and his men did the same. They were about to ride away when the sergeant stopped his horse again.

"You there, sir," he called back. Andre-Louis jumped over to the horse's side immediately. "We are looking for a criminal named Andre-Louis Moreau from Gavrillac. He is running from the law and will hang for treason. Have you seen anyone acting suspiciously on your travels?"

"Yes, we certainly have," Andre-Louis said boldly. His face looked eager, as if he really wanted to help.

"You have?" the sergeant shouted loudly. "Where? When?"

"Yesterday evening near Guignen."

"Yes, go on." The sergeant thought he was close to catching his man.

"There was a man who seemed very afraid that someone would recognize him. He was about fifty years old."

"Fifty," the sergeant cried, looking disappointed. "Bah. The man we want is your age. He is thin and about your height. He has black hair just like yours. Keep your eyes open while you travel, master actor. The King's Lieutenant in Rennes sent word this morning that he will pay ten gold louis to anyone who helps catch this criminal. You can earn ten louis just by looking out for him and telling the nearest judge. That would be a great prize for you."

"A great prize indeed, captain," Andre-Louis answered with a laugh.

The sergeant kicked his horse with his spurs and trotted away after his men. Andre-Louis kept laughing silently. He often did this when he found a joke especially funny.

Then he turned around slowly and walked back to Pantaloon and the rest of the actors, who were still watching him closely.

Pantaloon stepped forward and held out both his hands. For a moment, Andre-Louis thought the big man was going to hug him.

"You are our savior," the big man announced loudly. "We already felt the shadow of the jail cell closing in on us. We are poor, but we are honest people. Not a single one of us has ever suffered the shame of going to prison. None of us would survive it. But thanks to you, my friend, we are safe. What kind of magic did you use?"

"I used the magic of the King's portrait. As you have probably noticed, the French are a very loyal people. They love their King, but they love his portrait even more, especially when it is printed on gold. They even respect it on silver. The sergeant was so moved by the sight of the King's noble face on a three-livre coin that his anger vanished. He left us in peace."

"Ah, yes. He said we must leave. Let's get to work, boys. Come on."

"But not until after breakfast," Andre-Louis said. "That loyal officer gave us half an hour to eat because he was so moved. He did mention the Marquis's guards. But he knows as well as I do that we don't really have to fear them. If they do come, the King's portrait on copper coins will have the same melting effect on them. So, my dear Monsieur Pantaloon, please enjoy your breakfast. I can smell your cooking from here. Based on the smell, I don't even need to wish you a good appetite."

"My friend, my savior." Pantaloon threw a huge arm around the young man's shoulders. "You must stay and eat breakfast with us."

"I admit I was hoping you would ask me," Andre-Louis said.
