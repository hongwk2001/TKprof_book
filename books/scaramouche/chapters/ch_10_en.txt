André-Louis soon reached the Redon road. Following instinct more than reason, he turned south and trudged onward, tired and moving automatically. He had no clear idea where he was going or where he should go. All that mattered at the moment was to put as much distance as possible between himself and Gavrillac.

He had a vague, half-formed idea of returning to Nantes. There, he thought, he could use his newfound skill in public speaking to rouse the people. He would get them to shelter him as the first victim of the persecution he had predicted, the one he had sworn them to fight against. But this idea was just an indefinite possibility in his mind. He felt no real urge to act on it.

Meanwhile, he chuckled, remembering Fresnel as he had last seen him, face muffled and eyes glaring. He wrote, "For someone who was anything but a man of action, I felt that I had handled myself quite well." This phrase appears often in his brief "Confessions." He constantly reminds the reader that he is a man of thought, not physical activity. He apologizes whenever extreme necessity forces him into violence. I suspect this emphasis on his philosophical detachment, for which I admit he had good reason, actually reveals his persistent vanity.

As his fatigue grew, so did his depression and self-criticism. He had stupidly gone too far by insultingly denouncing Monsieur de Lesdiguieres. He says somewhere, "It is much better to be wicked than to be stupid. Most of this world's misery, as priests don't tell us, comes not from wickedness, but from stupidity." We know that of all stupidities, he considered anger the most regrettable. Yet he had allowed himself to get angry with a person like Monsieur de Lesdiguieres. This man was a mere lackey, a frivolous nobody, despite his potential for evil. André-Louis could have perfectly completed his self-appointed mission without provoking the King's Lieutenant's vengeful anger.

He saw himself vaguely launched into life. All he had was the riding suit he was wearing, one louis d'or, and a few silver coins as his entire capital. His knowledge of law had been insufficient to protect him from the consequences of breaking it.

He also possessed, though he didn't realize these would be his true salvation, his gift for laughter, which had been sadly suppressed lately. He had a philosophical outlook and a changeable temperament, qualities that are the hallmark of adventurers throughout history.

He continued to tramp mechanically through the night until he felt he couldn't go on. He had walked around the small town of Guichen and was now within half a mile of Guignen. Gavrillac was a good seven miles behind him. His legs simply refused to carry him any farther.

He was halfway across the vast common north of Guignen when he stopped. He had left the road and carelessly followed a footpath that cut across the expanse of sparse pasture, dotted with clumps of gorse. A stone's throw to his right, a thorn hedge bordered the common. Beyond it, a tall building loomed. He knew it was an open barn, standing at the edge of a long stretch of meadowland. Perhaps that dark, silent shadow had made him stop, suggesting shelter to his subconscious mind. He hesitated for a moment. Then he headed towards a spot where a five-barred gate closed a gap in the hedge. He pushed the gate open, went through, and now stood before the barn. It was as large as a house, yet it was just a roof supported by half a dozen tall brick pillars. But under that roof, a huge stack of hay was densely packed, promising a warm bed on such a cold night. Strong timbers had been built into the brick pillars, with ends sticking out to serve as ladders. Laborers could use these to climb up and pack or remove hay. With what little strength he had left, André-Louis climbed one of these and landed safely at the top. He had to kneel there, as there wasn't enough room to stand upright. Once up, he took off his coat and neckcloth, his soaked boots and stockings. Next, he cleared a hollow for his body. Lying down in it, he covered himself to the neck with the hay he had removed. Within five minutes, he was lost to all worldly worries and soundly asleep.

When he next woke up, the sun was already high in the sky. He concluded that morning was well underway, even before he fully realized where he was or how he got there. Then, to his waking senses, came a low hum of voices nearby. At first, he paid little attention. He felt wonderfully refreshed, luxuriously drowsy, and luxuriously warm.

But as his consciousness and memory became clearer, he lifted his head out of the hay. He wanted to free both ears to listen, his pulse quickening slightly with the dawning fear that those voices might mean trouble for him. Then he heard the distinct, reassuring voice of a woman, musical and silvery, though filled with alarm.

"Oh, my God, Leandre, let's separate at once. If it should be my father..."

A man's voice then broke in, calm and reassuring.

"No, no, Climene, you are mistaken. No one is coming. We are quite safe. Why do you jump at shadows?"

"Oh, Leandre, if he should find us here together! I tremble at the very thought."

André-Louis needed nothing more to be reassured. He had heard enough to know this was just a pair of lovers. They had less to fear in life, yet, like others of their kind, they were more timid than he was. Curiosity drew him from his warm hollow to the edge of the hay. Lying flat, he moved his head forward and peered down.

In the short-cropped meadow between the barn and the hedge stood a man and a woman, both young. The man was well-built and handsome, with a fine head of chestnut hair tied in a queue with a broad black satin bow. His clothes showed some cheap attempts at flashy embellishments, which didn't make a good first impression. His fashionably cut coat was made of faded plum-colored velvet, edged with silver lace that had long lost its shine. He wore ruffles, but without starch, they drooped like weeping willows over his fine, delicate hands. His breeches were plain black cloth, and his black stockings were cotton. These items were completely out of place with his magnificent coat. His sturdy, practical shoes were adorned with buckles made of cheap, dull paste. If not for his charming and innocent face, André-Louis would have immediately labeled him as a rogue who lived by his wits. As it was, he held his judgment, continuing his observation by studying the girl. It must be admitted from the start that this study greatly fascinated him. This was despite his bookish and studious nature, and despite his age, as he was not in the habit of spending time thinking about women.

The girl, for she was no more than that, perhaps twenty at most, possessed charms of face and figure that were almost perfect. She also had a sparkling liveliness and a grace of movement that André-Louis couldn't recall ever seeing combined in one person before. And her voice, that musical, silvery voice that had woken him, had its own irresistible charm in its exquisite modulations. He thought it would be irresistible even in the plainest woman. She wore a hooded mantle of green cloth. With the hood thrown back, her delicate head was fully revealed to him. The morning sun caught glints of gold in her light nut-brown hair, which hung in a cluster of curls around her oval face. Her complexion was so delicate he could only compare it to a rose petal. From that distance, he couldn't tell the color of her eyes, but he guessed them blue, admiring their sparkle beneath the fine, dark line of her eyebrows.

He couldn't have said why, but he felt annoyed to find her so intimate with this handsome young man. The fellow, it seemed, was partly dressed in a nobleman's cast-off clothes. André-Louis couldn't guess her social standing, but her speech, which reached him, was cultured in both tone and vocabulary. He strained to listen.

She was saying, "I will know no peace, Leandre, until we are safely married. Only then will I consider myself beyond his reach. Yet, if we marry without his consent, we'll only create trouble for ourselves. And I almost despair of ever getting his consent."

Clearly, André-Louis thought, her father was a sensible man. He saw through Monsieur Leandre's shabby finery and wouldn't be fooled by cheap paste buckles.

"My dear Climene," the young man replied, standing directly in front of her and holding both her hands. "You are wrong to lose hope. If I don't reveal to you the entire plan I've prepared to win your unnatural parent's consent, it's because I'm reluctant to spoil the surprise that awaits you. But place your faith in me, and in that clever friend I've mentioned, who should be here any moment."

What a pompous fool! Had he memorized that speech beforehand, or was he naturally a pedantic idiot who spoke in such a stiff, formal way? How could such a sweet flower waste her charms on such a conceited fellow? And what a ridiculous name the man had!

André-Louis thought this to himself from his hidden spot. Meanwhile, she was speaking.

"That is what my heart desires, Leandre, but I'm plagued by fears that your plan might be too late. I am to marry this horrible Marquis of Sbrufadelli today. He arrives by noon. He's coming to sign the contract, to make me the Marchioness of Sbrufadelli. Oh!" It was a cry of pain from her tender young heart. "The very name burns my lips. If it were mine, I could never utter it, never! The man is so detestable. Save me, Leandre. Save me! You are my only hope."

André-Louis felt a pang of disappointment. She didn't rise to the level he had expected of her. She was clearly influenced by her ridiculous lover's stiff manner. There was a terrible lack of sincerity in her words. They registered in his mind but left his heart untouched. Perhaps this was due to his dislike for Monsieur Leandre and the situation itself.

So her father was marrying her to a marquis! That meant she came from a noble family. And yet she was content to be with this dull young adventurer in his tarnished lace! It was, he supposed, exactly what one should expect from a sex that all philosophy had taught him to consider the craziest part of a crazy species.

"It shall never be!" Monsieur Leandre declared passionately. "Never! I swear it!" He shook his small fist at the blue sky, like Ajax defying Jupiter. "Ah, but here comes our clever friend..." André-Louis didn't catch the name, as Monsieur Leandre had just turned to face the gap in the hedge. "He will bring us news, I know."

André-Louis also looked towards the gap. Through it emerged a lean, slight man in a rusty cloak and a three-cornered hat pulled low over his nose to shade his face. When he soon took off his hat and made a sweeping bow to the young lovers, André-Louis admitted to himself that if he had been cursed with such a dejected face, he would have worn his hat in exactly that way, to hide as much of it as possible. If Monsieur Leandre seemed to be wearing, at least in part, a nobleman's hand-me-downs, the newcomer appeared to be wearing Monsieur Leandre's hand-me-downs. Yet despite his terrible clothes and even worse face, with its three-day growth of beard, the man carried himself with a certain style. He positively strutted as he approached, and he bowed with a courtly, practiced flourish.

He said, with the air of a conspirator, "Monsieur, the time for action has arrived. So has the Marquis. That is why."

The young lovers sprang apart in shock. Climene clasped her hands, her lips parted, and her chest rose and fell rapidly under her white fichu-menteur. Monsieur Leandre stood open-mouthed, the very picture of foolishness and dismay.

Meanwhile, the newcomer chattered on. "I was at the inn an hour ago when he arrived," he said. "I studied him carefully while he ate breakfast. After doing so, I have no doubt at all about our success. As for his appearance, I could tell you at length how nature has designed his utter foolishness. But that doesn't matter. We are concerned with who he is, with his intelligence. And I tell you confidently that I find him so dull and stupid that you can be sure he will fall headfirst into every single trap I have so cunningly prepared for him."

"Tell me, tell me! Speak!" Climene implored him, holding out her hands in a plea no sensitive man could have resisted. Then, in an instant, she gasped, letting out a faint scream. "My father!" she exclaimed, turning anxiously from one to the other of the two men. "He is coming! We are lost!"

"You must flee, Climene!" Monsieur Leandre said.

"Too late!" she sobbed. "Too late! He is here."

"Calm down, mademoiselle, calm down!" the clever friend urged her. "Keep calm and trust me. I promise you that everything will be fine."

"Oh!" cried Monsieur Leandre, weakly. "Say what you will, my friend, this is ruin. It's the end of all our hopes. Your cleverness will never get us out of this. Never!"

Through the gap now strode an enormous man. He had an inflamed, round face and a large nose, and was respectably dressed like a solid bourgeois. There was no mistaking his anger, but the way he expressed it was astonishing to André-Louis.

"Leandre, you're an idiot! Too much apathy, too much apathy! Your words wouldn't convince a farmhand! Have you even considered what they mean? Like this," he cried. He cast his round hat away with a broad gesture, then stood beside Monsieur Leandre. He repeated the very words Leandre had just spoken, while the three watched him calmly and carefully.

"Oh, say what you will, my friend, this is ruin. It's the end of all our hopes. Your cleverness will never get us out of this. Never!"

A frenzy of despair vibrated in his voice. He swung back to face Monsieur Leandre. "Like this," he told him contemptuously. "Let the passion of your hopelessness express itself in your voice. Remember, you're not asking Scaramouche here if he's patched your breeches. You are a despairing lover expressing..."

He stopped abruptly, startled. André-Louis suddenly realized what was happening and how he had been tricked. He burst out laughing. The sound of his laughter, echoing and booming strangely under the large roof that enclosed him, was startling to those below.

The fat man was the first to recover. He announced it in his usual way, with one of the quick sarcastic remarks he often used.

"Listen!" he cried. "The very gods are laughing at you, Leandre." Then he addressed the barn roof and its invisible occupant. "Hey! You up there!"

André-Louis revealed himself by sticking out his tousled head a bit more.

"Good morning," he said pleasantly. Rising to his knees, his view suddenly expanded to include the wide common beyond the hedge. There, he saw an enormous and very battered traveling carriage, a cart piled high with timbers partly visible under a sheet of oiled canvas, and a kind of house on wheels with a tin chimney, from which smoke slowly curled. Three heavy Flemish horses and a couple of donkeys, all hobbled, were contentedly grazing near these vehicles. If he had noticed them sooner, they would have given him a clue to the strange scene playing out below. Beyond the hedge, other figures were moving. At that moment, three crowded into the gap: a saucy-faced girl with a tip-tilted nose, whom he assumed to be Columbine, the soubrette; a lean, active youngster, who must be the lackey Harlequin; and another rather clumsy youth who might be a zany or an apothecary.

He took all this in with a single comprehensive glance, which took no longer than it had to say good morning. To his good morning, Pantaloon replied with a bellow:

"What the devil are you doing up there?"

"Precisely the same thing you are doing down there," was the answer. "I am trespassing."

"Eh?" said Pantaloon. He looked at his companions, some of the confidence draining from his big red face. Although they did this habitually, hearing it called by its proper name was unsettling.

"Whose land is this?" he asked, his confidence diminishing.

André-Louis answered, while pulling on his stockings. "I believe it belongs to the Marquis de La Tour d'Azyr."

"That's a high-sounding name. Is the gentleman strict?"

"The gentleman," André-Louis said, "is the devil. Or rather, upon reflection, I should prefer to say that the devil is a gentleman by comparison."

"And yet," the villainous-looking fellow who played Scaramouche interrupted, "by your own admission, you yourself don't hesitate to trespass on his property."

"Ah, but you see, I am a lawyer," André-Louis replied. "And lawyers are notoriously unable to observe the law, just as actors are notoriously unable to act. Moreover, sir, Nature imposes her limits on us. Nature conquers respect for law, just as she conquers all else. Nature conquered me last night when I had gotten this far. So I slept here without any regard for the very high and powerful Marquis de La Tour d'Azyr. At the same time, Monsieur Scaramouche, you'll notice that I didn't flaunt my trespass quite as openly as you and your companions."

After putting on his boots, André-Louis nimbly dropped to the ground in his shirt-sleeves, his riding coat draped over his arm. As he stood there to put it on, the little cunning eyes of the heavy father scrutinized him in detail. Monsieur Pantaloon observed that André-Louis's clothes, though plain, were well-made, that his shirt was of fine cambric, and that he spoke like a man of culture, as he claimed to be. Pantaloon was therefore inclined to be civil.

"I am very grateful to you for the warning, sir..." he began.

"Act on it, my friend. Monsieur d'Azyr's rural guards have orders to fire on trespassers. Do as I do, and leave quickly."

They followed him immediately through that gap in the hedge to the encampment on the common. André-Louis took his leave of them there. But as he was turning away, he noticed a young man from the company performing his morning wash at a bucket. The bucket was placed on one of the wooden steps at the back of the traveling house. He hesitated for a moment. Then he turned frankly to M. Pantaloon, who was still standing beside him.

“If it weren't too much to impose on your hospitality, monsieur,” he said, “I would ask if I might do the same as that excellent young gentleman before I leave you.”

“But, my dear sir!” Good nature radiated from every pore of the master player's plump body. “It's no trouble at all. Please, by all means. Rhodomont will provide what you require. He is the dandy of the company in real life, though a fierce character on the stage. Hi, Rhodomont!”

The young man who had been washing straightened his long body from the right angle in which it had been bent over the bucket. He looked out through a foam of soapsuds. Pantaloon gave an order, and Rhodomont, who was indeed as gentle and amiable off the stage as he was formidable and terrible upon it, offered the stranger the use of the bucket in the friendliest manner.

So André-Louis once more removed his cravat and his coat, and rolled up the sleeves of his fine shirt. Meanwhile, Rhodomont got him soap, a towel, and soon after, a broken comb, and even a greasy hair-ribbon, in case the gentleman had lost his own. André-Louis declined the ribbon, but he gratefully accepted the comb. After washing himself clean, he stood with the towel flung over his left shoulder. He began to restore order to his disheveled hair, looking into a broken piece of mirror attached to the door of the traveling house.

He was standing like this, with the gentle Rhodomont chatting aimlessly beside him, when he heard the sound of hooves. He looked over his shoulder carelessly, and then stood frozen, with his comb raised and mouth agape. Away across the common, on the road that bordered it, he saw a group of seven horsemen. They wore the blue coats with red facings of the *marechaussee*.

Not for a moment did he doubt what was the target of this patrolling *gendarmerie*. It was as if the chill shadow of the gallows had fallen suddenly upon him.

And then the troop halted, level with them, and the sergeant leading it shouted across the common.

“Hi, there! Hi!” His tone was menacing.

Every member of the company—and there were some twelve in all—stood staring. Pantaloon advanced a step or two, striding, his head thrown back, with the bearing of a King's Lieutenant.

“Now, what the devil's this?” he said, but it wasn't clear if he was addressing Fate, Heaven, or the sergeant.

There was a brief discussion among the horsemen, then they came trotting across the common straight towards the players’ encampment.

André-Louis had remained standing at the back of the traveling house. He was still passing the comb through his disheveled hair, but mechanically, without thinking. His mind was entirely focused on the approaching troop. His wits were sharp, ready to react in whatever way was needed.

Still in the distance, but evidently impatient, the sergeant shouted a question.

“Who gave you leave to encamp here?”

It was a question that didn't reassure André-Louis in the slightest. He wasn't fooled into thinking, or even hoping, that these men were just here to round up vagrants and trespassers. That wasn't their real duty. It was something they did in passing, perhaps hoping to collect their own unofficial tax. It was highly probable they were from Rennes, and their real business was hunting down a young lawyer accused of sedition. Meanwhile, Pantaloon was shouting back.

“Who gave us leave, do you say? What leave? This is communal land, free to all.”

The sergeant laughed unpleasantly, and came on, his troop following.

A voice at Pantaloon's elbow said, “There is no such thing as communal land in the true sense in all M. de La Tour d’Azyr’s vast domain. This is a *terre censive*, and his bailiffs collect his dues from anyone who sends their animals to graze here.”

Pantaloon turned to see André-Louis beside him in his shirt-sleeves, and without a cravat, with the towel still draped over his left shoulder, a comb in his hand, and his hair half-combed.

“Good heavens!” Pantaloon swore. “This Marquis de La Tour d’Azyr is an ogre!”

“I have told you already what I think of him,” André-Louis said. “As for these men, you'd better let me handle them. I have experience of their kind.” Without waiting for Pantaloon's consent, André-Louis stepped forward to meet the approaching *marechaussee*. He realized that only boldness could save him now.

When a moment later the sergeant pulled his horse to a stop beside this half-dressed young man, André-Louis combed his hair while he looked up with a half smile, a smile meant to be friendly, innocent, and disarming.

Despite this, the sergeant called out to him gruffly: “Are you the leader of this troop of vagabonds?”

“Yes... I mean, my father, there, is really the leader.” And he jerked a thumb toward M. Pantaloon, who stood staring in the background, out of earshot. “What is your pleasure, captain?”

“My pleasure is to tell you that you are very likely to be jailed for this, all of you.” His voice was loud and bullying. It carried across the common to the ears of every member of the company, and made them all stand at rigid attention where they were. The life of strolling players was hard enough without being thrown in jail.

“But why is that, Captain? This is communal land free to all.”

“It is nothing of the kind.”

“Where are the fences?” André-Louis said, waving the hand that held the comb, as if to point out how open the area was.

“Fences!” snorted the sergeant. “What do fences have to do with this? This is *terre censive*. There is no grazing here except by paying dues to the Marquis de La Tour d’Azyr.”

"But we are not grazing," André-Louis said innocently.

"To the devil with you, you fool! You are not grazing! But your animals are grazing!"

"They eat so little," André-Louis apologized, trying his ingratiating smile again.

The sergeant became even more menacing. "That's not the point. The point is, you're committing what amounts to theft. And thieves go to jail."

"Technically, I suppose you are right," André-Louis sighed. He started combing his hair again, still looking up at the sergeant's face. "But we have sinned out of ignorance. We are grateful to you for the warning." He moved the comb to his left hand. With his right, he fumbled in his breeches' pocket, and a faint jingle of coins came from within. "We are sorry to have inconvenienced you. Perhaps, for their trouble, your men would honor us by stopping at the next inn. They could drink to the health of Monsieur de La Tour d'Azyr, or anyone else they deem proper."

Some of the tension eased from the sergeant's face, but not all of it.

"Well, well," he said gruffly. "But you must pack up and leave, you understand." He leaned from the saddle to bring his outstretched hand to a convenient distance. André-Louis placed a three-livre coin in it.

"In half an an hour," André-Louis said.

"Why in half an hour? Why not at once?"

"Oh, but we need time to eat our breakfast."

They looked at each other. The sergeant then considered the large silver coin in his palm. Finally, his face relaxed.

"After all," he said, "it's not our job to act as Monsieur de La Tour d'Azyr's enforcers. We are of the marechaussee from Rennes." André-Louis's eyelids twitched, betraying him slightly. "But if you stay, look out for the Marquis's gardes-champetres. You'll find them not accommodating at all. Well, well—I wish you a good appetite, monsieur," he said in farewell.

"A pleasant ride, my captain," André-Louis answered.

The sergeant turned his horse around, and his troop turned with him. They were starting off when he stopped again.

"You, monsieur!" he called over his shoulder. André-Louis was beside his stirrup in a single bound. "We are looking for a scoundrel named André-Louis Moreau, from Gavrillac. He's a fugitive from justice, wanted for sedition, and facing the gallows. You've seen nothing, I suppose, of a man whose movements seemed suspicious to you?"

"Indeed, we have," André-Louis said boldly, his face eager to be helpful.

"You have?" cried the sergeant in a loud voice. "Where? When?"

"Yesterday evening, in the neighborhood of Guignen..."

"Yes, yes," the sergeant said, feeling close to finding him.

"There was a fellow who seemed very afraid of being recognized... a man of fifty or thereabouts..."

"Fifty!" cried the sergeant, and his face fell. "Rubbish! This man of ours is no older than yourself. He's a thin, wispy fellow, about your own height, with black hair, just like yours, according to the description. Keep an eye out during your travels, master player. The King's Lieutenant in Rennes sent us word this morning. He'll pay ten louis to anyone who gives information leading to this scoundrel's arrest. So, you could earn ten louis by keeping your eyes open and sending word to the nearest justices. That would be a nice windfall for you."

"A nice windfall, indeed, captain," André-Louis answered, laughing.

But the sergeant had spurred his horse and was already trotting away behind his men. André-Louis continued to laugh, quite silently, as he sometimes did when a joke was particularly sharp.

Then he turned slowly and came back towards Pantaloon and the rest of the company, who were now all grouped together, watching intently.

Pantaloon advanced to meet him with both hands outstretched. For a moment, André-Louis thought he was about to be embraced.

"We hail you our savior!" the big man announced dramatically. "Already the shadow of jail was creeping over us, chilling us to the very marrow. Even though we are poor, we are all honest folk. Not one of us has ever suffered the indignity of prison, and none of us would survive it. But for you, my friend, it might have happened. What magic did you perform?"

"The magic that works in France with a King's portrait. The French are a very loyal nation, as you will have observed. They love their King—and his portrait even better than himself, especially when it is made of gold. But even in silver, it is respected. The sergeant was so moved by the sight of that noble face—on a three-livre coin—that his anger vanished. He has gone his way, leaving us to depart in peace."

"Ah, true! He said we must pack up and leave. Let's get to it, my lads! Come on, come on..."

"But not until after breakfast," André-Louis said. "A half-hour for breakfast was granted to us by that loyal fellow, so deeply was he touched. True, he mentioned possible gardes-champetres. But he knows as well as I do that they are not to be seriously feared. If they came, again the King's portrait—made of copper this time—would produce the same softening effect on them. So, my dear Monsieur Pantaloon, eat your breakfast at your leisure. I can smell your cooking from here, and from the smell, I'd say there's no need to wish you a good appetite."

"My friend, my savior!" Pantaloon threw a big arm around the young man's shoulders. "You must stay and have breakfast with us."

"I admit I was hoping you would ask me," André-Louis said.
