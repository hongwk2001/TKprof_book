CHAPTER 7. THE SPADASSINICIDES

After an absence of just over a week, Monsieur le Marquis de La Tour d’Azyr was back in his seat on the Right side of the National Assembly. Strictly speaking, by this date, we should already refer to him as the former Marquis de La Tour d’Azyr. It was September of 1790, two months after the decree had passed. This decree, proposed by the outspoken Breton revolutionary Le Chapelier, stated that nobility should no more be hereditary than a criminal record. Just as the stain of the gallows must not tarnish the potentially worthy descendants of someone convicted of evil, neither should a coat of arms celebrating achievement glorify the potentially unworthy descendants of someone who had proven themselves good. So, the decree had been passed, abolishing hereditary nobility and relegating family crests to the rubbish heap of things an enlightened generation of philosophers would no longer tolerate. Monsieur le Comte de Lafayette, who had supported the motion, left the Assembly as plain Monsieur Motier. The great orator Count Mirabeau became plain Monsieur Riquetti. And Monsieur le Marquis de La Tour d’Azyr became simply Monsieur Lesarques. This change happened during one of those moments of fervor inspired by the approach of the great National Festival of the Champ de Mars. No doubt, many who supported it thoroughly regretted it the next day. Thus, although it was now law, it was a law that no one bothered to enforce just yet.

That, however, is beside the point. As I mentioned, it was September. The day was overcast and rainy, and some of its damp gloom seemed to fill the long Hall of the Manege. There, on eight rows of green benches arranged in ascending, elliptical tiers around the area known as La Piste, sat some eight or nine hundred representatives of the three estates that made up the nation.

Those drafting the constitution were debating several points. They discussed whether the next deliberative body, succeeding the Constituent Assembly, should collaborate with the King. They also debated if it should be temporary or permanent, and whether it should govern with two chambers or just one.

Abbé Maury, the son of a cobbler, was at the podium. In these contradictory times, he was the chief orator of the Right-wing party, known as the Blacks, who were fighting the losing battles of the privileged class. He seemed to be advocating for a two-chamber system, modeled after the English one. If anything, he was even more long-winded and tedious than usual. His arguments sounded more and more like a sermon, and the National Assembly's podium became increasingly like a pulpit. Conversely, the members became less and less like a congregation. They grew restless under his steady flow of pompous, wordy speech. The four ushers, dressed in black satin breeches with carefully powdered heads, wearing chains of office on their chests and gilded swords at their sides, circulated in the Piste, clapping their hands and hissing, all in vain.

"Silence! Take your seats!"

The president's intermittent ringing of the bell at his green-covered table, facing the podium, was equally futile. Abbé Maury had talked too long and had failed to interest the members for some time. Realizing it at last, he stopped, and the hum of conversation became general. Then it stopped abruptly. An expectant silence fell, with heads turning and necks craning. Even the group of secretaries at the round table below the president’s dais stirred from their usual indifference to watch this young man, who was approaching the Assembly's podium for the first time.

"Monsieur André-Louis Moreau, substitute deputy, replacing Emmanuel Lagron, deceased, for Ancenis in the Department of the Loire."

Monsieur de La Tour d’Azyr snapped out of the gloomy trance he had been in. The successor of the deputy he had slain would, in any event, be a source of grim interest to him. Imagine how that interest intensified when he heard the name. Looking across, he recognized in this André-Louis Moreau the young scoundrel who kept crossing his path, constantly exerting a profound, sinister influence against him. It made him regret sparing André-Louis's life that day at Gavrillac two years ago. That André-Louis should now step into Lagron's shoes seemed too fitting to be mere coincidence to Monsieur de La Tour d’Azyr. It felt like a direct challenge.

He looked at the young man with wonder rather than anger. As he watched him, a vague, almost premonitory uneasiness filled him.

Right from the start, the presence he perceived as a challenge would prove itself to be exactly that, in no uncertain terms.

André-Louis began, "I come before you as a substitute deputy to fill the place of one who was murdered some three weeks ago."

It was a challenging opening that instantly provoked an indignant uproar from the Blacks. André-Louis paused and looked at them, smiling a little. He was a remarkably self-confident young man.

"The gentlemen of the Right, Monsieur le President, do not appear to like my words. But that is not surprising. The gentlemen of the Right are famously averse to the truth."

This time, there was uproar. The members of the Left roared with laughter, while those of the Right roared menacingly. The ushers circulated faster than usual, becoming agitated. They clapped their hands and called for silence, but in vain.

The President rang his bell.

Above the general din came the voice of La Tour d’Azyr, who had half-risen from his seat. "Mountebank! This is not the theatre!"

"No, monsieur," came the answer, "it is becoming a hunting-ground for bully-swordsmen." The uproar grew.

The substitute deputy looked around and waited. Nearby, he saw the encouraging grin of Le Chapelier and the quiet, approving smile of Kersain, another Breton deputy he knew. A little farther off, he noticed Mirabeau's great head thrown back, his large eyes regarding him with a sort of wonder from under a frown. And over there, among that moving sea of faces, was the pale face of the Arras lawyer, Robespierre. Or rather, "de Robespierre," as the little snob now called himself, having adopted the aristocratic "de" as the right of a man of his standing in the country's councils. With his nose tilted up and his carefully curled hair, head tilted to one side, the deputy for Arras was observing André-Louis attentively. The horn-rimmed spectacles he used for reading were pushed up onto his pale forehead. He considered the speaker through a steady spy-glass, his thin-lipped mouth stretched a little in that tiger-cat smile that would later become so famous and feared.

Gradually, the uproar died down and diminished, so that at last the President could make himself heard. Leaning forward, he gravely addressed the young man at the podium:

"Monsieur, if you wish to be heard, I beg you not to be provocative in your language." Then, to the others, he added, "Gentlemen, if we are to proceed, I ask that you restrain your feelings until the substitute deputy has concluded his speech."

"I will try to obey, Monsieur le President, leaving provocation to the gentlemen of the Right. If the few words I have used so far have been provocative, I regret it. But it was necessary that I refer to the distinguished deputy whose place I come so unworthily to fill. And it was unavoidable that I refer to the event which has led to this sad necessity. Deputy Lagron was a man of remarkable nobility of mind. He was selfless, dutiful, and zealous, driven by the noble purpose of serving his electors and this Assembly. He possessed what his opponents would call a dangerous gift of eloquence."

La Tour d’Azyr writhed at the well-known phrase. It was his own phrase, the one he had used to explain his actions concerning Philippe de Vilmorin. From time to time, it had been thrown back at him with such vindictive menace.

And then the crisp voice of the witty Canales, the very rapier of the Privileged party, sharply interrupted the speaker’s momentary pause.

"Monsieur le President," he asked with great solemnity, "has the substitute deputy approached the podium to take part in the debate on the constitution of the legislative assemblies, or to pronounce a funeral oration for the late Deputy Lagron?"

This time, it was the Blacks who burst into laughter, until stopped by the substitute deputy.

"That laughter is obscene!" In this truly French fashion, he threw his glove in the face of Privilege, determined, you see, to take no half measures. The rippling laughter instantly died, quenched in speechless fury.

Solemnly, he proceeded.

"You all know how Lagron died. To refer to his death at all requires courage. To laugh while referring to it requires something I will not attempt to qualify. If I have referred to his death, it is because my own appearance among you seemed to make such a reference necessary. It is my duty to take up the burden he laid down. I do not pretend that I have Lagron's strength, courage, or wisdom. But I will bear that burden with every ounce of strength, courage, and wisdom I possess. And I trust, for the sake of those who might attempt it, that the methods used to silence that eloquent voice will not be used to silence mine."

A faint murmur of applause came from the Left, met by a splutter of contemptuous laughter from the Right.

“Rhodomont!” a voice called out to him.

He looked toward the voice. It came from the group of spadassins among the Blacks across the Piste. He smiled. His lips silently formed a reply: “No, my friend—Scaramouche. Scaramouche, the subtle, dangerous fellow who achieves his goals by winding paths.” Then, speaking aloud, he continued, “Mister President, some people here will not understand our purpose. We are assembled to create laws that will govern France fairly. We are here to lift France out of the swamp of bankruptcy, where she risks sinking. It seems some want not laws, but blood. I solemnly warn them that this blood will eventually choke them, if they do not learn in time to abandon force and let reason prevail.”

Again, something in that phrase stirred a memory in La Tour d’Azyr. He turned amid the fresh uproar to speak to his cousin Chabrillane, who sat beside him.

He said, “This bastard of Gavrillac’s is a daring rogue.”

Chabrillane looked at him with gleaming eyes, his face white with anger.

“Let him talk himself out,” Chabrillane said. “I don’t think he will be heard again after today. Leave this to me.”

La Tour could hardly have told you why, but he sank back in his seat with a sense of relief. He had been telling himself that this situation demanded action, a challenge he had to accept. But despite his rage, he felt a strange reluctance. This fellow, he supposed, had a way of unpleasantly reminding him of that young abbe, who had been killed in the garden behind the Breton arme at Gavrillac. It wasn't that Philippe de Vilmorin’s death weighed heavily on M. de La Tour d’Azyr’s conscience. He considered himself fully justified in his actions. It was simply that the whole memory, as it came back to him, painted an unpleasant picture: that distraught boy kneeling over the bleeding body of the friend he had loved, almost begging to be killed with him, calling the Marquis a murderer and a coward to provoke him.

Meanwhile, the deputy-suppleant had finally composed himself, moving past the subject of Lagron’s death. He was now speaking on the question under debate. He contributed nothing of value to it and urged nothing definite. His speech on the subject was very brief. This was merely a pretext, not the actual purpose for which he had ascended the tribune.

Later, as he left the hall at the end of the sitting, with Le Chapelier at his side, he found himself densely surrounded by deputies. They formed a kind of bodyguard. Most of them were Bretons, aiming to shield him from the provocations that his own bold words in the Assembly were sure to bring upon him. For a moment, the massive form of Mirabeau came up alongside him.

“Congratulations, M. Moreau,” the great man said. “You performed very well. They will want your blood, no doubt. But be discreet, monsieur, if I may presume to advise you. Do not allow yourself to be misled by any false sense of quixotry. Ignore their challenges. I do so myself. I place each challenger upon my list. There are some fifty there already, and there they will remain. Refuse them what they are pleased to call satisfaction, and all will be well.” André-Louis smiled and sighed.

“It requires courage,” the hypocrite said.

“Of course it does. But you appear to have plenty.”

“Hardly enough, perhaps. But I will do my best.”

They had come through the vestibule. It was lined with eager Blacks waiting for the young man who had insulted them so flagrantly from the rostrum. However, André-Louis’ bodyguard had prevented any of them from reaching him.

Now, emerging into the open, they were under the great awning at the head of the Carriere. This awning had been erected so carriages could reach the door under cover. Those in front of him dispersed a little. As he reached the edge of the awning, his front was entirely exposed for a moment. Outside, the rain was falling heavily, churning the ground into thick mud. For a moment, André-Louis, with Le Chapelier always at his side, hesitated to step out into the deluge.

The watchful Chabrillane had seen his chance. He took a detour that momentarily led him out into the rain, bringing him face to face with the overly daring young Breton. Rudely and violently, he thrust André-Louis back, as if to make room for himself under the shelter.

André-Louis was not for a second under any delusion about the man’s deliberate purpose. Neither were those who stood near him, who made a belated and ineffectual attempt to close around him. He was deeply disappointed. It was not Chabrillane he had been expecting. His disappointment showed on his face, but the arrogant Chevalier mistook it for something very different.

But if Chabrillane was the man assigned to deal with him, he would make the best of it.

“I think you are pushing against me, monsieur,” he said very civilly. Then, with his elbow and shoulder, he thrust M. de Chabrillane back into the rain.

“I desire to take shelter, monsieur,” the Chevalier demanded aggressively.

“You may do so without standing on my feet. I have a prejudice against anyone standing on my feet. My feet are very tender. Perhaps you did not know that, monsieur. Please say no more.”

“Why, I wasn’t speaking, you lout!” the Chevalier exclaimed, slightly flustered.

“Were you not? I thought perhaps you were about to apologize.”

“Apologize?” Chabrillane laughed. “To you! Do you know that you are amusing?” He stepped under the awning for the second time. Again, in full view of everyone, he rudely thrust André-Louis back.

“Ah!” cried André-Louis, with a grimace. “You hurt me, monsieur. I have told you not to push against me.” He raised his voice so that all might hear him. Once more, he pushed M. de Chabrillane back into the rain.

Now, despite his slenderness, his diligent daily sword practice had given André-Louis an arm of iron. He also threw his weight into the thrust. His assailant reeled backward a few steps. Then his heel struck a beam of timber left on the ground by some workmen that morning, and he suddenly sat down in the mud.

A roar of laughter rose from all who witnessed the fine gentleman’s downfall. He rose, mud-bespattered and in a fury. In that fury, he sprang at André-Louis.

André-Louis had made him ridiculous, which was completely unforgivable.

He spluttered, "You shall meet me for this! I shall kill you for it."

His face, red with anger, was just a foot from André-Louis's. André-Louis laughed. Everyone heard his laugh in the silence, and then the words that followed.

"Oh, is that what you wanted?" André-Louis asked. "But why didn't you say so before? You would have saved me the trouble of knocking you down. I thought gentlemen in your profession always handled these matters with decency, decorum, and a certain grace. If you had, you might have saved your breeches."

Chabrillane snapped, his face white with genuine fury, "How soon shall we settle this?"

"Whenever you please, monsieur," André-Louis replied. "It's for you to say when it suits your convenience to kill me. I believe that was the intention you announced, was it not?" André-Louis was perfectly calm.

"Tomorrow morning, in the Bois," Chabrillane said. "Perhaps you will bring a friend."

"Certainly, monsieur," André-Louis agreed. "Tomorrow morning, then. I hope we have fine weather. I hate the rain."

Chabrillane looked at him almost with amazement. André-Louis smiled pleasantly.

"I won't keep you any longer now, monsieur," André-Louis said. "We understand each other perfectly. I'll be in the Bois at nine o'clock tomorrow morning."

"That is too late for me, monsieur," Chabrillane stated.

"Any other hour would be too early for me," André-Louis countered. "I don't like to have my habits disturbed. Nine o'clock or not at all, as you please."

"But I must be at the Assembly at nine, for the morning session," Chabrillane protested.

"I'm afraid, monsieur, you will have to kill me first," André-Louis said. "And I have a prejudice against being killed before nine o'clock."

This was too much of a reversal of the usual procedure for Monsieur de Chabrillane to stomach. Here was a country deputy using the exact same tone of sinister mockery with him that his own class usually used against their victims from the Third Estate. To make the irritation worse, André-Louis—always the actor, Scaramouche—produced his snuffbox. He offered it with a steady hand to Le Chapelier before helping himself.

After all he had suffered, it seemed Chabrillane wasn't even going to be allowed to leave with dignity.

He said, "Very well, monsieur. Nine o'clock, then. And we'll see if you'll talk as pertly afterwards."

On that, he stormed off, facing the jeers of the provincial deputies. His rage wasn't soothed by the street children laughing at him all the way down the Rue Dauphine. They laughed because of the mud and filth dripping from his satin breeches and the tails of his elegant, striped coat.

The members of the Third Estate had jeered outwardly. But inwardly, they trembled with fear and indignation. This was too much. Lagron had been killed by one of these thugs. Now, his successor was challenged, and about to be killed by another of them. This was happening on his very first day, as he arrived to take the dead man's place. Several people came to beg André-Louis not to go to the Bois. They urged him to ignore the challenge and the whole affair, calling it a deliberate attempt to eliminate him. He listened seriously, shook his head sadly, and finally promised to think it over.

He was in his seat again for the afternoon session, as if nothing disturbed him.

But in the morning, when the Assembly met, his place was vacant. So was Monsieur de Chabrillane's. Gloom and resentment settled over the members of the Third Estate, bringing a more bitter tone than usual to their debates. They disapproved of the recklessness of this new member. Some openly condemned his lack of caution. Very few—only the small group close to Le Chapelier—ever expected to see him again.

So, a few minutes after ten, they saw him enter. He was calm, composed, and bland. He made his way to his seat, and everyone felt both amazement and relief. The speaker at the podium, a member of the Privileged Class, stopped short. He stared in disbelief and dismay. This was something he couldn't understand at all. Then, from somewhere, a voice contemptuously explained the situation. It seemed to satisfy the amazement on both sides of the assembly.

"They haven't met," the voice said. "He shirked it at the last moment."

"It must be so," everyone thought. The confusion ended, and men were settling back into their seats. But now, André-Louis had reached his place. He had heard the voice that explained the matter to everyone's satisfaction. He paused before taking his seat. He felt it was his duty to reveal the true facts.

"Monsieur le President, my apologies for my late arrival." There was no real need for this. It was just a theatrical gesture, one that Scaramouche, being an actor, couldn't resist. "I have been delayed by an urgent appointment. I also bring you Monsieur de Chabrillane's apologies. He, unfortunately, will be permanently absent from this Assembly from now on."

The silence was complete. André-Louis sat down.
