Chapter 12. THE OVERWHELMING REASON

Monsieur de La Tour d'Azyr was seen no more at the Manege. In fact, he disappeared from Paris entirely throughout the months the National Assembly remained in session to draft France's new constitution. Although his physical wound from the duel had been relatively minor, the blow to his immense pride was practically fatal.

Rumors circulated that he had emigrated, but that was only half the truth. He had actually joined a group of noble travelers who constantly moved between the Tuileries Palace and the emigres' headquarters at Coblenz. In short, he became an agent for the royalist secret service, the very organization that would ultimately bring the monarchy crashing down in ruins.

As for André-Louis, he never returned to his godfather’s house. He remained convinced that Monsieur de Kercadiou would stand by his written vow to cut all ties if the duel ever took place.

Instead, André-Louis threw himself into his duties at the Assembly with incredible zeal. When the Constituent Assembly finally dissolved in September of the following year, he was immediately thrust into membership of the newly elected Legislative Assembly.

Like many others, he believed the Revolution was now complete. He thought France simply needed to govern herself under the new Constitution and everything would be fine. And it might have been, if the Court had actually accepted this new reality. Instead, their constant scheming led half of Europe to arm itself against France. The King's quarrel was now a direct war against his own people. That betrayal was the horrific root of all the horrors yet to come.

Counter-revolutionary unrest, heavily stirred up by the clergy, was worse in Brittany than anywhere else. Hoping to leverage his influence in his native province, the Commission of Twelve asked André-Louis to go there and calm the tensions during the early days of the Girondin ministry. He was instructed to keep things peaceful, but his authority was practically absolute. His written orders commanded everyone to assist him and warned that anyone who hindered his work would do so at their own peril.

He accepted the task and traveled west in the spring of 1792 as one of five special envoys. The mission kept him away from Paris for four months. He might have stayed even longer, but he was suddenly recalled in early August. A crisis brewing in Paris itself had become far more urgent than the troubles in Brittany. The political climate was darker than it had been since 1789. Paris knew the final showdown between Equality and Privilege was rapidly approaching. André-Louis sped back from the West straight into this volatile city, where the climax of his own chaotic career was waiting.

Aline de Kercadiou was also in Paris during those early days of August. She was visiting her uncle’s cousin and dearest friend, Madame de Plougastel. The brewing unrest that signaled an incoming explosion couldn't have been more obvious. However, the cheerful, even joking, atmosphere at Court reassured them, as the two women visited almost daily. 

Monsieur de Plougastel had briefly returned before leaving again for Coblenz on the secret business that kept him away from his wife. But while he was home, he confidently assured her that all necessary precautions were in place. He insisted an uprising should actually be welcomed, because it could only end one way: with the final, crushing defeat of the Revolution in the courtyard of the Tuileries. That, he added, was exactly why the King remained in Paris. If the King wasn't entirely confident, he would have surrounded himself with his Swiss Guards and his knights of the dagger to flee the capital. They could easily carve a path out for him if anyone tried to stop them. But he believed even that wouldn't be necessary.

Yet, as August began and her husband left again, Madame de Plougastel watched events unfold and felt his confident words lose their power. Finally, on the afternoon of August 9th, a messenger arrived at the Hotel Plougastel from Meudon. He brought an urgent note from Monsieur de Kercadiou, begging his niece to join him immediately and advising her hostess to come along.

Monsieur de Kercadiou was the kind of man who made friends across all social classes. His ancient bloodline made him an equal among the noblesse, while his simple, somewhat rustic manners and natural warmth made him equally popular with commoners. The simple folk of Meudon knew and respected him deeply. It was Rougane, the town's friendly mayor, who learned about the storm brewing for the next day. Knowing Aline was in Paris, he warned Monsieur de Kercadiou to get her out of the city. He predicted that within twenty-four hours, Paris would become a deadly trap for anyone of noble birth, especially those suspected of having ties to the Court.

There was absolutely no doubt about Madame de Plougastel’s connections to the Court. The vigilant secret societies watching over the fragile revolution knew all about her husband's frequent trips to Coblenz. They had no illusions about why he was going. If the Court party lost the coming battle, Madame de Plougastel would be in terrible danger. Any noble guest staying at her home would share that risk.

Monsieur de Kercadiou’s deep affection for both women made him take Rougane’s warning very seriously. That was why he hastily sent the note, demanding his niece and pleading with his friend to flee to Meudon at once.

The helpful mayor even sent his own nineteen-year-old son to deliver the letter to Paris. It was late afternoon on a perfect August day when young Rougane arrived at the Hotel Plougastel. 

Madame de Plougastel received him graciously in the grand salon. The sheer luxury of the room, combined with the lady's commanding presence, completely overwhelmed the simple young man. Madame made her decision instantly. 

Monsieur de Kercadiou’s urgent warning only confirmed her own growing fears. She decided they would leave immediately.

"Very well, Madame," the young man said. "Then I have the honor to take my leave."

But she refused to let him go. She insisted he visit the kitchen for refreshments while she and Aline packed, and then offered him a seat in her carriage back to Meudon. She wouldn't dream of letting him walk all the way back. 

It was the least she could do, but her ability to think of someone else's comfort during such a stressful moment would soon save her. If she had been less kind, she would have suffered hours of even greater agony than what was already waiting for her.

About half an hour before sunset, their carriage set off toward the Porte Saint-Martin to leave Paris. They traveled with a single footman riding on the back. Young Rougane was terrified by the honor of sitting inside with the ladies. He promptly fell in love with Aline de Kercadiou, thinking she was the most beautiful creature he had ever seen. Yet she spoke to him simply and naturally, as if they were equals. The experience went to his head, shaking up the firm republican beliefs he thought he had fully embraced.

The carriage rolled up to the barrier but was stopped by a picket of the National Guard stationed at the iron gates. The commanding sergeant marched up to the door. The Countess leaned out the window.

"The barrier is closed, Madame," he told her sharply.

"Closed!" she repeated, stunned. "But... do you mean we cannot pass?"

"Not unless you have a permit, Madame." The sergeant leaned casually on his pike. "Our orders are that no one enters or leaves without proper papers."

"Whose orders?"

"Orders from the Commune of Paris."

"But I must go to the country this evening," Madame insisted, her voice edging on petulant. "I am expected."

"In that case, Madame should procure a permit."

"Where can I get one?"

"At the Hotel de Ville, or at the headquarters of your local section."

She thought for a moment. "To the section, then. Please tell my coachman to drive to the Bondy Section."

He saluted and stepped back. "Section Bondy, Rue des Morts," he called to the driver.

Madame sank back into her seat, highly agitated, and Aline looked just as worried. Rougane tried his best to calm them. He promised the section would sort it out immediately. They would definitely get a permit, what possible reason could the guards have to refuse them? It was just a minor formality!

His confident words lifted their spirits, which only made their crushing disappointment worse when the president of the section flatly rejected their request. 

"Your name, Madame?" he asked abruptly. He was a rude, radical republican who hadn't even bothered to stand up when the ladies entered the room. He made it clear he was there to do his job, not to teach etiquette. 

"Plougastel," he repeated after her, intentionally dropping her title as if she were a baker or a butcher. He pulled a heavy directory from a shelf, flipped through the pages, and found her entry. "Count de Plougastel, Hotel Plougastel, Rue du Paradis. Is that correct?"

"That is correct, Monsieur," she replied, staying as polite as possible despite his obvious disrespect.

A long silence followed as he studied some penciled notes scribbled next to the name. The local sections had been investigating their residents much more thoroughly than anyone realized.

"Is your husband with you, Madame?" he asked sharply, keeping his eyes on the book.

"Monsieur the Count is not with me," she answered, emphasizing his title.

"Not with you?" He looked up, shooting her a glance that was equal parts suspicious and mocking. "Where is he?"

"He is not in Paris, Monsieur."

"Ah! Is he at Coblenz, perhaps?"

Madame's blood ran cold. This felt like a trap. Why had the sections tracked everyone's movements so closely? What was about to happen? She felt like a fish caught in an invisible net.

"I do not know, Monsieur," she said, her voice shaking slightly.

"Of course not." He practically sneered. "No matter. And you wish to leave Paris as well? Where do you want to go?"

"To Meudon."

"And your business there?"

Her face flushed hotly. His insolence was agonizing for a woman who had spent her entire life being treated with total deference by everyone she met. Still, she realized she was dealing with a dangerous new kind of power. She swallowed her anger and kept her voice steady.

"I wish to escort this lady, Mademoiselle de Kercadiou, back to her uncle who lives there."

"Is that all? You can do that another day, Madame. This isn't an emergency."

"Pardon me, Monsieur, but it is extremely urgent to us."

"You haven't convinced me. The barriers are closed to everyone who cannot prove an absolute, urgent necessity to pass. You will wait, Madame, until the restriction is lifted. Good evening."

"But, Monsieur..."

"Good evening, Madame," he repeated. It was a dismissal far more arrogant and absolute than any royal command to leave.

Madame and Aline walked out, trembling with suppressed rage. They climbed back into the coach and asked to be driven home. 

Rougane was just as shocked as they were. "Why not try the Hotel de Ville, Madame?" he suggested.

"After that? It would be pointless. We just have to accept that we are stuck in Paris until they open the gates."

"Maybe it won't matter either way by then, Madame," Aline said quietly.

"Aline!" Madame gasped in horror.

"Mademoiselle!" Rougane echoed. He suddenly realized that keeping these women trapped in the city meant some terrible, unseen danger was approaching. He quickly put his mind to work. As they neared the Hotel Plougastel, he announced he had a solution.

"A passport issued from outside the city would work just as well," he said. "Listen to me and trust me. I will go back to Meudon right now. I'll have my father write two permits, one just for me, and one for three people traveling from Meudon to Paris and back. I’ll re-enter Paris using my solo permit, tear it up, and then the three of us will leave together using the group permit. We’ll just say we came from Meudon earlier today. It’s actually quite simple. If I leave right now, I'll be back tonight."

"But how will you get out?" Aline asked.

"Me? Oh, don't worry about that. My father is the Mayor of Meudon. I know plenty of people. I'll go to the Hotel de Ville and tell them the truth: I got trapped in Paris when the gates closed. My father expects me home tonight. They’ll let me through. It's perfectly simple."

His confidence brought their hopes back up. The plan sounded foolproof.

"Then please make the passport for four people, my friend," Madame requested. "There is Jacques," she added, pointing to the footman who had just helped them out of the carriage.

Rougane left, promising a quick return, and left them waiting with renewed hope. But hours dragged by. Night fell, bedtime passed, and there was still no sign of him.

They waited until midnight. Both women pretended to be completely confident just to comfort the other, though both felt a dark sense of dread creeping in. They played tric-trac in the grand salon to pass the time, acting as if they didn't have a single care in the world.

Finally, as the clock struck midnight, Madame sighed and stood up.

"It will have to wait until tomorrow morning," she said, even though she didn't believe it.

"Of course," Aline agreed. "He couldn't possibly have made it back tonight. And traveling tomorrow will be much better anyway. A late-night journey would be far too exhausting for you, dear Madame."

They kept up the charade.

Early the next morning, they were jolted awake by the violent clanging of bells, the tocsins of the local sections ringing the alarm. Soon after, they heard the heavy roll of drums and the thundering footsteps of a massive crowd on the march. Paris was rising up. Later, the sharp crack of muskets echoed in the distance, followed by the heavy, booming roar of cannons. The battle between the armed citizens and the Court had begun. The people had attacked the Tuileries Palace. 

Wild rumors flew across the city, and the servants soon brought terrifying news to the Hotel Plougastel. A brutal battle for the palace was raging. It would ultimately end in the senseless massacre of everyone the weak-willed King abandoned when he fled to the Assembly for protection. Indecisive to the bitter end, he only ever listened to terrible advice. He prepared his guards to fight. The moment the actual fighting started, he ordered a surrender, leaving the loyal men who stayed behind at the complete mercy of a bloodthirsty mob.

While the Tuileries burned, the two women at the Hotel Plougastel kept waiting for Rougane, though their hope was fading fast. And Rougane never returned. His father did not think the plan was nearly as simple as his son did. The elder Rougane was rightfully terrified of committing fraud against the revolutionary government.

He took his son to see Monsieur de Kercadiou, explaining what the boy had proposed and why he absolutely refused to do it.

Monsieur de Kercadiou begged him, pleaded with him, and even tried to bribe him. But the mayor wouldn't budge.

"Monsieur," Rougane said firmly. "If they catch me, and they absolutely will, I will hang for it. Beyond that, as much as I want to help you, forging documents is a breach of public trust that I simply cannot commit. Please do not ask me to do this."

"But what do you think is going to happen in there?" the half-crazed nobleman demanded.

"It is war," said Rougane, who was well aware of the political situation. "Open war between the people and the Court. I am heartbroken that my warning arrived too late. But honestly, I don't think you need to panic. They won't wage war on women."

Monsieur de Kercadiou clung desperately to that thought after the mayor and his son left. But in the back of his mind, he remembered exactly what kind of treasonous work Monsieur de Plougastel was doing. What if the revolutionaries knew about it? They almost certainly did. Throughout history, women had been punished for the political crimes of their husbands. Anything could happen in a violent popular uprising, and Aline was trapped right next to Madame de Plougastel.

Late that night, Monsieur de Kercadiou sat alone in his brother’s gloomy library. The pipe he had lit for comfort had gone out in his fingers. Suddenly, a sharp knock echoed from the front door.

Old Benoit, the seneschal of Gavrillac, opened the door to find a slim young man standing on the threshold. He wore a dark olive surcoat that fell to his calves, leather boots, buckskin breeches, and a small-sword at his hip. A tricolor sash was tied around his waist, and a tricolor cockade sat in his hat. He looked like an official of the new government, a terrifying sight for an old feudal servant who shared all of his master's worst fears.

"Can I help you, Monsieur?" Benoit asked, caught between respect and deep suspicion.

Then a crisp, familiar voice shocked him.

"Why, Benoit! For heaven's sake! Have you completely forgotten me?"

With a trembling hand, the old man raised his lantern to illuminate the visitor's lean face and wide mouth.

"Monsieur André!" he cried out. "Monsieur André!" He stared at the revolutionary sash and cockade, totally confused.

But André-Louis just stepped past him into the wide vestibule with its black-and-white marble floor. 

"If my godfather is still awake, take me to him. If he's asleep, wake him up and take me to him anyway."

"Oh, right away, Monsieur André, I know he will be absolutely thrilled to see you. No, he hasn't gone to bed yet. Right this way, please."

André-Louis had arrived in Meudon just half an hour earlier. He had gone straight to the mayor to find out what was happening in Paris, hoping to either confirm or dismiss the terrifying rumors he had heard on the road. Rougane told him the uprising was imminent, the sections had seized the gates, and no one could enter or leave the city without special clearance. 

André-Louis had nodded gravely. For a long time, he had feared this exact scenario: a second revolution rising up to destroy everything the first one had built, handing power over to violent extremists who would plunge France into anarchy. His worst fear was happening right now. He decided he would push on to Paris that very night to see the chaos for himself.

Just as he was leaving, he had turned back to ask if Monsieur de Kercadiou was still living in Meudon.

"You know him, Monsieur?" Rougane asked.

"He is my godfather."

"Your godfather! And you are a national representative! Why, you might be exactly the man he needs right now." Rougane quickly explained his son’s failed rescue mission that afternoon. 

André-Louis needed to hear nothing more. The fact that his godfather had banished him two years ago meant nothing now. He left his carriage at the local inn and walked straight to the house.

When the exhausted, terrified Monsieur de Kercadiou saw his estranged godson suddenly appear in his study, he greeted him with the exact same words he had used when he banished him.

"What do you want here, sir?"

"To help you if I can, my godfather," André-Louis replied gently.

But Monsieur de Kercadiou was not easily softened. "You stayed away for so long, I was hoping you would never bother me again."

"I wouldn't have dared to disobey your orders if I didn't think I could help. I just spoke with Mayor Rougane..."

"What do you mean, you didn't dare to disobey?"

"You banished me from your house, Monsieur."

Monsieur de Kercadiou stared at him, totally lost. "And that is why you haven't come to see me all this time?"

"Of course. Why else?"

Monsieur de Kercadiou kept staring. Then he cursed under his breath. It was infuriating to deal with a man who took everything so painfully literally. He had assumed André-Louis would eventually come back, apologize for his behavior, and beg for forgiveness. He said as much.

"But how could I assume you didn't mean exactly what you said, Monsieur? You were incredibly clear. What good would an apology do if I wasn't going to change my ways? And I had no intention of changing. But we might actually be thankful for that now."

"Thankful?"

"I am a representative of the Assembly. I have significant authority. And I am heading back to Paris at the perfect time. Can I do what Rougane refused to do? From what I suspect, the situation is incredibly urgent. Aline needs to be brought to safety immediately."

Monsieur de Kercadiou surrendered instantly. He walked over and grabbed André-Louis’ hand.

"My boy," he said, clearly emotional. "You have a certain nobility that no one can deny. If I was harsh with you back then, it was only because I was trying to stop you from going down the wrong path. I wanted to keep you out of the toxic politics that have dragged our poor country into this nightmare. Enemies on the borders, civil war breaking out at home. This is what you revolutionaries have achieved."

André-Louis didn't bother arguing. He got straight to the point.

"What about Aline?" he asked, answering his own question before the old man could. "She's in Paris, and we have to get her out before the city turns into a slaughterhouse. Once all the anger they've been brewing is finally unleashed, it's going to be a bloodbath. Young Rougane’s plan is actually very good. At least, I can't think of a better one."

"But Mayor Rougane absolutely refused to do it."

"He refused to do it on his own authority. But he agreed to do it on mine. I gave him a signed document stating that he issued the safe-conduct pass for Mademoiselle de Kercadiou under my direct official orders. My credentials gave him enough legal cover to obey me. He will only use that document if he gets investigated, just to protect himself. In exchange, he gave me this safe-conduct pass."

"You already have it?"

Monsieur de Kercadiou took the paper with a trembling hand. He held it close to the cluster of candles on the table and squinted his poor eyes to read it.

"If you send that to Paris with young Rougane in the morning," André-Louis explained, "Aline should be back here by noon. We obviously can't do anything tonight without looking suspicious. It's too late. And now, Monsieur my godfather, you know exactly why I broke your rules and came here. If there is anything else I can do for you while I am here, just say the word."

"There is, André. Didn't Rougane tell you there were others..."

"He mentioned Madame de Plougastel and her servant."

"Then why didn't you..." Monsieur de Kercadiou stopped, his face full of questions.

André-Louis shook his head very solemnly. "That is impossible," he said.

Monsieur de Kercadiou’s jaw dropped. "Impossible!" he repeated. "But why?"

"Monsieur, I can forge documents for Aline without feeling guilty. And frankly, for Aline, I'd ignore my conscience and do it anyway. But Madame de Plougastel is a completely different situation. Neither Aline nor her family have been plotting against the revolution, which is the real reason this whole country is about to explode. I can get Aline out of Paris with a clear conscience, knowing she hasn't done anything wrong and no one will come looking for her. But Madame de Plougastel is married to the Count de Plougastel. The whole world knows he is a spy brokering deals between the King and the emigres."

"That isn't her fault!" Monsieur de Kercadiou cried out in panic.

"I agree. But she could be arrested at any moment to prove she isn't involved in his treason. The authorities know she was in Paris today. If they look for her tomorrow and find out she escaped, there will definitely be an investigation. They will find out I abused my power and betrayed my office for personal reasons. I hope you understand, Monsieur, that the risk is simply too massive to take for a stranger."

"A stranger?" the Lord of Gavrillac asked, sounding deeply hurt.

"She is practically a stranger to me," André-Louis replied.

"But she is not a stranger to me, André. She is my cousin and a very dear, deeply valued friend. And dear God, everything you just said only proves how urgent it is to get her out! She has to be rescued, André, whatever the cost, she must be rescued! Her situation is infinitely more dangerous than Aline’s!"

The old nobleman stood pleading before his godson. He looked nothing like the stern man who had greeted him earlier. His face was ash-white, his hands were shaking, and sweat was pooling on his forehead.

"Monsieur my godfather, I would do anything within reason. But I cannot do this. Rescuing her could destroy me, you, and Aline."

"We have to take the risk."

"You have the right to risk your own life, of course."

"And yours, believe me, André, yours too!" He stepped close to the young man. "André, I am begging you to trust me and get that permit for Madame de Plougastel."

André stared at him, totally baffled. "This is absurd," he said. "I appreciate that she was kind to me for a few days when I was a child. I remember she tried to convert me to her royalist politics recently in Paris. But I am not risking my neck for her, and I won't risk yours or Aline's either."

"Ah! But, André..."

"That is my final answer, Monsieur. It is getting late, and I want to sleep in Paris tonight."

"No, no! Wait!" The Lord of Gavrillac was visibly falling apart. "André, you must!"

His frantic, desperate insistence was so bizarre that André-Louis finally realized there had to be some dark, massive secret behind it.

"I must?" he echoed. "Why must I? Give me a reason, Monsieur."

"André, my reasons are overwhelming."

"Please let me be the judge of that," André-Louis said, his voice turning commanding.

The demand seemed to push Monsieur de Kercadiou into absolute despair. He paced the room, wringing his hands behind his back, his face tight with anguish. Finally, he stopped in front of his godson.

"Can't you just take my word that the reasons exist?" he pleaded, sounding shattered.

"On a matter that could literally get me executed? No, Monsieur, that is not reasonable."

"I will break my word of honor, my sacred oath, if I tell you." Monsieur de Kercadiou turned away again, wringing his hands, looking utterly pathetic. Then he turned back. "But in this desperate emergency, since you are being so cruelly stubborn, I have to tell you. God help me, I have no choice. She will understand when she knows why I did it. André, my boy..." He stopped, looking terrified. He reached out and put a trembling hand on his godson’s shoulder. To André-Louis' absolute shock, he saw tears welling up in the old man's pale, short-sighted eyes. 

"Madame de Plougastel is your mother."

A long, heavy silence filled the room. The words didn't make sense at first. When the reality finally crashed down on him, André-Louis’ first instinct was to scream. But he forced himself to stay perfectly still and play the stoic philosopher. He always had to play a role. That was just who he was. And even in this world-shattering moment, he stayed true to his nature. He stayed quiet until his bizarre theatrical instincts kicked in, allowing him to speak without a shred of emotion. 

"I see," he said finally, his voice ice-cold.

His mind was racing back through the past. He quickly replayed every memory of Madame de Plougastel: her strange, random bursts of interest in him, the weird mix of deep affection and sad longing she always showed him. Suddenly, everything that had confused him for years made perfect sense.

"I see," he repeated. Then he added, "Honestly, anyone but a complete idiot would have figured it out years ago."

It was Monsieur de Kercadiou who cried out. He flinched backward as if he had been punched.

"My God, André, what is wrong with you? How can you hear something like this and act so completely heartless?"

"How am I supposed to act? Should I be shocked to learn that I have a mother? After all, a mother is fairly essential if you want to be born."

He sat down abruptly to hide the fact that his legs were shaking uncontrollably. He pulled a handkerchief from his pocket and wiped his face, which was suddenly dripping with cold sweat. And then, without warning, he realized he was crying.

When Monsieur de Kercadiou saw the silent tears streaming down the young man's pale face, he rushed to his side. He sat down next to him and threw a heavy, loving arm around his shoulders.

"André, my poor boy," he whispered. "I... I was stupid enough to think you didn't have a heart. You fooled me with your damned act, but now I see... I see..." He didn't seem sure what exactly he saw, or maybe he just didn't know how to say it.

"It's nothing, Monsieur. I am just exhausted, and... and I have a cold." But he couldn't keep up the act anymore. He dropped the mask completely. "Why... why all this secrecy?" he asked, his voice breaking. "Were you going to let me die without ever knowing?"

"Yes, André. We... we had to, to keep her safe."

"But why? Finish the story, sir. You can't just stop there. You've told me this much, you have to tell me the rest."

"The truth, my boy, is that you were born about three years after your mother married Monsieur de Plougastel. But her husband had been away with the army for eighteen months, and you were born four months before he came back. He has never suspected a thing, and for the sake of the family, he must never find out. That is why we had to keep it an absolute secret. That is why no one was ever allowed to know. Your mother came out to Brittany in secret and spent a few months hiding in the village of Moreau under a fake name. That is where you were born."

André-Louis processed the information. He wiped his tears and sat up, stiff and composed. 

"When you say no one was ever allowed to know, you are telling me, of course, that you, Monsieur..."

"Oh, good God, no!" The old man exploded. He jumped up, literally pushed away from André by the shock of the accusation. He looked completely horrified by the idea. "I was the only other person who knew. But it isn't what you think, André! Do you really think I would lie to you? Do you think I would deny you if you were my own son?"

"If you say I am not, Monsieur, that is all I need to hear."

"You are not. I was Therese’s cousin, and she knew I was her best friend. She knew she could trust me, so she came to me when she was desperate. Years ago, I actually wanted to marry her. But of course, I am not the kind of man women fall in love with. She trusted my love for her, and I have kept her secret safe ever since."

"Then who was my father?"

"I don't know. She never told me. It was her secret, and I didn't want to pry. That just isn't who I am, André."

André-Louis stood up and looked Monsieur de Kercadiou straight in the eye.

"You believe me, André?"

"Of course I do, Monsieur. And I am sorry. I am truly sorry that I am not your son."

Monsieur de Kercadiou squeezed his godson’s hand tightly, holding it in total silence for a long moment. Finally, he let go.

"And now, what will you do, André?" he asked quietly. "Now that you know?"

André-Louis stood still for a moment, thinking it over, and then suddenly burst out laughing. The whole situation was absurdly funny to him. He explained why.

"Does knowing this actually change anything? Does finding out we are related magically create a bond of deep, devoted love? Am I supposed to risk getting my head chopped off for a mother who was so careful about her reputation that she planned to never even tell me who she was? I only found out because of a random, desperate roll of the dice. Should that really change my mind?"

"The decision is yours, André."

"No, it isn't. Only a god could decide something like this. I certainly can't."

"You mean you are still refusing to do it?"

"I mean I accept. Since I can't logically decide what the right thing to do is, I have no choice but to do what a son is supposed to do. It's completely grotesque, but then again, life is grotesque."

"You will never, ever regret this."

"I certainly hope not," André replied. "But I think it's highly likely that I will. Now, I need to go see Rougane right now and get those other two permits. Then I suppose I should just take them into Paris myself in the morning. If you have a bed for me, Monsieur, I would really appreciate it. I... I admit, I am completely exhausted tonight."
