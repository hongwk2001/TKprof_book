Chapter 15: Safe-Conduct

Across the body of the sobbing woman, who was the mother of one and the former mistress of the other, the eyes of the two mortal enemies met. Their eyes were filled with a startled and appalled interest that went beyond words.

Aline stood on the other side of the table. She seemed turned to stone by this final, horrifying revelation.

The Marquis de La Tour d'Azyr was the first to move. His bewildered mind recalled what Madame de Plougastel had said about a letter on the table. He stepped forward, and no one stopped him. Now that the announcement was made, Madame de Plougastel no longer feared the consequences, so she let him pass. He walked unsteadily past his newly discovered son and picked up the sheet of paper lying next to the candlestick. He stood there for a long moment reading it, while everyone ignored him. Aline’s eyes were fixed entirely on André-Louis, filled with wonder and pity. Meanwhile, André-Louis stared down at his mother in stupefied fascination.

The Marquis slowly read the letter all the way through. Then, very quietly, he put it back. His next priority was to compose himself. He was a product of an artificial age that strictly taught the suppression of emotion. Then he stepped back to Madame de Plougastel's side and bent down to help her up.

"Thérèse," he said.

Instinctively obeying his implied command, she made an effort to stand and control herself. The Marquis half guided and half carried her to the armchair by the table.

André-Louis watched them. He was still numb and bewildered, and he made no effort to help. He felt like he was in a dream as he watched the Marquis bend over Madame de Plougastel. He heard the Marquis ask a question, and his voice sounded distant.

"How long have you known about this, Thérèse?"

"I have always known," she said. "I confided him to Kercadiou. I saw him once when he was a child. But what does that matter now?"

"Why was I never told?" the Marquis asked. "Why did you deceive me? Why did you tell me that this child had died a few days after he was born? Why, Thérèse? Why did you do that?"

"I was afraid," she said. "I thought it would be better this way. I wanted to make sure that nobody, not even you, would ever know. And nobody has known except Quintin until last night. I was forced to tell him the truth to convince him to come here and save me."

"But what about me, Thérèse?" the Marquis insisted. "I had the right to know."

"Your right?" she asked, letting out a strange, desperate laugh. "What could you have done? Would you have acknowledged him? And then what? There was Plougastel, and there was my family. And there was you. You had stopped caring about me, and your fear of discovery had killed any love you had. Why should I have told you then? I would not have told you now if there had been any other way to save you both. I suffered this exact same terrible fear once before, when you and he fought in the woods. I was on my way to stop the duel when you met me. I would have revealed the truth as a last resort to prevent that horror. But mercifully, God spared me from having to do it then."

None of them doubted her story, even though it seemed incredible. Her words cleared up all of their doubts and explained many things that had been confusing until that moment.

The Marquis was completely overcome. He stumbled away to a chair and sat down heavily. He lost control of himself for a moment and buried his haggard face in his hands.

Through the windows that were open to the garden, they could hear the faint, distant beating of a drum. It was a reminder of the chaos happening outside. But they ignored the sound. To each of them, it felt like they were facing a horror much greater than anything tearing Paris apart. Finally, André-Louis began to speak. His voice was steady and incredibly cold.

"Monsieur de La Tour d'Azyr," he said. "I assume you agree that this revelation is just as disgusting and horrible to you as it is to me. However, it changes nothing. It does not erase everything that stands between us. Or, if it changes anything, it only adds more to our score. But what is the point of talking? Here, monsieur. Take this safe-conduct. It is made out for Madame de Plougastel's footman. Use it to escape as best as you can. In return, I ask that you never allow me to see you or hear from you again."

"André!" His mother turned to him with a cry. And then she asked that same question again. "Do you have no heart? What has he ever done to you to make you hate him so much?"

"I will tell you, madame," André-Louis said. "Two years ago, in this very room, I told you about a man who brutally murdered my best friend and ruined the girl I was going to marry. Monsieur de La Tour d'Azyr is that man."

She answered only with a moan and covered her face with her hands.

The Marquis slowly stood back up. He stepped forward slowly, and his smoldering eyes scanned his son's face.

"You are a hard man," he said grimly. "But I recognize that hardness. It comes from the blood you carry."

"Spare me that," André-Louis said.

The Marquis bowed his head. "I will not mention it again. But I want you to at least understand me, and you too, Thérèse. You accuse me of murdering your best friend. I will admit that the methods I used were perhaps unworthy. But what other methods did I have to handle an emergency that has only grown more obvious every day since then? Monsieur de Vilmorin was a revolutionary. He was a man with new ideas who wanted to overthrow society and rebuild it the way he wanted. I belonged to the class that just as rightfully wanted society to stay the way it was. It was not only better for me and my family, but I also argue that it is better for the whole world. In fact, no other kind of society is even possible. Every human society must naturally be made up of different classes. You might temporarily mix it up into a disorganized mess with a revolution like this, but it is only temporary. Soon, out of the chaos that you and your kind create, order has to be restored, or everyone will die. And when order is restored, the different classes that are necessary for an organized society will return. The people who were at the top yesterday might lose everything in the new order, without it actually helping anyone else. That was the change I fought against. I fought the spirit of that change with whatever weapons I had, whenever and wherever I found it. Monsieur de Vilmorin was an agitator of the worst kind. He was an eloquent man full of false ideals. He tricked poor, ignorant men into believing that this proposed change could make the world a better place for them. You are an intelligent man, and I challenge you to answer me honestly. Could such a thing ever be true or possible? You know it is false, and you know it is a dangerous idea. What made it even worse was that Monsieur de Vilmorin was sincere and eloquent. His voice was a danger that had to be removed and silenced. That was necessary for self-defense, and I did it to protect myself. I had no personal grudge against Monsieur de Vilmorin. He was a man of my own class. He was a polite, likable, respectable, and capable gentleman."

The Marquis paused and then continued. "You imagine that I killed him just because I wanted to kill, like some jungle beast attacking its natural prey. That has been your mistake from the beginning. I did what I did with an incredibly heavy heart. Oh, save your sneers! I do not lie, and I have never lied. I swear to you right here and right now, on my hope for Heaven, that what I say is true. I hated what I did. But for my own sake and for the sake of my class, I had to do it. Ask yourself whether Monsieur de Vilmorin would have hesitated for even a moment if killing me would have brought his dream utopia a moment closer to reality."

He looked steadily at his son. "After that, you decided that the sweetest revenge would be to ruin my plans. You did this by reviving the voice that I had silenced. You took up the foolish crusade for equality that belonged to Monsieur de Vilmorin. You lacked the understanding to see that God did not create men equal. Well, tonight you are in a position to judge which of us was right and which was wrong. You see what is happening here in Paris. You see the ugly ghost of anarchy walking through a country that has fallen into chaos. You probably have enough imagination to understand what must happen next. Do you really fool yourself into thinking that a perfect society will rise out of this filth and ruin? Don't you understand that society will soon have to reorganize itself out of all this?"

"But why say more?" the Marquis added. "I must have said enough to make you understand the only thing that really matters. I killed Monsieur de Vilmorin out of duty to my class. And the truth is that tonight I can look back on that act with peace of mind. I do not have a single regret, except for how it affects the relationship between you and me. I know that truth might offend you, but it should also convince you. When you kneeled beside your friend's body that day in Gavrillac, you insulted and provoked me. If I were the tiger you imagined me to be, I would have killed you too. As you know, I am a man with a quick temper. But I controlled my natural anger because I could forgive an insult to myself, even though I could not ignore a calculated attack against my class."

He paused for a moment. André-Louis stood completely still, listening and wondering. The others did the same. Then the Marquis continued, sounding a little less confident. "I was unfortunate in the matter regarding Mademoiselle Binet. I wronged you by mistake. I had no idea about the relationship between you two."

André-Louis finally interrupted him sharply with a question. "Would it have made a difference if you had known?"

"No," the Marquis answered honestly. "I share the flaws of my class. I cannot pretend that the kind of morals you are suggesting would have influenced me. But if you are capable of any fair judgment, can you really blame me that much for it?"

"When I consider everything, monsieur, I am quickly being forced to conclude that it is impossible to blame any man for anything in this world," André-Louis said. "We are all just toys played with by destiny. Think about this family gathering here tonight, while out there... Oh my God, let us just end this! Let us go our separate ways and write the end to this horrible chapter of our lives."

The Marquis looked at him seriously and sadly in silence for a moment.

"Perhaps it is for the best," he finally said in a quiet voice. He turned to Madame de Plougastel. "If there is one wrong I must admit to in my life, one wrong that I must bitterly regret, it is the wrong that I have done to you, my dear."

"Not now, Gervais! Not now!" she stammered, interrupting him.

"I must say it now, for the first and last time," he said. "I am leaving. It is unlikely that we will ever meet again. I will probably never see any of you again, even though you should have been the closest and dearest people to me. He says we are all toys played with by destiny. But that is not entirely true. Destiny is an intelligent force that moves with a purpose. In life, we pay for the evil we do. That is the lesson I have learned tonight. Through an act of betrayal, I unknowingly fathered a son. This son, who was just as ignorant of our relationship as I was, became the evil force in my life. He crossed me, blocked my path, and finally helped to pull me down into ruin. It is justice. It is poetic justice. My full and resigned acceptance of that fact is the only apology I can offer you."

He bent down and took one of madame's hands, which was resting limply in her lap.

"Goodbye, Thérèse!" His voice broke. He had finally reached the end of his iron self-control.

She stood up and hugged him for a moment, unashamed in front of the others. The ashes of their dead romance had been deeply stirred that night, and some lingering embers still glowed brightly before they finally burned out. But she did not try to stop him from leaving. She understood that their son had suggested the only smart and possible path, and she was grateful that the Marquis accepted it.

"God protect you, Gervais," she whispered. "You will take the safe-conduct, and... and you will let me know when you are safe?"

He held her face between his hands for a moment. Then he gently kissed her and pulled away. He stood up straight and looked outwardly calm again. He looked across the room at André-Louis, who was holding out a piece of paper to him.

"Here is the safe-conduct," André-Louis said. "Take it, monsieur. It is my first and last gift to you, and it is certainly the last gift I ever would have thought of giving you. It is the gift of life. In a way, it makes us even. The irony is not mine, but fate's. Take it, monsieur, and go in peace."

The Marquis took the paper. His eyes stared hungrily into the thin, stern face in front of him. He shoved the paper into his coat and then suddenly and convulsively held out his hand. His son's eyes asked a silent question.

"Let there be peace between us, in God's name," the Marquis said thickly.

Finally, André-Louis felt a stir of pity. Some of the sternness left his face, and he sighed. "Goodbye, monsieur," he said.

"You are hard," his father told him, sounding sad. "But perhaps you have the right to be. Under different circumstances, I would have been proud to call you my son. As it is..." He stopped suddenly and then added just as quickly, "Goodbye."

He let go of his son's hand and stepped back. They bowed formally to each other. Then the Marquis bowed to Aline in complete silence. His bow conveyed a sense of total surrender and finality.

After that, he turned and walked stiffly out of the room, and out of all their lives. Months later, they would hear that he was serving the Emperor of Austria.
