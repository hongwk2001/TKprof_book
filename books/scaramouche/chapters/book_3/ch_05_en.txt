Chapter 5. MADAME DE PLOUGASTEL

The postilion pulled the reins. The footman opened the door, lowering the steps and offering his arm to assist his mistress, just as she had requested. Then he opened one wing of the iron gates and held it for her. She was a woman in her early forties who had clearly been stunning in her youth and remained exceptionally beautiful, possessing that refined elegance that age brings to certain women. Her dress and posture both radiated high aristocratic rank.

"I will leave you here, since you have a visitor," André-Louis said.

"But it is an old acquaintance of yours, André. Do you remember Madame la Comtesse de Plougastel?"

He looked at the approaching lady, whom Aline was now hurrying to meet. Because she had been named, he recognized her. He realized that had he simply looked, he would have recognized her anywhere without a prompt, even though sixteen years had passed since he last saw her. The sight of her brought it all back, a treasured memory that subsequent events had never managed to erase.

When he was ten years old, just before being sent to school at Rennes, she had visited his godfather, her cousin. At that time, Rabouillet had taken him to the Manor of Gavrillac, where he was introduced to Madame de Plougastel. The great lady, in the full glory of her youthful beauty, with her gentle, cultured voice. Cultured it sounded like a foreign language to the little Breton boy, and her majestic worldly air, had intimidated him at first. But she gently calmed his fears and, through some mysterious charm, completely won his heart. He remembered his initial terror when told to hug her, and his reluctance to leave those soft, warm arms. He also remembered her sweet scent, a lilac perfume, for memory clings tightly to such details.

During her three days at Gavrillac, he visited the manor daily, spending hours with her. A childless woman with strong maternal instincts, she had warmly embraced this precociously smart, wide-eyed boy.

"Give him to me, Cousin Quintin," he remembered her asking his godfather on her final day. "Let me take him back to Versailles as my adopted child."

But the Seigneur had silently and gravely shaken his head, closing the matter. And then, when she said goodbye to him, the memory flooded back now, there had been tears in her eyes.

"Think of me sometimes, André-Louis," were her last words.

He remembered how flattered he had felt to win this great lady's affection so quickly. It gave him a sense of importance that lasted for months before finally fading into oblivion.

But now, seeing her again after sixteen years, it all came back vividly. She had profoundly changed and matured. The young girl from his past was now a worldly woman with an air of calm dignity and complete self-possession. Yet he remained certain he would have known her anywhere.

Aline hugged her affectionately. Then, noticing the Countess raising her eyebrows slightly at Aline's companion, she explained.

"This is André-Louis," Aline said. "Do you remember André-Louis, Madame?"

Madame stopped. André-Louis watched surprise wash over her face, draining her color and leaving her breathless for a moment.

Then her voice, that familiar, rich, musical voice, now even deeper and richer than before, repeated his name.

"André-Louis!"

The way she said it suggested a rush of memories, perhaps of the lost youth tied to his name. She paused for a long time, looking at him wide-eyed as he bowed before her.

"But of course I remember him," she finally said, stepping forward and extending her hand. He kissed it dutifully, submissively, and instinctively. "And this is what you have grown into?" She studied him, and he flushed with pride at the satisfaction in her tone. He felt like he had traveled back sixteen years, once again the little Breton boy at Gavrillac. She turned to Aline. "How wrong Quintin was in his assumptions. He was pleased to see him again, wasn't he?"

"So pleased, Madame, that he showed me the door," André-Louis replied.

"Ah!" She frowned, still studying him with her dark, thoughtful eyes. "We must change that, Aline. He is naturally very angry with you. But that is no way to win people over. I will plead your case, André-Louis. I am a good advocate."

He thanked her and prepared to leave.

"I leave my case in your hands with deep gratitude. My respects, Madame."

And so, despite his godfather's hostile reception, André-Louis hummed a tune as his yellow carriage rushed him back to Paris and the Rue du Hasard. The meeting with Madame de Plougastel had lifted his spirits; her promise to champion his cause alongside Aline reassured him that everything would work out.

He was proven right the following Thursday around noon, when Monsieur de Kercadiou barged into his academy. Gilles, the boy, brought the news. André-Louis immediately stopped his fencing lesson, pulled off his mask. Went down to the modest salon, still wearing his chamois waistcoat buttoned to the chin and carrying his foil under his arm, where his godfather was waiting.

The red-faced Lord of Gavrillac stood defensively to receive him.

"I have been persuaded to forgive you," he announced aggressively, making it sound like he only agreed to stop the annoying pleading.

André-Louis wasn't fooled. He recognized the Seigneur's act, designed to let him back down without losing face.

"Bless those who persuaded you, whoever they were. You give me back my happiness, Monsieur my godfather."

He took the offered hand and kissed it, acting on the deep-rooted habits of his childhood. It was a symbolic gesture of complete submission, restoring the bond of protector and protected between them, along with all its mutual duties. No words could have better made peace with this man who loved him.

Monsieur de Kercadiou's face turned a deeper shade of pink. His lip trembled, and his voice grew husky as he muttered, "My dear boy!" Then he caught himself, tossed his large head back, and frowned. His voice returned to its usual high pitch. "I hope you realize you have behaved terribly... terribly, and with complete ingratitude?"

"Doesn't that depend on your point of view?" André-Louis asked, deliberately keeping his tone soothing.

"It depends on facts, not points of view. Since I have been convinced to overlook it, I hope you intend to change your ways."

"I... I will stay out of politics," André-Louis said, offering the most truthful compromise he could.

"That is something, at least." His godfather allowed himself to calm down, now that a concession, or at least the appearance of one, had smoothed his ruffled feathers.

"Please take a chair, Monsieur."

"No, no. I am taking you with me to pay a visit. You owe my forgiveness entirely to Madame de Plougastel. I want you to come with me and thank her."

"I have my classes here..." André-Louis began, then stopped. "It doesn't matter! I will arrange things. Just a moment." He turned to head back into the academy.

"What classes? You aren't by any chance a fencing instructor?" Monsieur de Kercadiou had noticed the leather waistcoat and the foil tucked under his arm.

"I am the master of this academy, the academy of the late Bertrand des Amis, the most successful school of arms in Paris today."

Monsieur de Kercadiou raised his eyebrows.

"And you are the master?"

"Maître en fait d'Armes. I took over the academy after des Amis died."

He left Monsieur de Kercadiou to process the news and went to make his arrangements and change his clothes.

"So that is why you have started wearing a sword," Monsieur de Kercadiou remarked as they climbed into his waiting carriage.

"That, and the need to defend oneself these days."

"Are you telling me that a man earning his living in an honorable profession, supported mainly by the nobility, can also associate with those cheap lawyers and low-class writers stirring up trouble and rebellion?"

"You forget that I am a cheap lawyer myself, thanks to your own wishes, Monsieur."

Monsieur de Kercadiou grunted and took a pinch of snuff. "You say the academy is successful?" he asked after a moment.

"It is. I have two assistant instructors, and I could use a third. It is hard work."

"That must mean you are quite well off."

"I have reason to be satisfied. I earn much more than I need."

"Then you will be able to do your part in paying off this national debt," the nobleman grumbled, clearly pleased by the idea that the trouble André-Louis helped start would now cost him money.
Then the conversation shifted to Madame de Plougastel. André-Louis gathered that Monsieur de Kercadiou strongly disapproved of this visit, though he didn't explain why. But the Countess was a strong-willed woman nobody could refuse, someone the whole world obeyed. Monsieur de Plougastel was currently in Germany but would be returning soon. This careless slip made it easy to guess that Monsieur de Plougastel was one of the secret messengers traveling between the Queen of France and her brother, the Emperor of Austria.

The carriage pulled up to a beautiful town house in the Faubourg Saint-Denis, at the corner of the Rue Paradis. A slick servant led them into a small, elegant sitting room filled with gold and silk. It opened onto a terrace overlooking a miniature park. Madame was waiting for them there. She stood, dismissed the young woman reading to her, and came forward with both hands outstretched to greet her cousin Kercadiou.

"I was almost afraid you wouldn't keep your promise," she said. "I was wrong. But I really didn't expect you to succeed in bringing him." Smiling warmly, she gestured toward André-Louis.

The young man replied with polite charm.

"My memory of you, Madame, is too deeply engraved in my heart for any persuasion to be necessary."

"Ah, the charmer!" she said, giving him her hand. "We need to have a little talk, André-Louis," she told him, with a seriousness that made him slightly uncomfortable.

They sat down, and for a while, the conversation stayed on general topics, mostly focusing on André-Louis, his work, and his opinions. All the while, Madame watched him closely with those gentle, thoughtful eyes, until that sense of unease crept over him again. He instinctively knew he had been brought here for a deeper reason than the one they claimed.

Finally, as if it had been planned, and the clumsy Lord of Gavrillac was terrible at hiding his intentions, his godfather stood up. Using the excuse of wanting to see the garden, he strolled out through the windows onto the terrace, where red geraniums spilled wildly over the white stone railing. From there, he disappeared into the greenery below.

"Now we can talk more freely," Madame said. "Come here and sit next to me." She pointed to the empty space on her sofa.

André-Louis obeyed, though he felt a bit awkward. "You know," she said softly, resting a hand on his arm, "that you have behaved very badly. That your godfather has every right to be angry?"

"Madame, if I truly believed that, I would be the saddest, most desperate man alive." He then explained himself, just as he had done to his godfather on Sunday. "What I did, I did because it was the only way possible in a country where Privilege blocks justice. I had to fight a despicable monster who murdered my best friend, a cruel, senseless murder the law refused to punish. And as if that wasn't enough, please forgive my bluntness, Madame, he later ruined the woman I was supposed to marry."

"Ah, my God!" she cried out.

"Forgive me. I know it sounds awful. Perhaps you understand now what I went through, and what drove me. That last incident I am blamed for, the riot starting in the Feydau Theatre and spreading across Nantes, was caused by this."

"Who was this girl?"

It figures a woman would focus on the minor details, he thought.

"Oh, an actress, a foolish girl I don't miss at all. Her name was La Binet. I was acting in her father's troupe at the time. This was after the trouble in Rennes, when I had to hide from the kind of justice France offers, the hangman's justice for commoners. This final insult pushed me to start a riot in the theater."

"Poor boy," she said gently. "Only a woman's heart can truly understand your suffering; that is why I can so easily forgive you. But now..."

"But you don't understand, Madame. If I believed today that I only helped destroy Privilege for personal reasons, I think I would kill myself. My real justification is the hypocrisy of those who planned to make the States-General a sham, just a trick to deceive the nation."

"Wasn't it perhaps wise to use trickery in such a situation?"

He stared at her in disbelief.

"Can it ever be wise, Madame, to be insincere?"

"Oh, it certainly can. Trust me, I am twice your age and I know how the world works."

"I would argue, Madame, that nothing complicating life can be wise; and nothing complicates it more than insincerity. Just look at the mess this has caused."

"But surely, André-Louis, your views haven't become so twisted that you deny a country needs a ruling class?"

"Of course it does. But it doesn't have to be a hereditary one."

"What else could it be?"

He gave her a quick, sharp answer. "A person, Madame, is the product of their own actions. Let rights only be inherited from such achievements. That way, a nation's best people will always lead, and that nation will achieve greatness."

"So you think birth doesn't matter?"

"Not at all, Madame, otherwise, my own origins might bother me." Seeing her face flush deeply, he worried his bluntness had offended her. But the scolding he expected never came. Instead, 

"And it doesn't?" she asked. "Never, André?"

"Never, Madame. I am perfectly content."

"You have never... never regretted not having parents to care for you?"

He laughed, brushing off her sweet, unnecessary pity. "On the contrary, Madame. It terrifies me to imagine what they might have turned me into. I am thankful I got to shape myself."

She looked at him sadly for a moment, then smiled and gently shook her head.

"You certainly don't lack self-confidence... Still, I wish you saw things differently, André. This is a time of incredible opportunity for a talented, spirited young man. I could help you; I could help you go very far, if you would let me help you my way."

"Right," he thought. "Help me get hanged by sending me on treasonous missions to Austria for the Queen, just like Monsieur de Plougastel. That would certainly secure me a high position, on the gallows."

Out loud, he answered with polite restraint. "I appreciate it, Madame. But surely you see that, holding the ideals I do, I could never serve a cause trying to destroy them."

"You are blinded by prejudice, André-Louis, and by personal grudges. Will you really let them ruin your future?"

"If what I call ideals were just prejudices, would it be honest to act against them while still believing in them?"

"If only I could show you that you are wrong! I could help you find a worthy purpose for your talents. In the King's service, you would rise quickly. Please think about it, André-Louis, and let's discuss it again later."

He answered her with cool, formal politeness.

"I am afraid that would be pointless, Madame. Still, your interest in me is very flattering, and I thank you. It is just my bad luck that I am so stubborn."

"And who is being insincere now?" she asked him.

"Ah, but you see, Madame, my insincerity isn't meant to deceive."

Just then, Monsieur de Kercadiou stepped back through the window. He fussily announced that he needed to return to Meudon and would drop his godson off at the Rue du Hasard on the way.

"You must bring him back soon, Quintin," the Countess said as they said their goodbyes.

"Perhaps someday," Monsieur de Kercadiou replied vaguely, sweeping his godson out the door.

In the carriage, he bluntly asked what Madame had discussed.

"She was very kind, a sweet woman," André-Louis said thoughtfully.

"Devil take you, I didn't ask for your opinion of her. I asked what she said to you."

"She tried to show me the error of my ways. She talked about the great things I could achieve, with her kind help, if I came to my senses. But since miracles are rare, I didn't give her much hope."

"I see. I see. Did she say anything else?"

His tone was so demanding that André-Louis turned to look at him.

"What else did you expect her to say, Monsieur my godfather?"

"Oh, nothing."

"Then she met your expectations."

"What? Oh, a thousand devils, why can't you just talk normally so a plain man can understand you without solving a puzzle?"

He sulked for most of the ride back to the Rue du Hasard, or at least that's how it seemed to André-Louis. He sat in silence, looking dark and brooding.

"You can come visit us again at Meudon soon," he told André-Louis as they parted. "But please remember, no revolutionary politics next time, if we want to stay friends."
