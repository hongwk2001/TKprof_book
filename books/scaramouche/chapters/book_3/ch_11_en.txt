Chapter 31. INFERENCES

André-Louis drove fast and reached the dueling ground a few minutes early, despite their slight delay in setting out. He found Marquis de La Tour d'Azyr already waiting for him. A dark-complexioned young gentleman named d'Ormesson, wearing the blue uniform of a captain in the Gardes du Corps, stood by the Marquis's side.

André-Louis remained silent and preoccupied throughout the drive. His recent interview with Aline de Kercadiou, and the rash assumptions he had made about her motives, deeply unsettled him.

"Decidedly, this man must be killed," he said.

Le Chapelier did not answer. The Breton almost shuddered at his compatriot's cold-blooded attitude. He often thought lately that Moreau was hardly human. He also found him incomprehensibly inconsistent. André-Louis had been lofty and disdainful when he first heard the proposal to hunt down the duelist assassins. Yet he now went about the grim business with a revolting, ghoulish flippancy, or an even more revolting detachment.

They made their preparations quickly and in silence. Neither side showed undue haste or nervousness. The same grim determination prevailed in both men. The opponent had to be killed. They would accept no half-measures here. Both men stripped off their coats and waistcoats, removed their shoes, and rolled their shirt-sleeves to the elbow. They finally faced each other, sharing a common resolve to settle their long-standing score in full. Neither of them likely harbored a single misgiving about the outcome.

Le Chapelier and the young captain stood opposite each other beside them, alert and watchful.

"Allez, messieurs!"

The slender, wickedly delicate blades clashed together. They whirled after a momentary glide, moving as swift and bright as lightning, and becoming almost impossible to follow with the eye. The Marquis led the attack with impetuous vigor. André-Louis realized almost immediately that he faced an opponent of very different mettle from the duelists he fought last week, even including the terrible La Motte-Royau.

This man possessed extraordinary speed and almost perfect technique, gained through constant and intense practice. He also enjoyed physical advantages in strength and reach over André-Louis, which made him altogether formidable. He remained cool, self-contained, fearless, and purposeful. André-Louis wondered if anything could shake that calm.

He wanted to make the punishment as severe as possible. He was not content merely to kill the Marquis as the Marquis had killed Philippe. He wanted the Marquis to realize first his absolute powerlessness to avert that death, just as Philippe had experienced. Nothing less would satisfy André-Louis. The Marquis had to begin by tasting that cup of despair. That was part of the account, part of the debt owed.

André-Louis parried the heavy lunge that ended the first series of passes with a breaking sweep, and he actually laughed. He laughed gleefully, like a boy enjoying a sport he loves.

That extraordinary and ill-timed laugh made La Tour d'Azyr's recovery hastier and less dignified than it would have been. It startled and unsettled him. He was already disconcerted by his failure to strike home with such a beautifully timed and accurately delivered lunge.

He realized now that his opponent's strength exceeded his expectations, despite his own status as a fencing master. He had therefore put forth his utmost energy to end the fight at once.

The laugh that accompanied the parry, more than the parry itself, seemed to turn that intended end into merely a beginning. Yet it marked the end of something else. It ended the absolute confidence that had previously inspired La Tour d'Azyr. He no longer viewed the outcome as a foregone conclusion. He realized that he must fight cautiously and fence as he had never fenced in his life if he hoped to survive this encounter.

They settled down again. The Marquis initiated the attack once more, operating this time on the principle that the soundest defense is a strong offense. André-Louis allowed him to do so. He actually wanted him to spend his energy and magnificent speed against the even greater speed that the master had acquired through entire days of continuous fencing over nearly two years. André-Louis kept himself completely covered during that second bout with a beautiful, easy pressure of the strong part of his blade against the weak part of his opponent's. This exchange also culminated in a lunge.

André-Louis expected the lunge this time and parried it with merely a deflecting touch. He stepped suddenly forward at the same moment, moving right inside the other's guard. This placed his man so completely at his mercy that the Marquis did not even attempt to recover himself, as if frozen in fascination.

André-Louis did not laugh this time. He merely smiled into the dilating eyes of La Tour d'Azyr and made no attempt to press his advantage.

"Come, come, monsieur!" he told him sharply. "Am I to run my blade through an uncovered man?" He deliberately fell back, while his shaken opponent finally recovered his stance.

d'Ormesson exhaled the breath he had held in momentary horror. Le Chapelier swore softly.

"Name of a name! It is tempting Providence to play the fool in this fashion!" he muttered.

André-Louis observed the ashen pallor that now spread across his opponent's face.

"I think you begin to realize, monsieur, what Philippe de Vilmorin must have felt that day at Gavrillac. I desired that you should experience that first. Since we have accomplished that, let us make an end."

He went in with lightning rapidity. His point seemed to be everywhere at once to La Tour d'Azyr for a moment. Then André-Louis stretched forward from a low engagement in sixte with swift and vigorous ease to lunge in tierce. He drove his point to transfix his opponent, having uncovered him in that line through a series of calculated disengages. To his amazement and chagrin, La Tour d'Azyr parried the stroke. However, infinitely more to his chagrin, La Tour d'Azyr parried it just a fraction too late. All would have been well if he had parried it completely. But by striking the blade in the last fraction of a second, the Marquis deflected the point from his body's center, yet not enough to prevent a couple of feet of hard-driven steel from tearing through the muscles of his sword-arm.

None of these details were visible to the seconds. They only saw a swift whirl of flashing blades, followed by André-Louis stretching almost to the ground in an upward lunge that pierced the Marquis's right arm just below the shoulder.

The sword fell from La Tour d'Azyr's suddenly relaxed fingers, which were now completely powerless. He stood disarmed, his lip bitten, his face white, and his chest heaving, facing his opponent who had instantly recovered his stance. André-Louis rested the blood-tinged tip of his sword on the ground and surveyed him grimly, much as one surveys a prey that escapes at the last moment through sheer clumsiness.

The Assembly and the newspapers might hail this as another victory for the Paladin of the Third Estate. Only he knew the full extent and bitterness of his failure.

d'Ormesson sprang to his principal's side.

"You are hurt!" he cried stupidly.

"It is nothing," La Tour d'Azyr said. "A scratch." His lip writhed, however, and the torn sleeve of his fine cambric shirt quickly filled with blood.

d'Ormesson, being a practical man in such matters, produced a linen kerchief and tore it quickly into strips to improvise a bandage.

André-Louis continued to stand there, looking on as if bemused. He remained frozen until Le Chapelier touched his arm. He finally roused himself, sighed, and turned away to put on his clothes. He did not speak to or look at his former opponent again, and he left the dueling ground at once.

He walked slowly and in silent dejection toward the entrance of the Bois with Le Chapelier, heading to where they had left their carriage. A calèche carrying La Tour d'Azyr and his second passed them; it had originally driven almost right up to the site of the encounter. The Marquis's wounded arm rested in a sling improvised from his companion's sword-belt. His sky-blue coat with three collars was buttoned over the sling, leaving the right sleeve hanging empty. Otherwise, aside from a certain pallor, he looked much his usual self.

This explains how he returned first. How the two ladies, who had hurried to prevent the encounter, assumed their worst fears were realized upon seeing him arrive apparently safe and sound.

Madame de Plougastel tried to call out, but her voice failed her. She attempted to throw open the door of her own carriage, but her fingers fumbled clumsily and ineffectively with the handle. The calèche passed slowly in the meantime, and La Tour d'Azyr's fine eyes met her anguished gaze somberly yet intently. Then she noticed something else. d'Ormesson leaned back after leaning forward to join his companion in saluting the Countess. This movement revealed the empty right sleeve of La Tour d'Azyr's blue coat. Furthermore, the near side of the coat itself fell open from the point near the throat where a single button held it together, revealing the slung arm beneath in its blood-soaked cambric sleeve.

Even now, she feared jumping to the obvious conclusion. She feared that the Marquis, despite being wounded himself, might have dealt his adversary a much deadlier blow.

She finally found her voice and simultaneously signaled the driver of the calèche to stop.

d'Ormesson alighted as the carriage halted and met madame in the small space between the two vehicles.

"Where is M. Moreau?" she asked, surprising him with the question.

"Following at his leisure, no doubt, madame," he answered, recovering his composure.

"He is not hurt?"

"Unfortunately it is we who..." d'Ormesson began, when La Tour d'Azyr's voice cut in crisply from behind him.

"This interest on your part in M. Moreau, dear Countess..."

He broke off, noticing a vague challenge in the way she confronted him. His sentence needed no completion, however.

A vaguely awkward pause followed. Then she looked at d'Ormesson. Her manner changed. She offered what seemed to be an explanation for her concern about Moreau.

"Aline de Kercadiou is with me. The poor child has fainted."

She would have said much more right then, had d'Ormesson not been present.

Moved by deep concern for Aline, La Tour d'Azyr sprang up despite his wound.

"I am in poor condition to render assistance, madame," he said, an apologetic smile crossing his pale face. "But..."

He got down from the calèche with d'Ormesson's help and despite the latter's protests. The carriage then moved forward a short distance to clear the road for another carriage approaching from the direction of the Bois.

This is how André-Louis beheld a very touching scene when the approaching cabriolet overtook and passed the halted vehicles a few moments later. He stood up to get a better view and saw Aline in a half-swooning condition. She was beginning to revive by then, seated in the carriage doorway and supported by Madame de Plougastel. La Tour d'Azyr bent over the girl in an attitude of deepest concern, despite his wound, while d'Ormesson and madame's footman stood behind him.

The Countess looked up and saw him as he drove past. Her face lit up. It seemed to him that she was about to greet him or call out to him. He anticipated her by bowing frigidly to avoid any difficulty arising from his recent antagonist's presence there. His mood was cold, made even colder by what he witnessed, and he then resumed his seat with his eyes focused deliberately ahead.

Nothing could have more completely confirmed his conviction that Aline had come to plead with him that morning entirely on La Tour d'Azyr's behalf. He interpreted what he saw simply as a lady overcome with emotion at the sight of her dear friend's blood, while that same dear friend comforted her with assurances that his injury was far from mortal. He would blame his own perverse stupidity much later. He was perhaps too severe in his self-condemnation. Given his preconceptions, how else could he have interpreted the scene he beheld?

He now considered his earlier suspicions proven. Aline had lacked candor regarding her feelings for La Tour d'Azyr. He supposed it was simply a woman's way to be secretive about such matters, and he decided he must not blame her. Nor could he truly blame her in his heart for succumbing to the singular charm of a man like the Marquis. Even his hostility could not blind him to La Tour d'Azyr's attractions. The weakness that overtook her upon seeing him wounded proved her devotion, he thought.

"My God!" he cried aloud. "What must she have suffered, then, if I had killed him as I intended!"

If only she had been honest with him, she could so easily have won his consent to her request. If only she had told him what he now saw, that she loved La Tour d'Azyr, instead of letting him assume her regard for the Marquis stemmed from unworthy worldly ambition, he would have yielded immediately.

He sighed heavily and breathed a prayer for forgiveness to the shade of Vilmorin.

"It is perhaps just as well that my lunge went wide," he said.

"What do you mean?" Le Chapelier wondered.

"That I must relinquish all hope of restarting this business."
