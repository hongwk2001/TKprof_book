Chapter 3. PRESIDENT LE CHAPELIER

The unrest in Paris delayed the burial of Bertrand des Amis until the Wednesday of that eventful week. During the two following days, the city resembled an armed camp rather than a capital. The death of a fencing master passed almost unnoticed amid the events that were shaking the nation to its foundations. Even his pupils paid little attention. Most of them did not come to the academy during the two days his body lay there. However, a few did come. They shared the news with others. As a result, a group of about twenty young men followed the master to Pere Lachaise cemetery. Andre-Louis walked at their head as the chief mourner.

As far as Andre-Louis knew, there were no relatives to notify. However, within a week of the death of Monsieur des Amis, his sister arrived from Passy to claim his inheritance. This estate was quite large. The master had been successful and had saved money. He had invested most of it in the Compagnie des Eaux and the National Debt. Andre-Louis directed her to the lawyers and never saw her again.

The death of des Amis left Andre-Louis with a deep sense of loneliness and sorrow. He did not care about the sudden wealth it automatically brought him. The master's sister received the money he had saved. But Andre-Louis inherited the source of that wealth itself. He took over the fencing school. By this time, he was so well established as an instructor that his many students expected him to take charge and run it successfully. Furthermore, fencing academies had never seen such prosperous times. In these troubled days, every man was sharpening his sword and learning how to use it.

It took a couple of weeks for Andre-Louis to realize what had actually happened to him. He was completely exhausted. During those two weeks, he had been doing the work of two men. He would have found the task impossible if he had not come up with a clever idea. He paired off his more advanced students to fence with each other. He stood nearby to criticize, correct, and instruct them. Even so, he had to fence for about six hours every day. He brought his weariness from one day to the next. He was in danger of collapsing under the heavy burden of fatigue. Finally, he hired an assistant to teach the beginners, which was the hardest work. He was lucky enough to easily find one of his own students for the job. His name was Le Duc. As the summer went on, the number of students kept growing. It became necessary for Andre-Louis to hire another assistant. This was a skilled young instructor named Galoche. Andre-Louis also had to rent another room on the floor above.

These were busy days for Andre-Louis. They were more exhausting than the time he spent building up the Binet Company. Naturally, they were also days of incredible financial success. He noted with regret that Bertrand des Amis had died by bad luck right before sword fighting became so profitable.

The coat of arms of the Academie du Roi still hung outside his door, even though Andre-Louis had no right to display it. He solved this problem in a clever way worthy of Scaramouche. He kept the shield and the original sign. The sign read, "Academie de Bertrand des Amis, Maitre en fait d'Armes des Academies du Roi." Then, he simply added another line below it. It read, "Conducted by Andre-Louis."

He now had very little time to go outside. He learned about the revolutionary events happening around him from his students and the newspapers. The freedom of the press had created a flood of new newspapers in Paris. He heard about the fall of the Bastille. That major event felt almost like an anticlimax. It happened while Monsieur des Amis lay dead. It occurred the day before they buried him. In fact, it was the main reason his funeral had been delayed. The attack on the Bastille was inspired by the reckless charge of Prince Lambesc. That was the same charge that had killed the fencing master.

The angry citizens had besieged the electors in the Hotel de Ville. They demanded weapons to defend their lives against the foreign soldiers hired by the corrupt government. In the end, the electors agreed to give them weapons. Actually, they had no weapons to give. They simply gave the people permission to arm themselves. The electors also gave them a cockade colored red and blue, which were the colors of Paris. White was the color of the ancient standard of France. They added white to the cockade because red and blue were also the colors of the Duke of Orleans. This is how the tricolor flag was born. Additionally, a permanent committee of electors was formed to maintain public order.

The empowered people worked quickly. They forged sixty thousand pikes within thirty-six hours. By nine o'clock on Tuesday morning, thirty thousand men stood outside the Invalides. They broke in and stole about thirty thousand muskets by eleven o'clock. Meanwhile, other groups had seized the Arsenal and taken its gunpowder.

The citizens prepared to fight back against the attack that the royal army planned to launch on the city from seven different directions that evening. However, Paris did not wait to be attacked. The people took the initiative. Fueled by wild enthusiasm, they came up with the crazy idea of attacking the Bastille, that terrifying and threatening fortress. As you know, they actually succeeded before five o'clock that evening. The French Guards and their cannons helped them in this task.

Lambesc fled with his soldiers from the massive armed crowd that seemed to spring up from the very streets of Paris. He carried the news to Versailles. This news made the Court stop and think. The people now possessed the cannons they had captured from the Bastille. They were building barricades in the streets and placing those cannons on them. The royal army had waited too long to attack. Now they had to abandon their plans. An attack would only lead to useless killing. It would also further damage the already weak reputation of the royal family.

Fear made the Court briefly act sensibly again. They decided to stall for time. They planned to bring Necker back once more. The three estates would sit together as the National Assembly, just as the people demanded. This was a complete surrender to the threat of force. Force had become the only argument that mattered. The King went alone to tell the National Assembly about this last-minute decision. His surrender brought great relief to the members of the Assembly. They had been watching the terrible situation in Paris with pain and fear. The Assembly's motto was "No force but the force of reason and argument." They maintained this peaceful stance for two more years. They showed incredible patience and courage while facing constant provocation. History has not given them enough credit for this.

As the King was leaving the Assembly, a woman grabbed his knees. She asked the question that was on the minds of everyone in France.

"Ah, sire, are you really sincere? Are you sure they will not make you change your mind?"

A couple of days later, the King came to Paris to finish making peace and surrendering his absolute power. He came alone, guarded only by the representatives of the Nation. No one asked him if he was sincere this time. The Court was terrified by his journey. Weren't these rebellious Parisians the enemy? Should a King walk right into the middle of his enemies? If the King shared their fear, as his gloomy face suggested, his fear was unnecessary. Two hundred thousand armed men were waiting for him. They had no uniforms, and they carried the strangest collection of weapons ever seen. However, they waited for him as an honor guard.

Mayor Bailly met him at the city gate and handed him the keys to the city.

He said, "These are the same keys that were presented to Henri IV. He had reconquered his people. Now the people have reconquered their King."

Mayor Bailly offered the King the new tricolor cockade at the Hotel de Ville. The cockade was the symbol of the new constitutional France. The King officially approved the creation of the Garde Bourgeoise. He also confirmed the appointments of Bailly and Lafayette. Then, he left for Versailles while his loyal people shouted, "Vive le Roi!"

Now you see the privileged class finally submitting while staring down the barrel of a cannon. If they had surrendered sooner, they could have saved oceans of blood, mostly their own blood. The nobles and the clergy came to join the National Assembly. They planned to work together on the constitution that would rebuild France. However, this reunion was a joke. It was just as fake as the Archbishop of Paris singing a hymn of praise for the fall of the Bastille. That was the most ridiculous and unbelievable event of all these strange times. The only real result for the National Assembly was that it now contained five or six hundred enemies who wanted to block and delay its work.

But this is a famous story that you can read about in detail in other books. I am only giving you the parts of it that I found in the writings of Andre-Louis. I am using almost his exact words. They show how his own thinking changed. He was quiet now. However, he had come to truly believe in the political ideas he had preached earlier without actually believing in them.

During this time, his financial situation improved, and his legal troubles also faded away. The political changes around him changed his status. He no longer needed to hide. Who would accuse him of treason for what he had done in Brittany now? What court would dare to hang him for saying things early that everyone in France was saying today? As for the other possible charge of murder, no one cared about the death of the miserable Binet. Andre-Louis hoped he had killed the man, and he knew he had acted in self-defense.

One nice day in early August, Andre-Louis took a day off from the academy. The school was running smoothly under the guidance of his assistants. He rented a carriage and drove out to Versailles. He went to the Cafe d'Amaury. He knew this cafe was the meeting place of the Club Breton. This club was the beginning of the Society of the Friends of the Constitution, which later became famous as the Jacobins. He went there to find Le Chapelier. Le Chapelier had helped start the club. He was now a very important man. He was the president of the Assembly during this crucial time when they were debating the Declaration of the Rights of Man.

Le Chapelier's importance was obvious from how the waiter acted. Andre-Louis asked the waiter in the white apron and rolled-up sleeves about the politician. The waiter instantly became very submissive.

The waiter said that Monsieur Le Chapelier was upstairs with friends. He wanted to help the gentleman, but he was afraid to interrupt the meeting.

Andre-Louis gave the waiter a silver coin to encourage him to try. Then, he sat down at a marble table near the window. He looked out over the large square surrounded by trees. The main room of the cafe was empty in the middle of the afternoon. The great politician came down to meet him there. Less than a year ago, Le Chapelier had let Andre-Louis take the lead in a tricky situation. Today, Le Chapelier was one of the most powerful leaders of the struggling Nation. Meanwhile, Andre-Louis was just an ordinary citizen hiding in the background.

Both men were thinking about this difference as they looked at each other. They each noticed how much the other had changed in just a few months. Andre-Louis saw that Le Chapelier was dressing better. His face also looked more refined and thoughtful. He was thinner than before. His face was pale. He looked tired as he stared at his visitor through a gold-rimmed spyglass. Le Chapelier had sharp, tired eyes. He noticed even bigger changes in Andre-Louis. Andre-Louis had been practicing sword fighting constantly for months. This gave him graceful movements and good posture. He had a strange, hard-to-describe air of dignity and authority. He looked taller because of it. He was dressed quietly, but his clothes were expensive and elegant. He wore a small sword with a silver handle. He wore it like a man who knew how to use it. Le Chapelier remembered Andre-Louis having stringy black hair hanging over his skinny cheeks. Now, his hair was shiny and tied neatly in the back. He almost looked like a wealthy young nobleman.

However, these changes were only on the outside. They both quickly showed each other that they were still the same men inside. Le Chapelier was still a direct and blunt man from Brittany. He spoke and acted abruptly. He stood smiling for a moment. He looked surprised and happy. Then, he opened his arms wide. They hugged each other. The waiter watched them with awe and quickly backed away.

"Andre-Louis, my friend! Where did you come from?"

"I came from the bottom. I am here to get a close look at a man who has reached the top."

"The top! But if you had wanted to, you could be standing in my place right now."

"I am afraid of heights, and the air is too thin up there. Honestly, you do not look very healthy up there yourself, Isaac. You are pale."

"The Assembly was in session all night long. That is all. These damned privileged nobles make everything difficult. They will keep doing it until we officially get rid of their special rights."

They sat down. 

"Abolish their rights! Are you planning to do that much? I am not surprised, though. You have always been an extremist."

"I plan to abolish their rights so that I can save them. I want to remove their privileges officially. If we don't, the angry people will get rid of the nobles in a much more violent way."

"I see. And what about the King?"

"The King represents the Nation itself. We will save both the King and the Nation from the trap of noble privilege. Our new constitution will make this happen. Do you agree?"

Andre-Louis shrugged his shoulders. 

"Does it matter? I am a dreamer when it comes to politics. I am not a man of action. I have been very moderate until recently. I have been more moderate than you realize. But now, I am almost a republican. I have been watching. I have noticed that this King is basically nothing. He is just a puppet who dances when someone else pulls the strings."

"You say this King is useless? But what other king could we have? You are not one of those people who want the Duke of Orleans to take power, are you? He has a small group of supporters. Most of them only follow him because they hate the Queen, and they know the Queen hates him. Some people have thought about making him the regent. A few want him to have even more power. Robespierre is one of them."

"Who?" asked Andre-Louis. He had never heard the name.

"Robespierre. He is a ridiculous little lawyer who represents Arras. He is a shabby, clumsy, and shy bore. He gives speeches through his nose, and nobody listens to him. He is an extreme royalist. Both the royalists and the supporters of Orleans are using him for their own plans. He is stubborn, and he demands to be heard. People might listen to him one day. But the idea that he, or any of the others, will ever make the Duke of Orleans into a real leader is nonsense. Orleans himself might want power. But the man has no talent for villainy. He wants to do bad things, but he cannot. Mirabeau said that."

Le Chapelier stopped talking about politics. He asked Andre-Louis to tell him about his own life.

"You did not treat me like a friend when you wrote to me," he complained. "You did not tell me where you were. You told me you were completely broke, but you did not give me a way to send you money. I have been worried about you, Andre. But looking at you now, I see I did not need to worry. You look successful and confident. Tell me all about it."

Andre-Louis told him the whole story honestly. 

"Do you know that you constantly amaze me?" said the politician. "First you were a lawyer. Then you were an actor. Now you are a swordsman! I wonder how you will end up."

"Probably on the gallows."

"Nonsense! Be serious. Why not become a powerful senator in our new France? You could have that position right now if you wanted it."

"That is the fastest way to get to the gallows," laughed Andre-Louis.

Le Chapelier looked annoyed for a moment. I wonder if he remembered that conversation four years later. That was when he rode in the death cart to the guillotine at the Place de Greve.

"We have sixty-six representatives from Brittany in the Assembly. If one of them leaves, will you take his place as a substitute? I can say a good word for you. You also have a famous name in Rennes and Nantes. We could easily get you the job."

Andre-Louis laughed out loud. 

"Do you know, Isaac, that every time we meet, you try to push me into politics?"

"I do it because you have a talent for politics. You were born to be a politician."

"Ah, yes. I would be Scaramouche in real life. I have already played that role on the stage. Let that be enough. Tell me, Isaac, do you have any news about my old friend, the Marquis de La Tour d'Azyr?"

"He is here in Versailles, curse him. He is a constant problem for the Assembly. The peasants burned down his castle at La Tour d'Azyr. Unfortunately, he was not inside it when it happened. The fire did not destroy his arrogance. He dreams that when this crazy revolution is over, he will have slaves to rebuild his castle for him."

"So there has been trouble in Brittany?" Andre-Louis looked serious. He immediately thought of Gavrillac.

"There has been plenty of trouble in Brittany, and everywhere else too. Are you surprised? They are delaying the political reforms while people are starving. Castles have been burning all over the country for the last two weeks. The peasants copied the people of Paris. They treated every castle like it was the Bastille. Order is being restored everywhere now, and things have calmed down."

"What about Gavrillac? Do you know what happened there?"

"I believe everything is fine. Monsieur de Kercadiou was not like the Marquis de La Tour d'Azyr. He cared about his people. It is unlikely that they would attack Gavrillac. But don't you write letters to your godfather?"

"Under the circumstances, no. What you just told me makes it harder than ever. He must think I am one of the people who started the fires that are destroying his world. Please find out for sure if everyone at Gavrillac is safe. Let me know what you hear."

"I will find out immediately."

Andre-Louis was about to get into his carriage to return to Paris. Before he left, he asked one last question.

"Do you happen to know if Monsieur de La Tour d'Azyr has gotten married?" he asked.

"I do not know. That probably means he is not married. If a powerful noble like him got married, everyone would have heard about it."

"You are right." Andre-Louis spoke casually. "Goodbye, Isaac! You must come and see me at 13 Rue du Hasard. Come soon."

"I will come as soon as my work allows. But they keep me chained to my desk here right now."

"You are a poor slave to your duty, even with your gospel of liberty!"

"That is true! And because of my duty, I will definitely come to see you. I have a duty to Brittany. I need to make Omnes Omnibus one of our representatives in the National Assembly."

"That is one duty I hope you will ignore," laughed Andre-Louis. He drove away.
