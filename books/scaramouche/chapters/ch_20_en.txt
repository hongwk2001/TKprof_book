[Book 2 - Chapter 11: THE FRACAS AT THE THEATRE FEYDAU]

Chapter 11. THE FRACAS AT THE THEATRE FEYDAU

Leaving his host to negotiate with Mademoiselle de Kercadiou, and to explain his sudden departure without a formal goodbye, the Marquis drove away from Sautron in a gloomy mood. Twenty-four hours with La Binet had been more than enough for a man of his refined taste. He looked back on the affair with nausea. He marveled that he had found her so desirable just yesterday. He cursed himself for risking his chances with Mademoiselle de Kercadiou for such a worthless thrill. His mindset was not unusual, as it stemmed from the classic conflict between base desires and noble intentions in every man.

The Chevalier de Chabrillane sat across from him in the massive traveling carriage. He acted essentially as a gentleman-in-waiting for the Marquis. A small folding table was set up between them. The Chevalier suggested a game of piquet, but the Marquis was in no mood for cards. He was lost in thought. As they rattled over the cobblestones of Nantes, he remembered he had promised La Binet that he would watch her perform in "The Faithless Lover" that night. Now he was running away from her. The idea disgusted him for two reasons. He was breaking his promise, and he was acting like a coward. On top of that, he had led the greedy little actress to expect even more favors from him. She had practically tried to negotiate her future with him. She wanted him to take her to Paris, set her up in a furnished apartment, and use his powerful influence to open the doors of the capital's great theaters to her. Thankfully, he had not explicitly committed to anything, but he had not firmly refused her either. He knew he had to set the record straight now. He had to choose between his faded passion for her and his deep devotion to Mademoiselle de Kercadiou.

He believed his honor required him to escape this false position immediately. La Binet would undoubtedly cause a scene. However, he knew exactly how to handle that kind of hysteria. Money always proved useful.

He pulled the cord to stop the carriage. A footman appeared at the door.

"To the Theatre Feydau," he ordered.

The footman vanished, and the carriage rolled on. Chabrillane laughed cynically.

"I would prefer it if you were not amused," the Marquis snapped. "You do not understand." He then explained himself. It was a rare moment of openness for him, but he could not bear being misunderstood on such a matter. Chabrillane quickly grew serious to match the Marquis's mood.

"Why not just write a letter?" he suggested. "I would find that much easier."

The Marquis's answer perfectly revealed his state of mind.

"Letters can get lost or misunderstood. I refuse to take either risk. If she did not reply, I would never know if she received it. I will have no peace until I know this affair is officially over. The carriage can wait while we are at the theater. We will leave right after. We can travel all night if necessary."

"Damn it," Chabrillane muttered with a grimace. But he said nothing more.

The large traveling carriage stopped at the brightly lit entrance of the Feydau. The Marquis stepped out. He walked into the theater with Chabrillane, completely unaware that he was walking straight into a trap set by André-Louis.

André-Louis was furious. Climène had been gone for a long time with the Marquis. His anger grew worse as he watched Binet’s unbearable smugness about the situation.

He tried to act stoic and detached, but in his heart, André-Louis felt tormented and disgusted. He did not blame Climène. He realized he had been wrong about her. She was just weak, driven by anyone who promised her success. She was infected by greed, and he was glad he discovered this before marrying her. Now, he felt nothing for her but pity and mild contempt. The pity was a remnant of the love he had recently felt for her. It was like the bitter dregs left at the bottom of a wine glass. He saved his real anger for her father and her seducer.

His thoughts on that Monday morning were already dark. Climène had still not returned from her Sunday excursion in the Marquis's coach. Leander, who was entirely distraught, only made his anger worse.

Up until now, the two men had despised each other. Now, their shared misfortune brought them into a strange alliance. At least, that was how Leander felt when he went looking for André-Louis. He found him calmly smoking a pipe on the quay right in front of the inn.

"In the name of a pig!" Leander shouted. "How can you sit there and smoke at a time like this?"

Scaramouche looked up at the sky. "It is not too cold," he replied. "The sun is shining. I am perfectly fine here."

"I am not talking about the weather!" Leander exclaimed in frustration.

"Then what are you talking about?"

"About Climène, of course."

"Oh, the lady no longer interests me," André-Louis lied.

Leander stood squarely in front of him. He was a handsome man, dressed elegantly with well-powdered hair and silk stockings. His face was pale, and his large eyes looked even wider than usual.

"She does not interest you? Were you not going to marry her?"

André-Louis exhaled a cloud of smoke. "I assume you are not trying to be offensive, but you make it sound like I live on other men's leftovers."

"My God!" Leander gasped. He stared in disbelief before bursting out again. "Are you completely heartless? Are you always playing Scaramouche?"

"What do you expect me to do?" André-Louis asked, showing mild surprise.

"I expect you to fight for her."

"But she has already left." André-Louis puffed on his pipe while Leander clenched his fists in helpless rage. "Why fight against the inevitable? Did you fight when I took her from you?"

"She was not mine to lose. I only hoped to win her, and you beat me to it. Even if things were different, there is no comparison. That was an honorable situation, but this is hell."

Leander's intense emotion moved André-Louis. He grabbed Leander's arm. "You are a good man, Leander. I am glad I stepped in to save you from this fate."

"Oh, you do not love her!" Leander cried out passionately. "You never did. You do not know what love is, or you would not talk like this. If she were my fiancée and this happened, I would have killed the man! Do you hear me? But you just sit here, smoking and talking about her like she is someone's leftovers. I am surprised I did not punch you for saying that."

He ripped his arm out of André-Louis's grip. He looked ready to hit him right then.

"You should have," André-Louis said calmly. "It fits your character."

Leander cursed and turned to walk away. André-Louis stopped him.

"Wait a moment, my friend. Let me ask you a question. Would you still marry her now?"

"Would I?" Leander's eyes burned with passion. "Would I? If she said she would marry me, I would be her slave."

"Slave is the perfect word. A slave in hell."

"It would never be hell as long as I was with her, no matter what she had done. I love her, man. I am not like you. I love her. Do you understand?"

"I have known that for a while," André-Louis replied. "I just did not realize your condition was so severe. Well, God knows I loved her too. I loved her enough to share your desire to kill. For me, spilling the blue blood of La Tour d'Azyr would hardly be enough. I would want to mix it with the filthy blood of the despicable Binet."

For a split second, André-Louis lost control of his emotions. His biting tone revealed the intense fury burning beneath his cold exterior. Leander grabbed his hand.

"I knew you were just acting," Leander said. "You feel the same way I do."

"Look at us, partners in crime. It seems I have given myself away. So, what now? Do you want to see this pretty Marquis torn apart? I might be able to arrange that for you."

"What?" Leander stared at him, wondering if this was another one of Scaramouche's dark jokes.

"It is not really that difficult, as long as I have some help. I just need a little assistance. Will you help me?"

"Anything you ask," Leander blurted out. "You can have my life if you need it."

André-Louis took his arm again. "Let us walk," he said. "I will tell you what to do."

When they returned, the company was already eating dinner. Mademoiselle Climène had still not come back. A tense silence hung over the table. Columbine and Madame looked anxious. The relationship between Binet and his acting troupe was growing more strained every day.

André-Louis and Leander sat in their usual seats. Binet watched them with a nasty gleam in his small eyes. His thick lips twisted into a crooked smile.

"You two have become very friendly all of a sudden," he mocked.

"You are very observant, Binet," Scaramouche replied. His cold disgust was a clear insult. "Can you also guess the reason?"

"It is easy to figure out."

"Then please, share it with the rest of us!" André-Louis challenged him, and waited. "What? Are you hesitating? Is it possible that even your shamelessness has limits?"

Binet raised his massive head. "Are you trying to pick a fight with me, Scaramouche?" His deep voice rumbled like thunder.

"A fight? You must be joking. A man does not fight with creatures like you. We all know how society views husbands who ignore their wives' affairs. But, in God's name, what kind of respect is there for a father who ignores his daughter's affairs?"

Binet heaved his huge body up. He aggressively shook off Pierrot, who was sitting next to him and trying to calm him down.

"A thousand devils!" Binet roared. "If you talk to me like that, I will break every bone in your filthy body!"

"If you lay a single finger on me, Binet, you will give me the excuse I need to kill you." André-Louis remained perfectly calm, which made him even more intimidating. The rest of the company panicked. He pulled the handle of a newly purchased pistol from his pocket. "I carry a weapon, Binet. I think it is only fair to warn you. If you provoke me, I will kill you without any hesitation. It would be like stepping on a slug, which is exactly what you are. A fat, slimy slug. Just a foul creature without a soul or a brain. Honestly, it turns my stomach just thinking about sitting at the same table as you."

He pushed his plate away and stood up. "I am going to eat with the commoners downstairs."

Columbine immediately jumped to her feet.

"I am coming with you, Scaramouche!" she declared.

Her action served as a signal. If they had planned it, it could not have been executed more perfectly. In fact, Binet was convinced they had conspired against him. Leander followed Columbine, then Polichinelle, and soon the entire troupe walked out. Binet found himself sitting alone at the head of an empty table in an empty room. He was badly shaken. His anger could not hide the sudden fear creeping into his mind.

He sat there trying to figure out what to do. About half an hour later, his daughter finally walked into the room, returning from her trip.

She looked pale and a bit frightened. She felt very self-conscious, knowing she had to face the judgment of the entire company.

When she saw only her father in the room, she stopped in the doorway.

"Where is everyone?" she asked, trying hard to sound normal.

M. Binet lifted his massive head and stared at her with bloodshot eyes. He scowled, puffed out his thick lips, and made a harsh grunting noise in his throat. Still, he took a moment to look at her. She looked graceful and beautiful, a true lady of fashion in her long, fur-trimmed green coat. She held a muff, and her wide hat was decorated with a sparkling rhinestone buckle, sitting perfectly on her beautifully styled brown hair. He thought he had nothing to worry about as long as he had such an attractive daughter, regardless of whatever tricks Scaramouche tried to pull.

However, he did not share any of these comforting thoughts with her.

"So, you are finally back, you little fool," he grumbled. "I was starting to wonder if we would have to cancel the show tonight. I would not have been surprised if you had missed it completely. You decided to play your hand your own way and ignored my advice, so nothing surprises me anymore."

She walked over to the table and leaned against it, looking down at him with clear disdain.

"I have nothing to regret," she stated.

"That is what every fool says at first. Even if you did regret it, you would never admit it. That is just how you are. You stubbornly ignore the advice of older and wiser people. By my life, girl, what do you really know about men?"

"I am not complaining," she reminded him.

"No, but you will be complaining soon enough. You will soon realize that you should have listened to your old father. As long as your Marquis was desperate for you, you could have made him do anything. If you had just let him kiss your fingertips and nothing more, that would have been the time to secure your future. You could live for a thousand years and never get a chance like that again. You threw it all away, and for what?"

Mademoiselle sat down. "You are disgusting," she said with obvious disgust.

"I am disgusting?" His thick lips curled into a sneer. "I have had enough of living at the bottom of the barrel, and I thought you had too. You had the winning hand to secure a fortune, if you had only played it the way I told you. Well, you played it, and where is the fortune? We can wish for it all we want, but it is gone. And, by God, we are going to need a miracle if things with the acting troupe keep going the way they are. That scoundrel Scaramouche has been turning them against me. They have suddenly developed a strong moral compass. They refuse to sit at the table with me." He spat the words out, caught between anger and bitter laughter. "Your friend Scaramouche started all of this. He actually threatened my life! He threatened me! He called me... But that does not matter. What matters is that soon the Binet Troupe is going to realize they can survive without M. Binet and his daughter. This ungrateful bastard I took in has slowly stolen everything from me. He has the power to take my troupe away from me today, and he is ruthless enough to do it."

"Let him," mademoiselle replied dismissively.

"Let him?" Binet was shocked. "And what will happen to us?"

"The Binet Troupe will not concern me for much longer anyway," she said. "I am going to Paris soon. There are much better theaters there than the Feydau. There is Mademoiselle Montansier's theater in the Palais Royal. There is the Ambigu Comique. There is the Comédie Française. It is even possible I might get a theater of my own."

For a moment, Binet's eyes widened. He reached out his fat hand and placed it on hers. She noticed it was shaking.

"Did he promise you that? Did he actually promise?"

She looked at him, tilting her head with a sly, strange little smile on her perfect lips.

"He did not refuse when I asked him for it," she answered. She sounded completely convinced that everything would go her way.

"Bah!" He snatched his hand back and heaved himself up. Disgust covered his face. "He did not refuse!" he mocked her, and then his voice filled with anger. "If you had done what I told you, he would have agreed to anything you wanted! More importantly, he would have bought you whatever you asked for. He has endless wealth! You turned a sure thing into a vague possibility. I hate possibilities! God above! I have lived my whole life on possibilities, and I have nearly starved to death because of them."

If she had known about the conversation happening at the Chateau de Sautron at that very moment, she would not have laughed at her father's grim warnings. But she was destined never to find out, which was the cruelest punishment of all. When everything suddenly fell apart, destroying all her hopes of a future with the Marquis and ending the Binet Troupe, she would blame it entirely on Scaramouche's malicious interference.

She was partly right. Without the warning from the Lord of Sautron, the Marquis might have used the events at the Theatre Feydau that night as a convenient excuse to end a messy affair. And the destruction of the Binet Troupe was undoubtedly André-Louis's doing. However, it was not an outcome he had planned or even expected.

In fact, during the intermission after the second act, André-Louis went to the dressing room that Polichinelle shared with Rhodomont. Polichinelle was in the middle of changing his costume.

"I would not bother changing if I were you," André-Louis said. "The play will probably not get past my opening scene with Leander in the next act."

"What do you mean?"

"You will see." He placed a piece of paper on Polichinelle's makeup table. "Take a look at this. It is a sort of last will and testament for the troupe. I used to be a lawyer, so the document is perfectly legal. I am signing over my entire share of the company to all of you."

"Wait, you are not leaving us, are you?" Polichinelle cried out in panic. Rhodomont also stared at him with the same question in his eyes.

Scaramouche just shrugged. Polichinelle continued gloomily, "Of course, we should have seen this coming. But why should you be the one to leave? You are the one who made us successful. You are the brains behind this whole operation. You turned us into a real theatrical company! If anyone has to go, it should be Binet, him and his miserable daughter. Or, by God, if you leave, we will all leave with you!"

"Exactly," Rhodomont agreed. "We have had enough of that fat scoundrel."

"I considered that, of course," André-Louis said. "I knew you would support me. Let us wait and discuss it after tonight, if I survive."

"If you survive?" they both shouted.

Polichinelle stood up. "Alright, what crazy plan do you have in mind now?" he asked.

"Well, for one thing, I am doing Leander a favor. For another, I am settling an old score."

Just then, the three knocks signaling the start of the next act echoed through the theater.

"I have to go. Hold on to that paper, Polichinelle. Who knows, maybe we will not need it after all."

He hurried away. Rhodomont stared at Polichinelle. Polichinelle stared at Rhodomont.

"What on earth is he planning?" Rhodomont wondered aloud.

"The quickest way to find out is to go and watch," Polichinelle replied. He quickly finished changing, completely ignoring Scaramouche's advice. Then he and Rhodomont headed for the stage.

As they got close to the stage wings, they heard a massive roar of applause from the audience. But it was not normal applause. It sounded different, more intense. As the noise faded, Scaramouche's voice rang out, clear as a bell.

"So you see, my dear M. Leander, when you talk about the Third Estate, you need to be more specific. What exactly is the Third Estate?"

"It is nothing," Leander answered.

The audience gasped collectively. The sound was loud enough to be heard clearly in the wings. Then Scaramouche quickly asked his next question.

"True. It is a tragedy! But what should it be?"

"It should be everything," Leander declared.

The audience erupted into wild cheers. Leander's unexpected answer drove them into a frenzy.

"True again," Scaramouche said loudly. "And more importantly, that is what it will become. In fact, that is what it already is. Do you doubt it?"

"I hope for it," said Leander, sticking perfectly to his script.

"You can believe it," Scaramouche promised, and the crowd roared like thunder once more.

Polichinelle and Rhodomont looked at each other. Polichinelle even winked, clearly enjoying the chaos.

"Holy name!" a voice growled behind them. "Is that scoundrel playing his political tricks again?"

They turned and saw M. Binet. He had sneaked up behind them with his silent footsteps. He stood there in his red Pantaloon costume, wearing a trailing nightgown. His tiny eyes glared fiercely from behind his fake nose. But they quickly turned their attention back to Scaramouche's voice. He had walked right up to the front of the stage.

"He doubts it," Scaramouche told the audience. "But then again, M. Leander belongs to those who worship the outdated system of Privilege. Naturally, he is a little afraid to accept a truth that is becoming clear to the rest of the world. Shall I convince him? Shall I tell him how a group of noblemen and their armed servants, six hundred men in total, tried to dictate the law to the Third Estate in Rennes just a few weeks ago? Should I remind him how bravely the Third Estate stood their ground? Do I need to tell him how they swept the streets clean of that mob of nobles? That noble scum!"

Wild applause cut him off. The phrase hit perfectly. The common people had suffered under that exact insult from the wealthy, and they loved hearing it thrown right back at the nobles.

"But let me tell you about their leader. He is either the noblest of the scum, or the scummiest of the nobles! You all know him. He is afraid of many things, but he fears the truth most of all. Men like him want to silence the eloquent truth the moment it is spoken. So he gathered his noble friends and their servants and led them out to slaughter the poor citizens who dared to speak up. But those citizens refused to be slaughtered in the streets of Rennes. They decided that if the nobles wanted blood to flow, it might as well be noble blood! So the commoners organized themselves. They formed their own mob to fight the noble mob! They fought so well that they drove M. de La Tour d'Azyr and his violent followers right off the battlefield, leaving them with broken heads and shattered egos! They ran away and begged the monks for shelter! The monks gave them sanctuary in their convent. Among the survivors was their proud leader, M. de La Tour d'Azyr. Have you heard of this brave Marquis, this great lord who controls life and death?"

The audience in the pit went wild. They finally quieted down as Scaramouche continued.

"Oh, it was an incredible sight to see this mighty hunter running for his life like a scared rabbit, hiding in the convent! Rennes has not seen him since. Rennes would love to see him again. But he may be brave, he is also very careful. Where do you think he is hiding now? Where is this great lord who wanted to wash the streets of Rennes in the blood of its citizens? Where is the man who wanted to butcher innocent people just to silence the voice of reason and liberty that is ringing throughout France today? Where do you think he is hiding? He is right here in Nantes!"

The theater erupted in chaos again.

"What are you saying? You think it is impossible? My friends, he is in this very theater right now! He is hiding up there in that private box. He is too shy to show his face. Oh, he is a very modest gentleman. But he is right there behind the curtains. Will you not show yourself to your friends, M. de La Tour d'Azyr? Monsieur the Marquis, who thinks eloquent speech is such a dangerous thing! Look, they want to have a word with you! They do not believe me when I tell them you are here!"

Now, no matter what André-Louis thought of him, M. de La Tour d'Azyr was certainly not a coward. Saying he was hiding in Nantes was simply not true. He walked around the city openly and proudly. It was just a coincidence that the people of Nantes did not know he was there until this moment. He was far too proud to announce his arrival, and too proud to hide it.

However, once he was challenged directly, he acted. Despite the angry reaction of the crowd to Scaramouche's speech, and despite Chabrillane's attempts to hold him back, the Marquis pulled open the curtain of his box. He stood there, pale but completely calm and full of scorn. He looked down at the bold Scaramouche, and then at the angry crowd shouting at him.

People booed and yelled at him. They shook their fists and waved their canes threateningly.

"Assassin! Scoundrel! Coward! Traitor!"

He stood firm against the storm, smiling down at them with absolute contempt. He was waiting for the noise to die down so he could speak to them. However, he quickly realized he was waiting for nothing.

His obvious contempt only made the crowd angrier.

Complete chaos broke out in the pit. People were throwing punches left and right. Groups were wrestling each other, and swords were being drawn here and there. Luckily, the crowd was packed too tightly for anyone to use a sword properly. Men with women, and those who hated violence, scrambled to escape the theater. The whole building was turning into a war zone. People were smashing chairs to use as weapons, and ripping pieces off the chandeliers to throw.

A gentleman in one of the boxes threw a piece of a chandelier directly at Scaramouche. It narrowly missed his head as he stood there, looking down in dark triumph at the riot he had caused. He knew exactly how volatile the audience was, and he had intentionally thrown a lit match into the powder keg to start this fire.

He watched as men quickly chose sides in the great conflict that was already tearing France apart. Their battle cries echoed through the theater.

"Down with the commoners!" some yelled.

"Down with the privileged!" others screamed back.

Then, above all the noise, one single cry rang out clearly and repeatedly.

"To the box! Death to the butcher of Rennes! Death to La Tour d'Azyr, who makes war on the people!"

A large group rushed toward one of the pit doors that led to the staircase up to the private boxes.

The fight and confusion spread like wildfire, spilling out of the theater and into the street. La Tour d'Azyr's box became the main target for the angry citizens. It also became a rallying point for the gentlemen in the theater, and for anyone who supported the nobles.

La Tour d'Azyr stepped back from the front of the box to greet the men who came to defend him. Meanwhile, down in the pit, a group of angry gentlemen tried to rush across the empty orchestra pit to attack the comedian who started the riot. They were immediately blocked and pushed back by a group of commoners who agreed with André-Louis's speech.

Seeing this, and remembering the piece of the chandelier that had almost hit him, André-Louis turned to Leander, who was still standing next to him.

"I think it is time to leave," he said.

Leander looked terrifyingly pale under his stage makeup. He was completely shocked by the riot, which was far worse than anything he could have imagined. He mumbled a quick agreement. However, it looked like they had waited too long. They were suddenly attacked from behind.

M. Binet had finally managed to push past Polichinelle and Rhodomont, who had been trying to hold him back because of his murderous rage. Half a dozen gentlemen from the green room had run onto the stage to kill the actor who started the riot. They were the ones who threw aside the two comedians holding Binet. Now they were charging at André-Louis with their swords drawn. But right behind them came Polichinelle, Rhodomont, Harlequin, Pierrot, Pasquariel, and Basque the artist. They were armed with whatever props and tools they could grab. They were determined to protect the man they supported, the man who was their only hope.

Binet charged ahead of everyone else. He was moving faster than anyone had ever seen him move. He was violently swinging the long cane that was always part of his Pantaloon costume.

"You infamous scoundrel!" he roared. "You have ruined me! In the name of a name, you are going to pay for this!"

André-Louis turned to face him. "You are confusing cause and effect," he said calmly. But he did not get a chance to finish. Binet swung the cane viciously, and it struck André-Louis on the shoulder, snapping in half. If André-Louis had not moved quickly, the blow would have hit his head and knocked him out. As he dodged, he reached into his pocket. Right after the crack of the breaking cane came the crack of André-Louis's pistol.

"I warned you, you filthy pimp!" he shouted. As he said it, he shot Binet right in the stomach.

Binet screamed and collapsed. The fierce Polichinelle, who looked even fiercer in the middle of a real fight, leaned in and spoke quickly into André-Louis's ear.

"You fool! That was completely unnecessary! Get out of here right now, or you will die! Run!"

André-Louis realized it was good advice, and he took it. The gentlemen who had followed Binet onto the stage were distracted. The actors were fighting them with their makeshift weapons, and the sight of Scaramouche holding a second pistol made them hesitate. They backed off and let him go. He ran into the wings, only to run straight into two police officers. The authorities were already rushing into the theater to stop the riot. Seeing the police reminded him of the serious trouble he was in, especially with a bullet currently lodged in Binet's fat body. He aimed his pistol at them.

"Get out of my way, or I will blow your brains out!" he threatened. The officers, who did not have guns, were frightened and stepped back to let him pass. He slipped past the green room, where the actresses had locked themselves inside to hide from the riot. Finally, he escaped into the deserted street behind the theater. He ran down the dark street, desperate to reach the inn so he could grab his clothes and his money. He knew he could not escape the city dressed as Scaramouche.
