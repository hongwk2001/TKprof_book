BOOK 3: THE SWORD
CHAPTER 1. TRANSITION

André-Louis wrote to Le Chapelier from Paris in a letter that survives, "You may agree that it is to be regretted I should definitely have discarded the costume of Scaramouche, since clearly there could be no attire more fitting for me. It seems to be my part always to stir up trouble and then to slip away before I get caught in the crash of the warring elements I have aroused. It is a humiliating thought. I seek consolation in Epictetus's reminder, do you ever read Epictetus, that we are merely actors in a play, assigned whatever role the Director chooses. It does not, however, console me to have been cast in such a contemptible role, to find myself always excelling at running away. But if I am not brave, at least I am prudent, so that where I lack one virtue, I can claim to possess another almost to an extreme. On a previous occasion, they wanted to hang me for sedition. Should I have stayed to be hanged? This time, they may want to hang me for several things, including murder, for I do not know whether that scoundrel Binet is alive or dead after the lead I pumped into his fat belly. And I can't say I care much. If I have any hope at all in the matter, it's that he is dead and damned. But I am really indifferent. My own concerns are troubling me enough. I have almost spent the little money I managed to hide on me before I fled from Nantes on that dreadful night. Both of the only two professions I know anything about—law and theater—are closed to me, since I cannot find employment in either without revealing myself as a man urgently wanted by the hangman. As things are, it is very possible that I may die of hunger, especially considering the current price of food in this ravenous city. Again, I turn to Epictetus for comfort. 'It is better,' he says, 'to die of hunger having lived without grief and fear, than to live with a troubled spirit amid abundance.' I seem likely to perish in the state that he considers so enviable. The fact that it doesn't seem exactly enviable to me simply proves that as a Stoic, I am not a success."

Around the same time, he also wrote another letter to the Marquis de La Tour d’Azyr. M. Emile Quersac later published this letter in his work, "Undercurrents of the Revolution in Brittany." Quersac had discovered it in the archives of Rennes, where M. de Lesdiguieres had sent it after receiving it from the Marquis for legal purposes.

He writes in this letter, "The Paris newspapers, which have reported in considerable detail the incident at the Feydau Theater and revealed the true identity of the Scaramouche who caused it, also inform me that you escaped the fate I intended for you when I stirred up that storm of public opinion and outrage. Don't take satisfaction in thinking I regret your escape. I don't. I rejoice in it. Delivering justice through death has one drawback: the victim never knows that justice has found him. If you had died, if you had been torn limb from limb that night, I would now regret your eternal, untroubled slumber. The guilty should atone not through a peaceful death, but through mental torment. You see, I'm not sure that hell in the afterlife is a certainty, but I'm quite sure it can be a certainty in this life. I want you to continue living for a while longer, so you can taste some of its bitterness.

"You murdered Philippe de Vilmorin because you feared what you called his very dangerous gift of eloquence. That day, I swore an oath: your evil deed would be fruitless. I vowed to make it so. The voice you murdered to silence would, despite your actions, ring like a trumpet across the land. That was my conception of revenge. Do you realize how I've been fulfilling it? How I'll continue to fulfill it whenever the opportunity arises? In the speech that ignited the people of Rennes the very day after that deed, didn't you hear Philippe de Vilmorin's voice? He spoke his own ideas with a fire and passion greater than he could have commanded, because Nemesis herself lent me her inspiring aid. At Nantes, in the voice of Omnes Omnibus, my voice spoke again. It demanded the petition that sounded the death knell for your hopes of coercing the Third Estate. Didn't you hear Philippe de Vilmorin's voice once more? Didn't you realize that it was the mind of the man you murdered, resurrected in me, his surviving friend, that made your futile armed attempt last January necessary? Your order, finally beaten, was driven to seek sanctuary in the Cordelier Convent. And that night, when you were denounced to the people from the Feydau stage, didn't you hear it again? In the voice of Scaramouche, you heard Philippe de Vilmorin's voice, using that dangerous gift of eloquence you so foolishly imagined you could silence with a sword thrust. It's becoming a persecution, isn't it? This voice from the grave insists on being heard. It won't rest until you've been cast into the pit. By now, you'll be regretting that you didn't kill me too, as I invited you to do that day. I can imagine the bitterness of this regret, and I contemplate it with satisfaction. Regret over a neglected opportunity is the worst hell a living soul can inhabit, especially a soul like yours. Because of this, I'm glad to know you survived the riot at the Feydau, even though at the time, I didn't intend for you to. Because of this, I'm content that you should live to be enraged and suffer in the shadow of your evil deed. You'll know at last, since you hadn't the sense to realize it before, that Philippe de Vilmorin's voice will follow you. It will denounce you ever more loudly, ever more insistently, until, having lived in dread, you go down in blood under the just rage that your victim's dangerous gift of eloquence is kindling against you."

I find it odd that he omitted all mention of Mademoiselle Binet from this letter. I'm inclined to believe it's at least partly insincere that he attributed his actions at the Feydau entirely to his self-imposed mission. He didn't attribute them at all to his wounded feelings concerning Climene.

Both letters, written in April of 1789, had only one immediate effect. They intensified the search for André-Louis Moreau.

Le Chapelier would have found him to offer assistance, to urge him once again to pursue a political career. The electors of Nantes would have found him—or at least, they would have found Omnes Omnibus, whose true identity they still didn't know—on each of the several occasions when a vacancy occurred in their assembly. And the Marquis de La Tour d’Azyr and M. de Lesdiguieres would have found him to send him to the gallows.

M. Binet, now unhappily recovered from his wound and facing complete ruin, sought him with a purpose no less vindictive. His troupe had deserted him during his illness. Reconstituted under Polichinelle's direction, it was now striving with tolerable success to continue along the lines André-Louis had established. The Marquis, prevented by the riot from personally telling Mademoiselle Binet his intention to end their relationship, had been forced to write to her from Azyr a few days later. He softened the blow by enclosing a bill on the Caisse d’Escompte for a hundred louis, settling all liabilities. Nevertheless, it almost crushed the unfortunate woman. When her father recovered, he enraged her by pointing out that she owed this turn of events to her premature surrender, made in defiance of his sound worldly advice. Both father and daughter, naturally enough, attributed the Marquis's desertion to the riot at the Feydau. They blamed that, along with everything else, on Scaramouche. In bitterness, they were forced to admit that the scoundrel had taken a superb revenge. Climene may even have come to consider that it would have been better for her to have stayed true to Scaramouche. By marrying him, she could have trusted his undoubted talents to place her at the summit her ambition craved, a summit she now futilely aspired to. If so, that thought must have been punishment enough for her. For, as André-Louis so truly says, there is no worse hell than the regrets for wasted opportunities.

Meanwhile, the fiercely sought André-Louis Moreau had completely vanished for the time being. The efficient Paris police, urged on by the King's Lieutenant from Rennes, hunted for him in vain. Yet, he could have been found in a house on the Rue du Hasard, just a stone's throw from the Palais Royal, where pure chance had led him.

What he described in his letter to Le Chapelier as a future possibility was, in fact, his current situation. He was destitute. His money was gone, including what he got from selling any decorative items that weren't absolutely necessary.

His situation was so desperate that one gusty April morning, as he walked down the Rue du Hasard, sniffing the wind for any opportunity, he stopped. He read a notice outside a house door on the left side of the street, as you approach the Rue de Richelieu. There was no particular reason for him to have gone down the Rue du Hasard. Perhaps its name, "Street of Chance," appealed to him, fitting his circumstances.

The notice, written in a large, round hand, announced that M. Bertrand des Amis on the second floor needed a young man of good manners with some knowledge of swordsmanship. Above this notice was a black oblong board. On it was a shield, which, in simple terms, could be described as red, featuring two crossed swords and four fleurs-de-lys, one in each corner of the cross. Under the shield, in gold letters, read the legend:

BERTRAND DES AMIS
Maitre en fait d’Armes des Academies du Roi

André-Louis stood there, thinking. He thought he could claim to have the required qualifications. He was certainly young, and he believed his manners were acceptable. The fencing lessons he'd received in Nantes had given him at least an elementary knowledge of swordsmanship. The notice looked like it had been there for several days, suggesting that not many people had applied for the position. In that case, perhaps M. Bertrand des Amis wouldn't be too demanding. Anyway, André-Louis hadn't eaten for twenty-four hours. While the job offered here, the exact nature of which he still needed to find out, didn't seem like something André-Louis would deliberately choose, he was in no position to be picky now.

He also liked the name Bertrand des Amis. It happily combined suggestions of chivalry and friendliness. Also, since the man's profession had a touch of romance, it was possible that M. Bertrand des Amis wouldn't ask too many questions.

Finally, he climbed to the second floor. On the landing, he paused outside a door. It read, "Academy of M. Bertrand des Amis." He pushed it open and found himself in a sparsely furnished, empty antechamber. From a closed door in a room beyond, came the stamping of feet, the click and slither of steel on steel. Dominating these sounds was a vibrant, resonant voice speaking French—but a kind of French never heard outside a fencing school.

"Coulez! But flow, then! So! Now the flanconnade—en carte. And here is the riposte. Let's begin again. Come! The ward of tierce. Make the coupe, and then the quinte par dessus les armes. Oh, but lunge! Lunge! Go deep!" the voice cried in protest. "Come, that was better." The blades ceased.

"Remember: hand in pronation, elbow not too far out. That will do for today. On Wednesday, we'll see you tirer au mur. It's more deliberate. Speed will come when the movements' mechanism is more assured."

Another voice murmured in response. The steps moved away. The lesson was over. André-Louis tapped on the door.

A tall, slender, gracefully proportioned man, perhaps forty years old, opened it. From the waist down, he wore black silk breeches and stockings, ending in light shoes. Above, he was encased to the chin in a closely fitting leather plastron. His face was aquiline and swarthy, his eyes full and dark, his mouth firm. His clubbed hair was a lustrous black, with a few threads of silver showing here and there.

In the crook of his left arm, he carried a fencing mask—a leather item with a wire grating to protect the eyes. His keen glance swept over André-Louis from head to foot.

He inquired politely, "Monsieur?"

It was clear he mistook André-Louis's social standing. This wasn't surprising, because despite his sadly reduced fortunes, André-Louis's appearance was impeccable. M. des Amis couldn't have guessed that he carried all his worldly possessions on his back.

He said, "You have a notice downstairs, monsieur." From the swift lighting in the fencing master's eyes, André-Louis saw he had been correct in assuming that applicants for the position weren't exactly crowding the doorway. Then, that flash of satisfaction was followed by a look of surprise.

"You've come about that?"

André-Louis shrugged and half-smiled. "One must live," he said.

"But come in. Sit down there. I'll be with you... I'll be free to attend to you in a moment."

André-Louis took a seat on the bench against one of the whitewashed walls. The room was long and low, with a bare floor. Simple wooden benches, like the one he sat on, were placed here and there along the wall. The walls themselves were covered with fencing trophies: masks, crossed foils, padded plastrons, and a variety of swords, daggers, and targets from different eras and countries. There was also a portrait of an obese, big-nosed gentleman in an elaborately curled wig, wearing the blue ribbon of the Saint Esprit. André-Louis recognized him as the King. A framed parchment, M. des Amis’s certificate from the King’s Academy, hung nearby. A bookcase occupied one corner, and near it, facing the last of the four windows that brightly lit the long room, stood a small writing table and an armchair. A plump and beautifully dressed young gentleman stood by this table, in the process of putting on his coat and wig. M. des Amis sauntered over to him, moving, André-Louis thought, with extraordinary grace and elasticity. He chatted with the young gentleman while helping him finish getting dressed.

At last, the young gentleman left, dabbing himself with a fine handkerchief that left a trail of perfume in the air. M. des Amis closed the door and turned to André-Louis, who immediately rose.

“Where have you studied?” the fencing master asked abruptly.

“Studied?” André-Louis was surprised by the question. “Oh, at Louis Le Grand.”

M. des Amis frowned, looking up sharply as if to see whether his applicant was trying to be amusing.

“For Heaven’s sake! I’m not asking where you got your general education, but in what academy you studied fencing.”

“Oh, fencing!” It had hardly ever occurred to André-Louis that the sword was considered a serious subject. “I never studied it very much. I had some lessons in… in the country once.”

The master’s eyebrows went up. “Then why?” he exclaimed. “Why bother to come up two flights of stairs?” He was impatient.

“The notice doesn’t demand a high degree of proficiency. If I’m not proficient enough, I know the basics and can easily improve. I learn most things readily,” André-Louis said, praising himself. “As for the rest: I possess the other qualifications. I am young, as you can see, and I’ll let you judge whether I’m wrong in assuming that my manner is good. I am by profession a legal professional, though I realize that the motto here is *cedat toga armis*.”

M. des Amis smiled approvingly. Undoubtedly, the young man had a good bearing and a certain quickness of wit, it seemed. He ran a critical eye over André-Louis’s physical attributes. “What is your name?” he asked.

André-Louis hesitated a moment. “André-Louis,” he said.

The dark, keen eyes studied him more searchingly.

“Well? André-Louis what?”

“Just André-Louis. Louis is my surname.”

“Oh! An odd surname. You come from Brittany by your accent. Why did you leave it?”

“To save my skin,” he answered, without thinking. Then he quickly tried to correct his mistake. “I have an enemy,” he explained.

M. des Amis frowned, stroking his square chin. “You ran away?”

“You may say so.”

“A coward, eh?”

“I don’t think so.” Then he told a romantic lie. Surely a man who lived by the sword would have a weakness for the romantic. “You see, my enemy is a swordsman of great strength—the best blade in the province, if not the best blade in France. That is his reputation. I thought I would come to Paris to learn something of the art, and then go back and kill him. That, to be frank, is why your notice attracted me. You see, I have no money to take lessons otherwise. I thought to find work here in the law. But I have failed. There are too many lawyers in Paris as it is, and while waiting, I have spent the little money I had, so that… so that, ultimately, your notice seemed to me something to which a special providence had directed me.”

M. des Amis gripped him by the shoulders and looked into his face.

“Is this true, my friend?” he asked.

“Not a word of it,” said André-Louis, ruining his chances, driven by an irresistible urge to say the unexpected. But he hadn’t ruined them at all. M. des Amis burst into laughter; and after laughing heartily, he admitted he was charmed by his applicant’s fundamental honesty.

“Take off your coat,” he said, “and let us see what you can do. Nature, at least, designed you for a swordsman. You are light, active, and supple, with a good length of arm, and you seem intelligent. I may train you well, teach you enough for my purpose, which is that you should give the elements of the art to new pupils before I take them in hand to refine their skills. Let us try. Take that mask and foil, and come over here.”

He led him to the end of the room, where the bare floor was scored with chalk lines to guide the beginner in managing their feet.

At the end of a ten-minute bout, M. des Amis offered him the position and explained it. In addition to teaching the basics of the art to beginners, he was to brush out the fencing room every morning, keep the foils polished, assist the gentlemen who came for lessons to dress and undress, and make himself generally useful. His wages for the present were to be forty livres a month, and he might sleep in an alcove behind the fencing room if he had no other lodging.

The position, you see, had its humiliations. But, if André-Louis wanted to eat, he had to begin by swallowing his pride first.

“And so,” he said, forcing back a grimace, “my legal profession gives way not only to the sword, but to the broom as well. So be it. I stay.”

True to his nature, once he made that choice, he threw himself into the work with enthusiasm. It was always his way to do whatever he did with his full mental and physical effort. When he was not instructing very young gentlemen in the elements of the art, showing them the elaborate and intricate salute—which he had mastered to perfection with a few days’ hard practice—and the eight guards, he was himself hard at work on those same guards, exercising his eye, wrist, and knees.

Perceiving his enthusiasm, and seeing the obvious possibilities it offered for making him a truly effective assistant, M. des Amis presently began to train him more seriously.

"Your hard work and dedication, my friend, are worth more than forty livres a month," the master told him at the end of a week. "For now, however, I will make up what else I consider due to you by sharing the secrets of this noble art. Your future depends on how well you profit from the exceptional good fortune of receiving instruction from me."

From then on, every morning before the academy opened, the master would fence for half an hour with his new assistant. Under this truly excellent instruction, André-Louis improved at a rate that both amazed and pleased Monsieur des Amis. He would have been less pleased and more amazed if he had known that at least half the secret of André-Louis' incredible progress lay in the fact that he was eagerly reading everything in the master's library. This library contained about a dozen books on fencing by great masters like La Bessiere, Danet, and Augustin Rousseau, the syndic of the King's Academy. Monsieur des Amis, whose swordsmanship was entirely based on practice and not at all on theory—indeed, he was no theorist or scholar in any sense—saw that little library as merely a suitable addition to a fencing academy, a decorative piece of furniture. The books themselves meant nothing else to him. He didn't have the kind of mind that could learn from them, nor could he understand why anyone else would. André-Louis, on the contrary, was a man who loved to study and had a knack for learning from books. He read those works with great benefit. He kept their principles in mind, critically compared the ideas of different masters, and then chose what he would put into practice.

At the end of a month, Monsieur des Amis suddenly realized that his assistant had become a very strong fencer. He was someone Monsieur des Amis had to really try hard against to avoid losing a match.

"I said from the beginning," he told him one day, "that Nature made you to be a swordsman. See how right I was, and also how well I've managed to shape the raw talent Nature gave you."

"To the master be the glory," said André-Louis.

Meanwhile, his relationship with Monsieur des Amis had become very friendly. André-Louis was now starting to receive more advanced pupils from him, not just beginners. In fact, André-Louis was becoming an assistant in a much more complete sense. Monsieur des Amis, a generous, open-hearted man, instead of taking advantage of what he suspected were the young man's financial difficulties, rewarded his dedication by raising his wages to four louis a month.

After earnestly and thoughtfully studying the theories of others, André-Louis began to develop his own ideas, as often happens. One June morning, he lay on his small trundle bed in the alcove behind the academy, thinking about a passage he had read last night in Danet's book about double and triple feints. When he read it, it had seemed to him that Danet had stopped just short of a great discovery in the art of fencing. As a natural theorist, André-Louis grasped the underlying theory that Danet had hinted at but hadn't fully understood himself. He lay now on his back, looking at the cracks in the ceiling and thinking about this further with the clear-headedness that early morning often brings to a sharp mind. Remember that for nearly two months now, the sword had been André-Louis' daily exercise and almost constant thought. This prolonged focus on the subject was giving him an extraordinary insight. Swordsmanship, as he learned, taught, and saw it practiced daily, consisted of a series of attacks and parries, a series of disengages from one line to another. But it was always a limited series. Strictly speaking, an engagement usually went no further than half a dozen disengages on either side. Then it would start over. Even then, these disengages were accidental. What if they were calculated from beginning to end?

That was one part of his theory, one of its two foundations. The other was this: what if one developed Danet's ideas on the triple feint so thoroughly that they merged into a series of actual, calculated disengages, culminating at the fourth, fifth, or even sixth disengage? In other words, what if you made a series of attacks, inviting ripostes that would then be countered? Each of these wasn't meant to land, but simply to guide the opponent's blade into a position that would ultimately, and as planned, leave them open for an irresistible lunge. Every counter by the opponent would have to be anticipated in this gradual widening of their guard. This widening would be so subtle that they wouldn't even notice it, all the while focused on landing their own point with one of those counters.

André-Louis had once been a strong chess player, and he excelled because he could think many moves ahead. Applying that skill to fencing would almost revolutionize the art. Of course, it was already applied in fencing, but only in a basic and very limited way, through simple, double, or triple feints. But even the triple feint would seem clumsy compared to the method he was theorizing about.

He considered further, and the conviction grew that he held the key to a discovery. He was impatient to put his theory to the test.

That morning, he was given a strong pupil, who usually gave him a tough time defending himself. As he took his guard, he decided to hit his opponent on the fourth disengage, planning out the four moves that would lead to it. They began in tierce, and André-Louis led the attack with a beat and a straightening of his arm. The demi-contre he expected came, which he promptly countered with a thrust in quinte. This was countered again, so he reentered still lower. When it was correctly parried again, just as he had calculated, he lunged, swirling his point into carte, and landed it squarely on his opponent's chest. The ease with which he did it surprised him.

They began again. This time he decided to land a hit on the fifth disengage, and he did so with the same ease. Then, complicating the matter further, he decided to try the sixth, and mentally worked out the combination of the five preliminary engagements. Once again, he succeeded just as easily as before.

The young gentleman he was fencing against laughed, with just a hint of embarrassment in his voice.

"I'm completely off my game this morning," he said.

"You're not at your usual strength," André-Louis politely agreed. Then, daring greatly, always wanting to test his theory to the absolute limit, he added, "So much so that I could almost be sure of hitting you exactly when and where I say I will."

The skilled pupil looked at him with a slight sneer. He said, "Ah, no, not that."

"Let us try. On the fourth disengage, I'll touch you. Come on! En garde!"

And as he promised, so it happened.

The young gentleman, who, until now, hadn't thought much of André-Louis' swordsmanship, considering him only good enough for practice when the master was busy, had his eyes widened. In a burst of mixed generosity and excitement, André-Louis was almost ready to reveal his method. This was a method that would later become common in fencing rooms. But he stopped himself just in time. To reveal his secret would destroy the prestige he would gain from using it.

At noon, with the academy empty, Monsieur des Amis called André-Louis for one of the occasional lessons he still gave him. And for the first time in all his experience with André-Louis, Monsieur des Amis took a full hit from him during the first bout. He laughed, quite pleased, like the generous man he was.

"Aha! You're improving very fast, my friend." He still laughed, though not quite as pleased, when he was hit in the second bout. After that, he settled down to fight seriously, and André-Louis was hit three times in a row. The fencing master's speed and accuracy, when he was really trying, threw off André-Louis' theory. It still required too much thought because he hadn't practiced it enough.

But he considered his theory fully proven and sound. And for the moment, he was content with that. All that remained was to perfect its application through practice. He now dedicated himself to this with the passionate enthusiasm of a discoverer. He focused on about half a dozen combinations, practicing them diligently until each became almost automatic. And he proved their effectiveness against the best of Monsieur des Amis' pupils.

Finally, a week or so after that last bout of his with des Amis, the master called him once more to practice.

Hit again in the first bout, the master resolved to use all his skill against his assistant. But today, it did him no good against André-Louis' impetuous attacks.

After the third hit, Monsieur des Amis stepped back and pulled off his mask.

He asked, "What's this?" He was pale, and his dark brows were furrowed in a frown. He hadn't been so wounded in his pride in years. "Have you been taught a secret botte?"

He had always boasted that he knew too much about swordsmanship to believe any nonsense about secret bottes, but André-Louis' performance had shaken his beliefs on that point.

André-Louis said, "No. I have been working hard, and it happens that I fence with my brains."

"So I see. Well, well, I think I've taught you enough, my friend. I have no intention of having an assistant who is better than me."

André-Louis said, smiling pleasantly, "Little danger of that. You've been fencing hard all morning, and you're tired, while I, having done little, am entirely fresh. That is the only secret of my momentary success."

André-Louis's tact, combined with M. des Amis's fundamental good nature, prevented the situation from escalating further. After that, whenever they fenced, André-Louis continued to perfect his theory into an almost infallible system. He made sure M. des Amis always scored at least two hits against him for every one of his own. He allowed this much out of discretion, but no more. He wanted M. des Amis to be aware of his strength, but without discovering its true extent. That would have only made M. des Amis unnecessarily jealous.

He managed things so well that he became an even greater help to the master. His style and overall fencing had also significantly improved. He was also a source of pride to M. des Amis, considered the most brilliant pupil ever to pass through the academy. André-Louis never disillusioned him by revealing that his skill came much more from M. des Amis's library and his own natural cleverness than from any lessons he had received.
