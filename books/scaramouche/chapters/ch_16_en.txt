Chapter 7. THE CONQUEST OF NANTES

The Binet Troupe opened in Nantes on the Feast of the Purification with "Les Fourberies de Scaramouche." You can find this in surviving copies of the "Courrier Nantais." But they didn't arrive in Nantes as they had in small country villages and towns until then, without any advance notice, relying entirely on their entrance parade to attract attention. André-Louis had adopted business methods from the Comédie Française. Taking charge decisively and in his own way, he had ordered playbills printed in Redon. Four days before the company's arrival in Nantes, these bills were pasted outside the Théâtre Feydau and elsewhere around town. They attracted considerable attention, as such announcements were still quite unusual at the time. He had entrusted this task to one of the company's newest recruits, an intelligent young man named Basque, sending him ahead of the company for that purpose.

You can see one of these playbills yourself in the Carnavalet Museum. It lists the performers only by their stage names, except for M. Binet and his daughter. It also doesn't mention that the actor playing Trivelin in one show might appear as Tabarin in another. This made the company seem much larger than it actually was. The bill announced that they would open with "Les Fourberies de Scaramouche," followed by five other titled plays. It also mentioned unnamed plays that would be added if the support they received in the distinguished and enlightened city of Nantes encouraged the Binet Troupe to extend their stay at the Théâtre Feydau. The playbill heavily emphasized that this was a company of improvisers in the old Italian style, a type of performance not seen in France for half a century. It urged the public of Nantes not to miss this chance to see these distinguished mimes, who were reviving the glories of the Comédie de l'Art for them. The announcement continued, stating that their visit to Nantes was a prelude to their visit to Paris. There, they intended to challenge the actors of the Comédie Française and demonstrate to the world how superior the art of the improviser was to that of an actor who relies on a writer for their lines, and who, as a result, always says the same thing every time they perform the same play.

It was an audacious playbill, and its boldness had terrified M. Binet, leaving him with even less sense than the Burgundy he could now afford to drink excessively. He had vehemently opposed it. André-Louis had dismissed some of his objections and simply ignored the rest.

"I admit it's audacious," Scaramouche said. "But at your age, you should have learned that nothing succeeds like audacity in this world."

"I forbid it; I absolutely forbid it," M. Binet insisted.

"I knew you would," Scaramouche replied. "Just as I know you'll be very grateful to me soon for not obeying you."

"You are inviting a catastrophe."

"I am inviting fortune," Scaramouche declared. "The worst catastrophe that can happen to you is ending up back in the market halls of the country villages from which I rescued you. I'll get you to Paris yet, despite yourself. Leave this to me."

He then went out to oversee the printing. His preparations didn't end there. He wrote a witty and engaging article about the glories of the Comédie de l'Art and its revival by the improvising troupe of the great mime, Florimond Binet. Binet's actual name wasn't Florimond; it was just Pierre. But André-Louis had a great sense of the theater. That article expanded on the exciting content of the playbills. He persuaded Basque, who had relatives in Nantes, to use all the influence he could, and all the bribery they could afford, to get that article printed in the "Courrier Nantais" a couple of days before the Binet Troupe arrived.

Basque succeeded, and considering the article's clear literary quality and inherent interest, this was not at all surprising.

And so, in that first week of February, Binet and his company arrived in an already expectant city. M. Binet would have made his entrance in the usual way: a full-dress parade with banging drums and crashing cymbals. But André-Louis offered the fiercest opposition to this idea.

"We would only reveal how poor we are," he said. "Instead, we will slip into the city unnoticed and let the public imagine us."

He got his way, of course. M. Binet, already exhausted from fighting against this young man's strong will, was completely unable to resist now that Climene was allied with Scaramouche. She added her demands to his, joining him in criticizing her father's slow and old-fashioned thinking. M. Binet, in a sense, threw up his arms. Cursing the day he had taken this young man into his troupe, he let the current carry him wherever it would. He was convinced he would be drowned in the end. Meanwhile, he would drown his frustration in Burgundy. At least there was plenty of Burgundy. Never in his life had he found Burgundy so plentiful. Perhaps things weren't as bad as he imagined, after all. He reflected that, after all, he had Scaramouche to thank for the Burgundy. While fearing the worst, he would hope for the best.

And it was very much the worst that he feared as he waited in the wings. The curtain rose on their first performance at the Théâtre Feydau to a reasonably full house. The advance announcements had thoroughly piqued the public's curiosity.

Although the plot outline of "Les Fourberies de Scaramouche" doesn't seem to have survived, we know from André-Louis' "Confessions" that it opens with Polichinelle. He plays an arrogant and fiercely jealous lover, shown trying to trick the waiting-maid, Columbine, into spying on her mistress, Climene. He starts with flattery, but this fails with the sassy Columbine. She prefers flatterers to be at least attractive and to show proper respect for her own very charming looks. So, the fierce, hunchbacked scoundrel moves on to threats of terrible vengeance he will inflict upon her if she betrays him or fails to obey him completely. Failing here, too, he finally resorts to bribery. After he generously pays the very expectant Columbine, he succeeds by these methods in getting her to agree to spy on Climene and report back to him about her lady's behavior.

The pair played the scene well together, perhaps stimulated by their nervousness at finding themselves before such an imposing audience. Polichinelle was fierce, contemptuous, and insistent. Columbine was the essence of sassy indifference under his flattery, cheekily mocking under his threats, and cleverly sly in getting the most possible when it came to accepting a bribe. Laughter rippled through the audience, a good sign. But M. Binet, standing trembling in the wings, missed the loud guffaws of the country audiences they had played to until then, and his fears steadily grew.

Then, no sooner had Polichinelle left through the door than Scaramouche bounded in through the window. It was an effective entrance, usually performed with an exaggerated comic effect that made people roar with laughter. Not this time. Thinking in bed that morning, Scaramouche had decided to present himself in a totally different way. He would cut out all the obvious antics, all the usual clowning that had delighted their previous unsophisticated audiences. Instead, he would achieve his impact through subtlety. He would portray a slyly humorous rogue, restrained and with a certain dignity, wearing a completely serious expression. He would speak his lines dryly, as if unaware of the humor he intended to infuse into them. Thus, even if it took the audience longer to understand and appreciate him, they would like him even more in the end.

True to that decision, he now played his part as the friend and hired ally of the lovesick Leander. He came on Leander's behalf for news of Climene, seizing the opportunity to advance his own romance with Columbine and his plans for Pantaloon's money. He had also taken some liberties with Scaramouche's traditional costume. He had the black doublet and breeches slashed with red, and the doublet cut to a sharper point, in the style of Henri III. He had replaced the conventional black velvet cap with a conical hat that had a turned-up brim and a tuft of feathers on the left. He had also gotten rid of the guitar.

M. Binet listened anxiously for the roar of laughter that usually greeted Scaramouche's entrance, and his dismay grew when it didn't come. Then he noticed something alarmingly unusual in Scaramouche's manner. The hissing foreign accent was there, but none of the loud boisterousness their audiences had loved.

He wrung his hands in despair. "It is all over!" he said. "The fellow has ruined us! I deserve this for being a fool and allowing him to take control of everything!"

But he was completely mistaken. He began to get a hint of this when he himself took the stage a little later. He found the audience attentive and noticed a grin of quiet appreciation on every upturned face. However, it wasn't until thunderous applause greeted the fall of the curtain on the first act that he felt quite sure they would be allowed to leave alive.

If Pantaloon's part in "Les Fourberies" had been anything other than a blundering, timid old idiot, Binet would have ruined it with his anxieties. As it was, those very anxieties amplified the hesitancy and bewilderment that were central to his role, actually contributing to the success. And it proved to be a success that more than justified all the advance publicity Scaramouche had orchestrated.

For Scaramouche himself, this success wasn't limited to the audience. At the end of the play, a great reception awaited him from his companions assembled in the theater's green room. His talent, resourcefulness, and energy had, in a few weeks, transformed them from a group of wandering street performers into a respected company of top-notch actors. They acknowledged this generously in a speech given by Polichinelle, adding a tribute to his genius: that just as they had conquered Nantes, they would conquer the world under his guidance.

In their enthusiasm, they were a little inconsiderate of M. Binet's feelings. He was already irritated enough by having his every wish overridden and by his awareness of his own weakness when opposing Scaramouche. And, although he had endured the gradual takeover of his authority because each step had brought him greater profit, deep down, resentment lingered, stifling any spark of gratitude he owed his partner. Tonight, his nerves had been on edge, and he had suffered agonies of anxiety. He blamed Scaramouche so bitterly for all of it that not even the ultimate success, which was almost miraculous considering all the factors, could justify his partner in his eyes.

And now, to find himself, in addition, ignored by this company, his *own* company, which he had so painstakingly assembled and selected from talented individuals he'd found in the less reputable parts of cities, stirred his anger and awakened the malice that usually only slumbered within him. Though his rage was deep, it didn't blind him to the foolishness of showing it. Yet, asserting himself at this moment was crucial. Otherwise, he would forever become insignificant in this troupe, a troupe he had led for months before this newcomer arrived to enrich himself and undermine Binet's authority.

So he stepped forward now, after Polichinelle had finished. His makeup helped him hide his bitter feelings. He claimed to add his own praise to Polichinelle's acclamations for his dear partner. But he did it in a way that made it clear Scaramouche's actions were thanks to M. Binet's favor, and that M. Binet's hand had guided everything. Aligning himself with Polichinelle, he wanted to thank Scaramouche, much like a lord thanking his steward for diligently performed services and scrupulously followed orders.

This neither fooled the troupe nor appeased him. In fact, his awareness of the mockery only increased his bitterness. But at least it saved his reputation and rescued him from insignificance, he who was their chief.

To say, as I have said, that it didn't deceive them is perhaps an overstatement, because it did deceive them regarding his true feelings. After ignoring the insinuations where he took all the credit for himself, they believed he was genuinely filled with gratitude, just like them. André-Louis himself shared that belief. In his brief, grateful reply, he was very generous to M. Binet, more than endorsing the claims M. Binet had made.

Then he announced that their success in Nantes was the sweeter to him because it made his dearest wish almost immediately possible: to make Climene his wife. He was the first to admit he was completely unworthy of such happiness. This would also bring him even closer to his good friend, Monsieur Binet, to whom he owed everything he had achieved for himself and for the company. The announcement was joyfully received. The world of the theater loves a lover just as much as the wider world does. So they cheered the happy couple, all except for poor Leandre, whose eyes looked more melancholy than ever.

That night, they were a happy family in the upstairs room of their inn on the Quai La Fosse. It was the same inn from which André-Louis had left weeks ago to play a vastly different role before a Nantes audience. Yet, he wondered, was it really so different? Hadn't he been a kind of Scaramouche back then? An intriguer, glib and deceptive, misleading people and cynically guiding them with opinions that weren't truly his own? Was it surprising at all that he had achieved such rapid and notable success as a mime? Wasn't this truly all he had ever been? The very thing Nature had designed him for?

The following night, they played "The Shy Lover" to a full house. News of their debut had spread, and Monday's success was confirmed. On Wednesday, they performed "Figaro-Scaramouche." Then, on Thursday morning, the "Courrier Nantais" published an article. It was over a column long and praised these brilliant improvisers, claiming they completely shamed those who merely recited memorized lines.

André-Louis read the newspaper at breakfast. He had no illusions about the falseness of the statement, and he laughed to himself. The novelty of their act, and the pretentiousness he had wrapped it in, had fooled them perfectly. He turned to greet Binet and Climene, who entered at that moment. He waved the newspaper above his head.

He announced, "It's settled. We're staying in Nantes until Easter."

"Are we?" Binet said sourly. "You settle everything, my friend."

"Read for yourself." He handed Binet the paper.

Monsieur Binet read moodily. He set the newspaper down in silence and turned his attention to his breakfast.

"Was I justified or not?" André-Louis asked. He found Monsieur Binet's behavior rather intriguing.

"In what?"

"In coming to Nantes?"

"If I hadn't thought so, we wouldn't have come," Binet said, and he began to eat.

André-Louis dropped the subject, wondering.

After breakfast, he and Climene went out to get some fresh air on the quays. It was a day of brilliant sunshine, less cold than it had been recently. Columbine tactlessly joined them as they were setting out. However, things improved a little when Harlequin came running after them and attached himself to Columbine.

Stepping out ahead with Climene, André-Louis spoke of what was most on his mind at that moment.

"Your father is behaving very oddly towards me," he said. "It's almost as if he's suddenly become hostile."

"You're imagining it," she said. "My father is very grateful to you, as we all are."

"He's anything but grateful. He's furious with me, and I think I know why. Don't you? Can't you guess?"

"I truly can't."

"If you were my daughter, Climene, and thank God you are not, I would feel aggrieved against the man who carried you away from me. Poor old Pantaloon! He called me a corsair when I told him I intend to marry you."

"He was right. You are a bold robber, Scaramouche."

"It's in the character," he said. "Your father believes in having his mimes play parts on stage that suit their natural temperaments."

"Yes, you take everything you want, don't you?" She looked up at him, half adoringly, half shyly.

"If it's possible," he said. "I took his consent to our marriage by force. I never waited for him to give it. In fact, when he refused, I just snatched it from him. Now I'll defy him to win it back from me. I think that's what he resents most."

She laughed and launched into an animated answer. But he didn't hear a word of it. Through the busy traffic on the quay, a cabriolet approached them. Its upper half was almost entirely made of glass. It was drawn by two magnificent bay horses and driven by a superbly lively coachman.

Alone in the cabriolet sat a slight young girl. She was wrapped in a lynx-fur pelisse, and her face had a delicate loveliness. She was leaning forward, her lips parted, her eyes devouring Scaramouche until they caught his gaze. When that happened, the shock of it brought him to an abrupt, dumbfounded halt.

Climene stopped mid-sentence, startled by his sudden halt, and plucked at his sleeve.

"What is it, Scaramouche?"

But he made no attempt to answer her. At that moment, the coachman, to whom the young lady had already signaled, brought the carriage to a standstill beside them. The coach itself was a gorgeous setting, with its escutcheoned panels, its portly coachman, and its white-stockinged footman. The footman swung instantly to the ground as the vehicle stopped. To Climene, its dainty occupant seemed like a princess out of a fairy tale. And this princess leaned forward, her eyes aglow and cheeks flushed, stretching out a choicely gloved hand to Scaramouche.

"André-Louis!" she called.

Scaramouche took the remarkable woman's hand, just as he might have taken Climene's. His eyes reflected her own joy, and his voice echoed her joyful surprise. Then, he called her by her first name, just as she had called him.

"Aline!"
