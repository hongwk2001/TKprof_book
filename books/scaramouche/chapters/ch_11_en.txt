[Chapter 2. THE SERVICE OF THESPIS]

André-Louis thought they were an odd yet attractive group. He sat down to breakfast with them behind their traveling wagon. The bright sunshine softened the cold air of that November morning. A cheerful atmosphere filled them. They pretended to have no worries and joked about the difficulties of their nomadic life. They were strangely, yet amiably, artificial. They were theatrical even when doing everyday things, exaggerated in their gestures, and formal and unnatural in their speech. They seemed, indeed, to belong to a world apart, a world of unreality that only became real on the planks of their stage, under the glare of their footlights. A strong bond of camaraderie held them together. André-Louis cynically reflected that this harmony might be the reason for their apparent unreality. In the real world, greedy ambition and the pursuit of possessions prevent the kind of friendship they shared.

There were exactly eleven of them: three women and eight men. They addressed each other by their stage names. These names indicated their specific roles and rarely, if ever, changed, no matter what play they performed.

Pantaloon informed him, "We are one of those few remaining staunch bands of real players. We uphold the traditions of the old Italian Commedia dell' Arte. We don't strain our memories or dull our minds with the awkward lines that come from some poor author's tedious work. Each of us, in a way, writes our own part as we develop the character assigned to us. We are improvisers—improvisers of the old and noble Italian school."

"I had guessed as much," André-Louis said, "when I discovered you rehearsing your improvisations."

Pantaloon frowned.

"I have observed, young sir, that your humor tends to be sharp, not to say bitter. That's fine. It's, I suppose, the kind of humor that suits your expression. But it might mislead you, as it did just now. That rehearsal—a most unusual thing for us—was necessary because of our Leandre's theatrical inexperience. We are trying to teach him, through training, an art that Nature failed to give him for his current role. Should he continue to fail to live up to our training... But we won't disturb our present harmony with the unpleasant thought of troubles we still hope to avoid. We love our Leandre, despite all his faults. Let me introduce you to our company."

He then proceeded to introduce everyone in detail. He pointed out the tall and friendly Rhodomont, whom André-Louis already knew.

"His tall build and hooked nose were the obvious reasons he played booming captains," Pantaloon explained. "His lungs have justified our choice. You should hear him roar. At first, we called him Spavento or Epouvapte. But that was unworthy of such a great artist. Not since the superb Mondor amazed the world has such a boastful bully been seen on stage. So we gave him the name Rhodomont, which Mondor made famous. And I give you my word, as an actor and a gentleman—for I am a gentleman, monsieur, or was—that he has justified us."

His small eyes beamed in his large, swollen face as he looked at the person he was praising. The terrible Rhodomont, confused by so much praise, blushed like a schoolgirl as he met André-Louis's serious gaze.

"Then here we have Scaramouche, whom you also already know. Sometimes he is Scapin and sometimes Coviello, but mostly Scaramouche. Let me tell you, he is best suited to that role—sometimes too well suited, I think. For he is Scaramouche not only on stage but also in the world. He has a gift for sly intrigue, an art for causing arguments among people, combined with a bold aggression sometimes, when he thinks he's safe from consequences. He is Scaramouche, the little skirmisher, perfectly. I could say more. But I am naturally kind and loving to everyone."

"As the priest said when he kissed the maid," Scaramouche snarled, and went on eating.

"His humor, like your own, you will observe, is bitter," said Pantaloon. He moved on. "Then that rascal with the lumpy nose and the grinning, rustic face is, of course, Pierrot. Could he be anyone else?"

"I could play lovers much better," said the rustic cherub.

"That is the typical delusion of Pierrot," Pantaloon said contemptuously. "This heavy, scowling brute, who has grown old in mischief and whose appetite grows with age, is Polichinelle. Each one, as you see, is naturally suited for the part he plays. This agile, freckled rascal is Harlequin. Not the sparkly Harlequin that modern decline has turned the original trickster into, but the true, original clown of the Commedia: ragged and patched, a cheeky, cowardly, villainous clown."

"Each one of us, as you see," said Harlequin, mimicking the troupe leader, "is naturally suited for the part he plays."

"Physically, my friend, only physically. Otherwise, we wouldn't have so much trouble teaching this handsome Leandre to be a lover. Then we have Pasquariel here, who sometimes plays an apothecary, sometimes a notary, sometimes a servant—an amiable, accommodating fellow. He is also an excellent cook, being from Italy, that land of hearty eaters. And finally, you have myself. As the father of the company, I rightly play father roles as Pantaloon. Sometimes, it's true, I am a deceived husband, and sometimes an ignorant, arrogant doctor. But I rarely find it necessary to call myself anything other than Pantaloon. For the rest, I am the only one who has a name—a real name. It is Binet, monsieur."

"And now for the ladies... First, in order of seniority, we have Madame over there." He waved one of his large hands towards a plump, smiling blonde woman of forty-five, who was seated on the lowest step of the traveling wagon. "She is our Duègne, or Mother, or Nurse, depending on the play. She is known quite simply and royally as Madame. If she ever had a name in the world, she has long since forgotten it, which is perhaps just as well. Then we have this lively young woman with the turned-up nose and the wide mouth, who is, of course, our maidservant Columbine. And lastly, my daughter Climene, a lover-character with talents unmatched outside the Comédie Française, which she, unfortunately, wants to join."

The lovely Climene—and lovely indeed she was—shook her brown curls and laughed as she looked across at André-Louis. Her eyes, he had noticed by now, were not blue, but hazel.

"Do not believe him, monsieur. Here I am queen, and I prefer to be queen here rather than a slave in Paris."

"Mademoiselle," André-Louis said quite solemnly, "will be queen wherever she chooses to reign."

Her only answer was a shy yet alluring glance from under fluttering eyelids. Meanwhile, her father was shouting at the handsome young man who played lovers, "You hear, Leandre! That is the sort of speech you should practice."

Leandre raised languid eyebrows. "That?" he said, and shrugged. "Just a common remark."

André-Louis laughed in approval. "Mr. Leandre is wittier than you give him credit for. There's a subtle point in calling it a 'commonplace' to refer to Miss Climene as a queen."

Some laughed, Mr. Binet among them, with good-natured teasing.

"You think he has the wit to mean it that way? Nonsense! His cleverness is all accidental."

As the conversation became general, André-Louis soon learned what else there was to know about this traveling troupe. They were on their way to Guichen, where they hoped to do well at the fair opening next Monday. They would make their grand entry into town at noon. Setting up their stage in the old market square, they would give their first performance that same Saturday night. It would be a new canevas, or scenario, written by Mr. Binet himself, which would make the country folk gape. Then Mr. Binet sighed and spoke to the elderly, dark-skinned, scowling Polichinelle, who sat on his left.

"But we shall miss Felicien," he said. "Indeed, I don't know what we'll do without him."

"Oh, we'll manage," said Polichinelle, with his mouth full.

"You always say that, no matter what happens, knowing that you won't be the one who has to figure it out."

Harlequin said, "He should not be difficult to replace."

"True, if we were in a civilized place. But where among the country folk of Brittany can we find someone with even his limited skills?" M. Binet turned to André-Louis. "He was our property master, our machinist, our stage carpenter, our business manager, and sometimes he acted."

André-Louis said, "The part of Figaro, I presume," which made everyone laugh.

"So you know Beaumarchais!" Binet looked at the young man with new interest.

"He's quite well known, I think."

"In Paris, certainly. But I hadn't imagined his fame had reached the wilds of Brittany."

"But then, I spent some years in Paris, at the Lycée of Louis le Grand. That's where I became familiar with his work."

Polichinelle said, with a solemn air, "A dangerous man."

"Indeed, you are right," Pantaloon agreed. "Clever, I don't deny him that, though I personally find little use for authors. But it's a sinister cleverness, responsible for spreading many of these rebellious new ideas. I think such writers should be silenced."

"Monsieur de La Tour d'Azyr would probably agree with you. He's the gentleman who, by simply asserting his will, turns this communal land into his own property." André-Louis then drained his cup, which had been filled with the cheap vin gris, the players' usual drink.

That remark might have started an argument, but it also reminded Monsieur Binet of the conditions under which they were camped there. It also reminded him that the half-hour was long past. In a moment, he was on his feet, leaping up with surprising agility for such a stout man. He began issuing commands like a general on a battlefield.

"Come on, my lads! Are we going to sit here drinking all day? Time is flying, and there's a lot to do if we want to enter Guichen by noon. Go, get dressed. We're breaking camp in twenty minutes. Hurry, ladies! Get to your carriage, and make sure you manage to look your best. Soon, the eyes of Guichen will be on you, and how you feel tomorrow will depend on the impression you make today. Away! Away!"

The absolute obedience this autocrat commanded sent them into a flurry of activity. Baskets and boxes were pulled out to hold the platters and leftovers from their meager meal. In an instant, the ground was cleared. The three ladies had already left for the carriage, which was reserved for them. The men were already climbing into the house on wheels when Binet turned to André-Louis.

He said dramatically, "We part here, sir, richer for having known you; your debtors and your friends." He extended his plump hand.

André-Louis slowly took his hand. He had been thinking quickly in the last few moments. He remembered the safety he had found from his pursuers within this company. It occurred to him that he couldn't be better hidden anywhere else for now, until the search for him had faded.

He said, "Sir, the debt is on my side. It's not every day one has the pleasure of sitting with such an illustrious and engaging company."

Binet's small eyes peered suspiciously at the young man, looking for irony. He found nothing but honesty and simple sincerity.

André-Louis continued, "I leave you reluctantly. Even more reluctantly, since I don't see any absolute need for us to part."

Binet frowned and said, "How?" He slowly withdrew his hand, which André-Louis had held for a moment longer than necessary.

André-Louis explained, "Here's how. You can think of me as a sort of knight of the sorrowful face, looking for adventure, with no fixed purpose in life right now. You won't be surprised that what I've seen of you and your distinguished troupe inspires me to want to get to know you better. On your side, you tell me you need someone to replace your Figaro, your Felicien, I think you called him. While it might be presumptuous of me to hope that I could handle such a varied and demanding role..."

Binet interrupted him. "You're indulging that sharp wit of yours again, my friend." He added slowly and thoughtfully, his small eyes narrowed, "Except for that, we might discuss this proposal you seem to be making."

"Alas! We can't make any exceptions. If you take me, you take me as I am. What else is possible? As for this wit, such as it is, which you criticize, you might turn it to good use."

"How so?"

"In several ways. I might, for instance, teach Leander how to make love."

Pantaloon burst into laughter. "You certainly don't lack confidence in your abilities. Modesty doesn't seem to trouble you."

"Therefore, I demonstrate the first quality necessary in an actor."

"Can you act?"

André-Louis said, "On occasion, I think." His thoughts were on his performances at Rennes and Nantes, and he wondered when in all his acting career Pantaloon's improvisations had so moved the hearts of crowds.

Monsieur Binet was deep in thought. He asked, "Do you know much about the theater?"

“Everything,” said André-Louis.

“I said that modesty will prove no obstacle in your career.”

“But consider. I know the work of Beaumarchais, Eglantine, Mercier, Chenier, and many other contemporary writers. Of course, I’ve also read Moliere, Racine, Corneille, and many other lesser French authors. As for foreign authors, I’m well-acquainted with the works of Gozzi, Goldoni, Guarini, Bibbiena, Machiavelli, Secchi, Tasso, Ariosto, and Fedini. And from antiquity, I know most of the work of Euripides, Aristophanes, Terence, and Plautus...”

“Enough!” roared Pantaloon.

“I am not nearly through with my list,” said André-Louis.

“You may keep the rest for another day. In Heaven’s name, what could have prompted you to read so many dramatic authors?”

“Humbly speaking, I am a student of humanity. Some years ago, I discovered that people are best studied through the reflections of them presented in the theatre.”

“That is a very original and profound discovery,” Pantaloon said, quite seriously. “It had never occurred to me. And yet, it’s true. Sir, it’s a truth that dignifies our art. You are a man of many talents; that’s clear to me. It’s been clear since I first met you. I can read a man. I knew you from the moment you said ‘good morning.’ Tell me, now: Do you think you could sometimes help me prepare a scenario? My mind is fully engaged with a thousand organizational details. It’s not always as clear as I’d like for this kind of work. Could you assist me there, do you think?”

“I am quite sure I could.”

“Hmm, yes. I was sure you would be. You would soon learn Felicien’s other duties. Well, well, if you are willing, you may come along with us. You’d want some salary, I suppose?”

“If it is usual,” said André-Louis.

“What would you say to ten livres a month?”

“I should say that it isn’t exactly the riches of Peru.”

“I might go as far as fifteen,” Binet said, reluctantly. “But times are bad.”

“I’ll make them better for you.”

“I’ve no doubt you believe it. Then we understand each other?”

“Perfectly,” André-Louis said dryly, and was thus committed to the service of Thespis.
