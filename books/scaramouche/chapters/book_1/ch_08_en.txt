Andre-Louis rode out from Rennes, committed to a deeper adventure than he had ever dreamed of when he left the sleepy village of Gavrillac. He spent the night at a roadside inn and set out again early the next morning. He reached Nantes soon after noon on the following day.

During that long and lonely ride across the dull plains of Brittany, which looked their absolute dreariest in their winter colors, he had plenty of time to think about his actions and his current situation. Up until now, he had taken a purely academic and rather unfriendly interest in the new philosophies of social life. He had only used these new ideas to sharpen his wits, much like a fencer practices his eye and wrist with foils, without ever letting himself believe the issues were truly real. Now, he found himself suddenly transformed into a revolutionary firebrand, fully committed to the most desperate kind of revolutionary action. He was the representative and delegate of a nobleman in the States of Brittany, yet he found himself simultaneously and incongruously acting as the representative and delegate for the entire Third Estate of Rennes.

It was difficult to determine how much he might have deceived himself yesterday, caught up in the heat of passion and swept along by the torrent of his own speech. But it was certainly true that, looking back on it calmly now, he had no illusions at all about what he had done. He had cynically presented his audience with only one side of the great question he had raised.

However, the established order in France acted as a protective wall for the Marquis de La Tour d'Azyr. This system gave the Marquis complete immunity for this crime and any other crimes he felt like committing. Therefore, the established order had to face the consequences of its own wrongdoing. In that fact, Andre-Louis saw his clear justification.

And so, he felt no doubts as he arrived on his errand of rebellion in the beautiful city of Nantes. With its spacious streets and splendid port, Nantes rivaled Bordeaux and Marseilles in prosperity.

He found an inn on the Quai La Fosse and stabled his horse. He ate dinner in the alcove of a window that looked out over the tree-lined quay and the broad expanse of the Loire river, where merchant ships from all nations rode at anchor. The sun had broken through the clouds again, shedding its pale winter light over the yellow waters and the tall-masted ships.

There was just as much bustling life along these quays as one would see on the quays of Paris. There were foreign sailors in strange clothes speaking harsh-sounding foreign languages. There were strong fishwives with baskets of herrings on their heads, wearing bulky skirts above bare legs and feet, shouting their wares loudly and almost incomprehensibly. There were boatmen in woolen caps and loose trousers rolled up to their knees. Peasants in goatskin coats whose wooden shoes clattered against the round cobblestones. There were shipbuilders and laborers from the dockyards, along with bellows-menders, rat-catchers, water-carriers, ink-sellers, and other traveling peddlers. Mixed throughout this working-class crowd that constantly moved back and forth, Andre-Louis saw tradesmen in plain clothes and merchants in long, fur-lined coats. Occasionally, a wealthy merchant prince rolled past in his two-horse carriage, accompanied by the crack of a whip and his coachman's shouts to clear the way. Sometimes, a delicate lady was carried past in her sedan chair, perhaps with an affected abbe from the bishop's court hurrying along beside her. Now and then, an officer in a red uniform rode by with a look of disdain. Once, the massive carriage of a nobleman passed by, displaying a coat of arms on its panels and carrying a pair of powdered footmen in gorgeous uniforms with white stockings standing on the back. There were Capuchin monks in brown and Benedictine monks in black. Plenty of secular priests, as religion was well served in the sixteen parishes of Nantes. In sharp contrast, there were also skinny, ragged adventurers, and police officers in blue coats and gaiters strolling around to keep the peace.

Representatives of every social class that made up the seventy thousand inhabitants of this wealthy and hard-working city could be seen in the human stream that flowed back and forth beneath Andre-Louis's window.

He asked the waiter who served his simple meal of soup, boiled beef. A measure of cheap gray wine about the state of public feeling in the city. The waiter, who was a strong supporter of the privileged classes, regretfully admitted that there was a general feeling of unease. He said that much would depend on what happened at Rennes. If it was true that the King had dissolved the States of Brittany, then everything should be fine. The troublemakers would have no excuse for further riots. There had already been more than enough trouble in Nantes, and they did not want it to happen again. All kinds of rumors were flying around. Since early that morning, crowds had been gathering at the doors of the Chamber of Commerce, desperate for confirmed news. But there was no definite news yet. Nobody even knew for a fact if His Majesty had actually dissolved the States.

It was striking two o'clock, the busiest hour of the day at the stock exchange, when Andre-Louis reached the Place du Commerce. The square was dominated by the imposing classical building of the Exchange. It was so crowded that he almost had to fight his way through to the steps of its magnificent porch. A single word would have been enough to clear a path for him immediately. But his cunning nature told him to keep quiet. He wanted to hit that waiting crowd like a thunderclap, exactly as he had done with the mob in Rennes the day before. He did not want to lose any of the surprise effect of his entrance.

The entrance to the Chamber of Commerce was strictly guarded by a line of ushers armed with wooden staffs. The merchants had clearly felt it was necessary to assemble this guard in a hurry. One of these ushers now blocked the young lawyer's path with his staff as Andre-Louis tried to climb the steps.

Andre-Louis announced himself in a whisper.

The staff was instantly lifted, and Andre-Louis walked past, following the usher up the steps. At the top, right on the threshold of the Chamber, he paused and stopped his guide.

He said, "I will wait here. Bring the president to me."

"What is your name, monsieur?" the usher asked.

Andre-Louis almost gave his name. Then he remembered Le Chapelier's warning about how dangerous this mission was, and his parting advice to hide his identity.

"My name means nothing to him," Andre-Louis replied. "I am just the mouthpiece of a people, nothing more. Go."

The usher left. Andre-Louis waited in the shadows of the tall, pillared porch, occasionally glancing out to look at the sea of faces turned up toward him.

Soon the president arrived, followed by others. They crowded out into the porch, pushing against each other in their eagerness to hear the news.

"Are you a messenger from Rennes?" the president asked.

"I am the delegate sent by the Literary Chamber of Rennes to tell you here in Nantes what is happening."

"What is your name?"

Andre-Louis paused. "It is probably better if we use no names."

The president's eyes widened with serious concern. He was a fat, red-faced man who looked very wealthy and arrogant.

He hesitated for a moment. Then he said, "Come inside the Chamber."

"With your permission, monsieur, I will deliver my message from right here on these steps."

"From here?" The great merchant frowned.

"My message is for the people of Nantes. From here, I can speak directly to the largest number of citizens from all classes. It is my desire, and the desire of those I represent, that as many people as possible should hear my message straight from the source."

"Tell me, sir, is it true that the King has dissolved the States?"

Andre-Louis looked at him. He smiled apologetically and waved a hand toward the crowd. The people were already straining to get a look at this slim young man who had brought out the president and more than half the members of the Chamber. The crowd had already guessed, with that strange instinct that crowds possess, that this man was the messenger they had been waiting for.

"Call the gentlemen of your Chamber outside, monsieur," Andre-Louis said, "and you shall hear everything."

"So be it," the president agreed.

He gave the order. The men came out to crowd onto the steps, leaving the very top step and a semi-circle in the middle clear.

Andre-Louis stepped forward very deliberately into this empty space. He stood there, looking out over the entire crowd. He took off his hat and launched the opening bombshell of his historic speech, which marked one of the great milestones in France's march toward revolution.

"People of this great city of Nantes, I have come to call you to arms!"

In the shocked and somewhat frightened silence that followed, he looked out at them for a moment before continuing.

"I am a delegate of the people of Rennes. I have been sent to tell you what is happening. To invite you to rise up and march to defend our country in this terrible hour of danger."

"Name! Tell us your name!" a voice shouted from the crowd. Others instantly joined in, until the whole square was ringing with the demand.

He could not answer this excited mob the same way he had answered the president. He had to compromise, and he did so very cleverly. "My name," he declared, "is Omnes Omnibus, which means 'all for all'. Let that be enough for you right now. I am a herald, a mouthpiece, and a voice. I am nothing more. I have come to tell you that the privileged classes, who gathered for the States of Brittany in Rennes, have resisted your will, which is also our will. They did this even though the King gave them a very clear warning. Because of their resistance, His Majesty has dissolved the States."

A wave of wild applause broke out. Men laughed and shouted, and cries of "Long live the King!" echoed like thunder. Andre-Louis waited. Slowly, the crowd began to notice his extremely serious expression, and they started to suspect that there might be more bad news to come. Silence gradually fell over the square again, and Andre-Louis was finally able to continue.

"You are celebrating too soon. Unfortunately, the nobles have chosen to ignore the King's dissolution in their arrogant pride. In spite of his orders, they insist on holding their meetings and conducting their business exactly as they please."

A stunned silence greeted this shocking addition to the news they had just cheered. After a moment's pause, Andre-Louis went on.

"These men were already rebelling against the people, against justice, against fairness, and against humanity itself. Now, they are also rebelling against their King. They have grown rich and powerful for far too long, causing misery for the entire nation. Now, rather than give up a single inch of their unreasonable privileges, they will make a joke of royal authority and show contempt for the King himself. They are determined to prove that the only real power in France is the power of their own parasitic laziness."

There was a weak scatter of applause, but most of the audience stayed completely silent, waiting for him to finish.

"This is not a new problem. It has always been exactly like this. Over the past ten years, every minister who saw the dangers facing our country has recommended the very same reforms we are demanding now. These reforms are the only way to stop our motherland from rushing headlong into a disaster. But every time, the privileged classes used their influence to force those ministers out of office. Monsieur Necker has already been appointed to the ministry twice. He was fired twice because his demands for reform threatened the privileges of the clergy and the nobility. Now he has been appointed for a third time. It finally seems we are going to have the States General in spite of the privileged classes. But the nobles and the clergy are determined to ruin what they can no longer prevent. They know the States General will meet. But unless we take action to stop them, they will pack the Third Estate with their own loyal followers and refuse to give the common people any real representation. They want to turn the States General into a tool to permanently protect the corrupt system that keeps them in power. They will stop at nothing to achieve this goal. They have insulted the King's authority, and now they are using murder to silence anyone who speaks out against them. Yesterday in Rennes, two young men were speaking to the people, exactly as I am speaking to you now. They were murdered in the streets by assassins hired by the nobility. Their blood is crying out for revenge!"

The anger of the crowd began as a low, sullen rumble and quickly swelled into a massive roar of outrage.

"Citizens of Nantes, our motherland is in danger! Let us march to defend her. Let us tell the world that we must free the Third Estate from the slavery it has suffered under for centuries. The only obstacles we face are those social classes whose crazy selfishness makes them view the tears and suffering of the poor as a cruel tax that they want to pass on to children who haven't even been born yet. Look at the barbaric methods our enemies are using to keep us oppressed. It shows us that we have everything to fear from the aristocracy they want to set up as the permanent government of France. Let us declare ourselves completely free from them right now!

"The goal of every citizen in the Third Estate should be to establish liberty and equality. To achieve this, we must stand united together. This is especially true for the young and strong. For those who have been lucky enough to be born in this modern eighteenth century and learn the valuable lessons of its new philosophy."

The crowd broke into wild, unrestricted cheering. He had caught them perfectly in the trap of his powerful speech, and he immediately pushed his advantage.

"Let us all swear an oath!" he shouted in a booming voice. "In the name of humanity and liberty, let us swear to build a wall against our enemies! Let us fight their bloodthirsty greed with the calm determination of men who know their cause is just! Let us protest right here and now against any unfair laws that might call us rebels when our intentions are completely pure and just. Let us swear on the honor of our motherland that if an unjust court arrests any of us for political reasons, which are really just acts of tyranny, we will fight back! Let us swear to use all our strength and do whatever nature, courage, and desperation demand to defend ourselves!"

Loud and long applause echoed across the square when he finished. He noticed with satisfaction. Even a little dark amusement, that the wealthy merchants standing on the steps were now crowding around him to shake his hand and cheer him on. They were not just joining in the wild enthusiasm; they were actually leading it.

This confirmed something he already believed, if he had even needed the confirmation. The new philosophies driving this movement had been created by thinkers from the middle class. But the actual need to put those ideas into practice was being felt most strongly right now by these wealthy businessmen. They found that the privileged classes were blocking them from expanding their power and influence as much as their wealth should have allowed. It might be true that Andre-Louis had lit the torch of the Revolution in Nantes that day. But it was even more true to say that the rich middle class had supplied the torch itself.

I don't need to spend much time explaining what happened next. It is a known historical fact that the oath Omnes Omnibus gave to the citizens of Nantes became the core of a formal protest that thousands of them signed. And the results of that powerful protest were not delayed for long, especially since it seemed to match what the King himself wanted. Who can really say how much this protest helped Monsieur Necker? Because on the 27th of that same month of November, Necker forced the Council to adopt the most important and sweeping reform that the clergy and nobility had been trying to block. On that day, a royal decree was published. It ordered that there must be at least one thousand deputies elected to the States General. That the Third Estate must be fully represented by having as many deputies as the clergy and nobility combined.
