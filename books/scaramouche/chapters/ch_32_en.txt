CHAPTER 12. THE OVERWHELMING REASON

M. de La Tour d'Azyr was not seen again in the Manege, or anywhere in Paris, during the months the National Assembly met to finalize France's constitution. His body wound had been relatively minor, but the blow to his pride was almost deadly.

Rumors spread that he had emigrated. But that was only half the truth. He had actually joined the group of noble travelers who moved between the Tuileries Palace and the émigré headquarters in Coblenz. Essentially, he became part of the royalist secret service, which would ultimately lead to the monarchy's downfall.

As for André-Louis, he no longer visited his godfather's house. He was convinced that M. de Kercadiou would not go back on his written promise never to receive him again if the duel took place.

He dedicated himself to his Assembly duties with such passion and effectiveness that when the Constituent Assembly, having achieved its purpose, was dissolved in September of the following year, he was immediately elected to the Legislative Assembly.

Like many others, he believed the Revolution was complete. He thought France only needed to govern itself by the new Constitution, and everything would be fine. And it might have been, if the Court had been able to accept the new reality. Instead, its intrigues led half of Europe to arm itself, preparing to attack France. Europe's quarrel was, in truth, the French King's quarrel with his own people. This was the fundamental horror that would lead to all future atrocities.

Of all the counter-revolutionary troubles instigated by the clergy, none were more severe than those in Brittany. Because of the influence he was expected to have in his home province, the Commission of Twelve, during the early days of the Girondin ministry, proposed that André-Louis go there to combat the unrest. He was asked to proceed peacefully, but his powers were almost absolute. His orders, which he carried, instructed everyone to assist him and warned that anyone who hindered him would do so at their own risk.

He accepted the task, becoming one of the five plenipotentiaries sent on the same mission in the spring of 1792. This kept him away from Paris for four months, and might have kept him longer, but he was recalled at the beginning of August. Trouble brewing in Paris itself was more imminent than any in Brittany. The political sky was blacker than it had been since '89. Paris realized that the hour was rapidly approaching, which would see the climax of the long struggle between Equality and Privilege. It was to a city in such a state that André-Louis came speeding from the West, only to find there also the climax of his own tumultuous career.

Mademoiselle de Kercadiou was also in Paris in those early August days, visiting her uncle's cousin and dearest friend, Madame de Plougastel. The seething unrest that foreshadowed the coming explosion was now undeniable. Yet the atmosphere of gaiety, even jocularity, at Court, where madame and mademoiselle went almost daily, reassured them. M. de Plougastel had come and gone again, back to Coblenz on secret business that kept him almost constantly away from his wife. While with her, he had positively assured her that all measures were taken. He said an insurrection was to be welcomed, because it could only have one conclusion: the final crushing of the Revolution in the Tuileries courtyard. That, he added, was why the King remained in Paris. Without that confidence, he would have placed himself among his Swiss Guards and his 'knights of the dagger' and left the capital. They would easily cut a path for him if his departure was opposed. But not even that would be necessary.

Yet in those early days of August, after her husband's departure, the effect of his inspiring words gradually faded as events unfolded before madame's own eyes. Finally, on the afternoon of the ninth, a messenger arrived at the Hotel Plougastel from Meudon. He carried a note from M. de Kercadiou, who urgently asked mademoiselle to join him there at once, and advised her hostess to come with her.

You may have realized that M. de Kercadiou was the kind of man who made friends across all social classes. His ancient lineage put him on equal footing with members of the nobility. His simple manners, a blend of rustic and bourgeois, and his natural friendliness, put him on equally good terms with those of lower birth. In Meudon, he was known and respected by all the common folk. Rougane, the friendly mayor, was informed on August 9th of the storm brewing for the next day. Knowing mademoiselle was in Paris, he had warned M. de Kercadiou to remove her. In the next twenty-four hours, Paris could become a danger zone for all persons of quality, especially those suspected of ties to the Court party.

There was now absolutely no doubt of Madame de Plougastel's connection with the Court. There was even proof to come that the vigilant and ubiquitous secret societies, which watched over the young revolution, were fully aware of M. de Plougastel's frequent trips to Coblenz. They had no illusions about his reasons. If the Court party were defeated in the coming struggle, Madame de Plougastel's position in Paris would be extremely dangerous. Any guest of noble birth at her hotel would share that danger.

M. de Kercadiou's affection for both women intensified the fears Rougane's warning had stirred in him. This led to that hastily dispatched note, asking his niece and imploring his friend to come at once to Meudon.

The friendly mayor went a step further in his helpfulness. He sent the letter to Paris with his own son, an intelligent nineteen-year-old lad. It was late in the afternoon of that perfect August day when young Rougane arrived at the Hotel Plougastel.

Madame de Plougastel graciously received him in the salon. Its splendors, combined with the lady's grand demeanor, overwhelmed the lad's simple, unsophisticated soul. Madame made up her mind at once.

M. de Kercadiou's urgent message only confirmed her own fears and inclinations. She decided to leave immediately.

"Very well, madame," the youth said. "Then I have the honor to take my leave."

But she would not let him go. First, she sent him to the kitchen to refresh himself while she and mademoiselle got ready. Then, she offered him a seat in her carriage as far as Meudon. She could not allow him to return on foot as he had come.

Though in all the circumstances it was only what he deserved, her kindness, thinking of another in such an agitated moment, would soon be rewarded. If she had done less, she would have experienced, if nothing worse, at least some hours of anguish even greater than what already awaited her.

Perhaps half an hour remained until sunset when they set out in her carriage, intending to leave Paris by the Porte Saint-Martin. They traveled with a single footman behind. Rougane, a terrifying act of condescension, was given a seat inside the carriage with the ladies. He proceeded to fall in love with Mademoiselle de Kercadiou. He considered her the most beautiful person he had ever seen, yet she spoke to him simply and naturally, as an equal. This went to his head a little and unsettled certain republican ideas he had previously thought he fully understood.

The carriage stopped at the barrier, halted by a picket of the National Guard posted before the iron gates.

The sergeant in command walked purposefully to the carriage door. The Countess leaned her head out the window.

The sergeant curtly informed her, "The barrier is closed, madame."

"Closed!" she echoed. It was incredible. "But... but do you mean that we cannot pass?"

"Not unless you have a permit, madame." The sergeant leaned casually on his pike. "The orders are that no one is to leave or enter without proper papers."

"Whose orders?"

"Orders of the Commune of Paris."

"But I must go into the country this evening." Madame's voice was almost petulant. "I am expected."

"In that case, let madame procure a permit."

"Where can I get one?"

"At the Hotel de Ville, or at your section's headquarters, madame."

She thought for a moment. "To the section, then. Please tell my coachman to drive to the Bondy Section."

He saluted her and stepped back. "Bondy Section, Rue des Morts," he told the driver.

Madame sank back into her seat, feeling agitated. Mademoiselle shared her anxiety. Rougane tried to calm and reassure them. He said the section would sort everything out. They would definitely get a permit. What reason could there possibly be to refuse them? It was just a formality, after all!

His reassurances only lifted their spirits temporarily. Soon, they faced an even deeper disappointment. The president of the section received the Countess and flatly refused their request.

"Your name, madame?" he had asked abruptly. He was a rude man, a staunch republican. He hadn't even stood up out of respect for the ladies when they entered. He would have told you he was there to do his job, not to teach manners.

"Plougastel," he repeated after her, omitting her title. He said it as if it were the name of a butcher or a baker. He took a heavy book from a shelf on his right, opened it, and turned the pages. It was a directory for his section. Soon, he found what he was looking for. "Comte de Plougastel, Hotel Plougastel, Rue du Paradis. Is that correct?"

"That is correct, monsieur," she replied, trying to remain civil despite his insulting rudeness.

A long moment of silence followed. He studied some penciled notes next to the name. The sections had been operating much more systematically in recent weeks than most people realized.

"Is your husband with you, madame?" he asked sharply, still scanning the page.

"Monsieur le Comte is not with me," she replied, emphasizing his title.

"Not with you?" He looked up suddenly, giving her a look that mixed suspicion with mockery. "Where is he?"

"He is not in Paris, monsieur."

"Ah! Is he in Coblenz, do you think?"

Madame felt a chill. There was something unsettling about all of this. Why had the sections gathered such detailed information about their residents' movements? What were they planning? She felt trapped, as if caught in an invisible net.

"I do not know, monsieur," she said, her voice trembling.

"Of course not." He seemed to sneer. "No matter. And you wish to leave Paris as well? Where do you want to go?"

"To Meudon."

"Your business there?"

Her face flushed. His insolence was intolerable to a woman who, her entire life, had only ever received the utmost respect from everyone, high or low. However, she realized she was dealing with entirely new forces. She controlled herself, suppressed her anger, and answered calmly.

"I wish to take this lady, Mademoiselle de Kercadiou, back to her uncle, who lives there."

"Is that all? Another day will do for that, madame. The matter isn't urgent."

"Excuse me, monsieur, but to us, the matter is very urgent."

"You haven't convinced me. The city gates are closed to anyone who cannot prove the most urgent and satisfactory reasons for wanting to leave. You will wait, madame, until the restriction is lifted. Good evening."

"But, monsieur..."

"Good evening, madame," he repeated pointedly. It was a dismissal more scornful and authoritarian than any royal "You may leave."

Madame and Aline left. Both were trembling with anger, which they had prudently forced themselves to hide. They climbed back into the coach, wanting to be driven home.

Rougane's astonishment turned to dismay when they told him what had happened. "Why not try the Hotel de Ville, madame?" he suggested.

"After that? It would be pointless. We must accept that we have to stay in Paris until the city gates open again."

"Perhaps it won't matter to us either way by then, madame," Aline said.

"Aline!" she exclaimed in horror.

"Mademoiselle!" Rougane cried, echoing her tone. He realized that people stopped like this must be in some danger, not yet clear, but all the more terrifying because of it. He quickly started thinking. As they approached the Hotel Plougastel again, he announced that he had solved the problem.

"A passport from outside Paris would work just as well," he announced. "Listen closely, and trust me. I'll go back to Meudon right away. My father will give me two permits: one for myself alone, and another for three people. Both will be for travel from Meudon to Paris and back to Meudon. I'll re-enter Paris with my own permit, then destroy it. Then, the three of us will leave together using the other permit, claiming we came from Meudon earlier in the day. It's quite simple, really. If I leave now, I'll be back tonight."

"But how will you leave?" Aline asked.

"Me? Pooh! Don't worry about that. My father is the Mayor of Meudon. Plenty of people know him. I'll go to the Hotel de Ville and tell them the truth: that I'm stuck in Paris because the barriers closed, and my father expects me home this evening. They'll let me through. It's quite simple."

His confidence lifted their spirits again. The plan seemed as easy as he described it.

"Then let your passport be for four, my friend," madame begged him. "There's Jacques," she explained, pointing to the footman who had just helped them get out of the carriage.

Rougane left, confident he would return soon, and they waited for him with the same confidence. But the hours passed, night fell, bedtime came, and still, there was no sign of him.

They waited until midnight. Each pretended for the other's sake to be completely confident, but both were filled with vague premonitions of trouble. Still, they passed the time by playing tric-trac in the grand salon, acting as if they didn't have a single anxious thought.

Finally, at the stroke of midnight, madame sighed and stood up.

"It will be for tomorrow morning," she said, not truly believing it.

"Of course," Aline agreed. "It really would have been impossible for him to return tonight. And it will be much better to travel tomorrow. A journey so late would tire you terribly, dear madame."

So they kept up the pretense.

Early in the morning, they were awakened by a clamor of bells—the tocsins of the sections ringing the alarm. Later, to their startled ears came the rolling of drums. At one point, they heard the sounds of a multitude on the march. Paris was rising. Still later, the rattle of small-arms echoed in the distance, followed by the deeper boom of cannon. Battle had begun between the men of the sections and the men of the Court. The armed people had attacked the Tuileries. Wildest rumors flew in all directions, and some reached the Hotel Plougastel through the servants. These told of the terrible fight for the palace, which would end in the senseless massacre of all those the indecisive monarch abandoned there. He had placed himself and his family under the protection of the Assembly. Senseless to the very end, always following the advice of bad counselors, he prepared for resistance only until resistance was truly needed. At that moment, he ordered a surrender, leaving those who had stood by him to the last at the mercy of a frenzied mob.

While this was happening in the Tuileries, the two women at the Hotel Plougastel still waited for Rougane's return, though now with ever-diminishing hope. Rougane did not return. The plan did not seem as simple to the father as it had to the son. The elder Rougane was rightly afraid to involve himself in such a deception.

He went with his son to inform M. de Kercadiou of what had happened. He frankly told him about the plan his son suggested, but which he dared not do himself.

M. de Kercadiou tried to persuade him with pleas and even offers of bribes. But Rougane remained firm.

"Monsieur," he said, "if this were discovered, and it inevitably would be, I would hang for it. Aside from that, and despite my eagerness to do everything in my power to serve you, it would be a breach of trust I simply couldn't consider. You must not ask me, monsieur."

"But what do you think is going to happen?" asked the half-demented gentleman.

"It is war," said Rougane, who, as we've seen, was well informed. "War between the people and the Court. I am devastated that my warning came too late. But, all things considered, I don't think you need to truly alarm yourself. War will not be waged against women." M. de Kercadiou clung to that assurance for comfort after the mayor and his son had left. But in the back of his mind, the knowledge of M. de Plougastel's activities lingered. What if the revolutionaries were equally well informed? Most probably, they were. In the past, women who were political offenders had been known to suffer for the sins of their men. Anything was possible in a popular uprising, and Aline would be exposed along with Madame de Plougastel.

Late that night, he sat gloomily in his brother's library. The pipe he had sought solace in was extinguished between his fingers when a sharp knocking came at the door.

When the old seneschal of Gavrillac went to open the door, a slim young man stood revealed on the threshold. He wore a dark olive surcoat, its skirts reaching down to his calves, along with boots, buckskins, and a small-sword. Around his waist was a tricolor sash, and in his hat, a tricolor cockade. This gave him an official look, which seemed extremely sinister to the old retainer of feudalism, who fully shared his master's current fears.

"Monsieur desires?" he asked, a mix of respect and mistrust in his voice.

Then a crisp voice startled him.

"Why, Benoit! Good heavens! Have you completely forgotten me?"

With a shaking hand, the old man raised the lantern he carried, aiming its light more fully upon that lean, wide-mouthed face.

"Monsieur André!" he cried. "Monsieur André!" Then he looked at the sash and the cockade, and hesitated, clearly at a loss for words.

But André-Louis stepped past him into the wide vestibule, with its tessellated floor of black-and-white marble.

"If my godfather hasn't retired yet, take me to him. If he has retired, take me to him all the same."

"Oh, but certainly, Monsieur André. I'm sure he'll be delighted to see you. No, he hasn't gone to bed yet. This way, Monsieur André; this way, if you please."

André-Louis had returned to Meudon a half-hour earlier. He went straight to the mayor, seeking clear news about Paris. He wanted to confirm or dispel the ominous rumors that had grown louder and more frequent as he neared the capital. Rougane informed him that an uprising was about to happen. The districts had already taken control of the city gates. It was impossible for anyone without proper authorization to enter or leave Paris.

André-Louis bowed his head, his thoughts deeply troubled. For some time, he had seen the danger of a second revolution emerging from the first. This new upheaval could destroy everything that had been achieved. It could hand power over to a dangerous group that would plunge the country into anarchy. The very thing he had feared was now more than ever about to happen. He decided he would go at once, that very night, and see for himself what was happening.

As he was leaving, he turned back to Rougane. He asked if Monsieur de Kercadiou was still in Meudon.

"You know him, monsieur?"

"He is my godfather."

"Your godfather! And you're a representative!" Rougane exclaimed. "Why, then, you might be exactly the man he needs." Rougane then told him about what his son had tried to do in Paris that afternoon and how it turned out.

Nothing more was needed. The fact that his godfather had, two years ago, forbidden him from his house under certain conditions, didn't matter at all right now. He left his traveling carriage at the small inn. Then he went straight to Monsieur de Kercadiou.

Monsieur de Kercadiou was startled at such a time by this sudden appearance. It was the man against whom he held a bitter grudge. He greeted André-Louis with almost the exact same words he had used in that very room on a similar occasion before.

"What do you want here, sir?"

"To serve you if possible, my godfather," André-Louis answered, his tone meant to disarm him.

But it did not disarm Monsieur de Kercadiou. "You have stayed away so long," he said, "that I hoped you would not disturb me again."

"I would not have dared to disobey you now," André-Louis replied, "if it weren't for the hope that I can be of service. I have seen Rougane, the mayor..."

"What's that you say about not daring to disobey?"

"You forbade me your house, monsieur."

Monsieur de Kercadiou stared at him, at a loss for words.

"And is that why you haven't come near me all this time?"

"Of course. Why else would I not?"

Monsieur de Kercadiou continued to stare. Then he muttered a curse under his breath. It unsettled him to deal with someone who took his words so literally. He had expected André-Louis to come remorsefully, admit his mistake, and ask for forgiveness. He said as much.

"But how could I hope that you didn't mean exactly what you said, monsieur?" André-Louis asked. "You were so very clear in your declaration. What good would expressions of remorse do if I didn't intend to change? And I had no intention of changing. We may yet be thankful for that."

"Thankful?"

"I am a representative," André-Louis explained. "I have certain powers. I am returning to Paris at a very opportune moment. Can I serve you where Rougane cannot? The need, monsieur, appears to be very urgent if even half of what I suspect is true. Aline should be placed in safety at once."

Monsieur de Kercadiou surrendered completely. He came over and took André-Louis' hand.

He said, visibly moved, "My boy, there is in you a certain nobility that's undeniable. If I seemed harsh with you back then, it was because I was trying to steer you away from your bad tendencies. I wanted to keep you out of the dangerous path of politics. That path has led this unfortunate country to such a terrible state. We have the enemy on the frontier, and civil war is about to erupt here at home. That is what you revolutionaries have done."

André-Louis did not argue. He moved on.

"About Aline?" he asked. Then he answered his own question. "She is in Paris, and she must be brought out of it at once. This must happen before the city becomes a slaughterhouse. It very well might, once the passions that have been building for months are unleashed. Young Rougane's plan is good. At least, I cannot think of a better one."

"But old Rougane won't agree to it."

"You mean he won't do it taking full responsibility himself," André-Louis clarified. "But he has agreed to do it under my responsibility. I have left him a signed note stating that a safe-conduct for Mademoiselle de Kercadiou to travel to Paris and return is issued by him, following my orders. The powers I hold, which I've shown him, are his sufficient justification for obeying me in this. I left him that note with the understanding that he should only use it in an extreme situation, for his own protection. In exchange, he has given me this safe-conduct."

"You already have it!"

Monsieur de Kercadiou took the sheet of paper that André-Louis held out. His hand shook. He brought it closer to the cluster of candles burning on the console table. Then he squinted his nearsighted eyes to read.

"If you send that to Paris by young Rougane in the morning," André-Louis said, "Aline should be here by noon. Of course, nothing could be done tonight without arousing suspicion. It's too late for that. And now, my godfather, monsieur, you know exactly why I'm intruding against your orders. If there's any other way I can serve you, just tell me while I'm here."

"But there is, André," Monsieur de Kercadiou replied. "Didn't Rougane tell you there were others..."

"He mentioned Madame de Plougastel and her servant."

"Then why...?" Monsieur de Kercadiou stopped, his look asking the question.

André-Louis shook his head very solemnly.

"That's impossible," he said.

Monsieur de Kercadiou's mouth fell open in astonishment. "Impossible!" he repeated. "But why?"

"Monsieur, I can do what I'm doing for Aline without offending my conscience. In fact, for Aline, I would offend my conscience and still do it. But Madame de Plougastel is in a very different situation. Neither Aline nor any of her family have been involved in counter-revolutionary work, which is the real cause of the disaster threatening us now. I can arrange for her removal from Paris without feeling guilty. I'm convinced I'd be doing nothing anyone could criticize, or that might lead to an investigation. But Madame de Plougastel is the wife of Monsieur le Comte de Plougastel, who everyone knows acts as an agent between the Court and the émigrés."

"That's no fault of hers!" cried Monsieur de Kercadiou, dismayed.

"Agreed. But she might be called upon at any moment to prove she's not involved in these schemes. It's known she was in Paris today. If she's looked for tomorrow and found to be gone, investigations will certainly be made. This would inevitably reveal that I've betrayed my trust and misused my authority for personal gain. I hope, monsieur, you'll understand that the risk is too great to take for a stranger's sake."

"A stranger?" the Lord of Gavrillac said reproachfully.

"Practically a stranger to me," André-Louis said.

"But she's not a stranger to me, André. She's my cousin and a very dear, valued friend. And, my God, what you're saying only makes it more urgent to get her out of Paris. She must be rescued, André, no matter the cost—she must be rescued! Why, her case is infinitely more urgent than Aline's!"

He stood pleading before his godson, very different now from the stern man who had greeted him on his arrival. His face was pale, his hands shook, and beads of perspiration dotted his brow.

"My godfather, monsieur, I would do anything within reason. But I cannot do this. To rescue her could mean ruin for Aline, for you, and for me."

"We must take the risk."

"You have a right to speak for yourself, of course."

"Oh, and for you, believe me, André, for you!" He came close to the young man. "André, I beg you to trust me on this, and to obtain this permit for Madame de Plougastel."

André looked at him mystified. "This is fantastic," he said. "I have grateful memories of the lady's interest in me for a few days when I was a child, and again more recently in Paris when she tried to convert me to what she considers the true political ideology. But I won't risk my neck for her—no, nor yours, nor Aline's."

"Ah! But, André..."

"That's my final word, monsieur. It's growing late, and I want to sleep in Paris."

"No, no! Wait!" The Lord of Gavrillac was showing signs of extreme distress. "André, you must!"

There was something so unreasonable in this insistence, and even more so in his frantic manner, that André couldn't help but assume some dark, mysterious motive was behind it.

"I must?" he echoed. "Why? What are your reasons, monsieur?"

"André, my reasons are absolutely vital."

"Please allow me to be the judge of that." André-Louis' manner was almost commanding.

The demand seemed to plunge Monsieur de Kercadiou into despair. He paced the room, his hands clasped tightly behind his back, his brow furrowed. At last, he came to stand before his godson.

"Can't you take my word for it that these reasons exist?" he cried out in anguish.

"In a matter like this, one that could cost me my life? Oh, monsieur, is that reasonable?"

"I'll break my word of honor, my oath, if I tell you." Monsieur de Kercadiou turned away, wringing his hands, looking visibly distressed. Then he turned again to André. "But in this extreme, desperate situation, and since you insist so ungenerously, I shall have to tell you. God help me, I have no other choice. She'll understand when she knows. André, my boy..." He paused again, a man filled with fear. He set a hand on his godson's shoulder, and to his increasing amazement, André-Louis saw a film of tears over those pale, short-sighted eyes. "Madame de Plougastel is your mother."

A long moment of complete silence followed. What he had just been told didn't sink in immediately. When he finally understood, André-Louis' first instinct was to cry out. But he controlled himself, acting like a Stoic. He always had to be playing a role; it was in his nature. And he stayed true to that nature even in this critical moment. He remained silent until, following that strange theatrical instinct, he felt he could speak without emotion. "I see," he said at last, quite calmly.

His mind raced back through the past. He quickly reviewed his memories of Madame de Plougastel: her unique, if inconsistent, interest in him, the strange mix of affection and wistfulness she always showed him. Finally, he understood so many things that had puzzled him until now.

"I see," he said again. Then he added, "Of course, anyone but a fool would have guessed it long ago."

It was Monsieur de Kercadiou who cried out. Monsieur de Kercadiou recoiled as if struck.

"My God, André, what are you made of? You can take news like this so calmly?"

"And how would you like me to take it? Should I be surprised to discover I had a mother? After all, a mother is absolutely necessary for someone to be born."

He sat down abruptly, trying to hide the fact that his limbs were shaking too obviously. He pulled a handkerchief from his pocket to wipe his brow, which had grown damp. Then, quite suddenly, he found himself weeping.

Seeing those tears stream silently down André-Louis’ now pale face, Monsieur de Kercadiou quickly came over to him. He sat down beside him and affectionately put an arm around his shoulder.

"André, my poor boy," he murmured. "I… I was foolish enough to think you had no heart. You fooled me with your infernal act, and now I see… I see…" He wasn't sure what he saw, or perhaps he hesitated to say it.

"It’s nothing, monsieur. I’m exhausted, and… and I have a cold." Then, finding he couldn't maintain the act any longer, he abruptly gave up, completely abandoning all pretense. "Why… why has there been all this mystery?" he asked. "Was I never supposed to know?"

"It was, André. It… it had to be, for the sake of prudence."

"But why? Tell me everything, sir. Surely you can’t just leave it there. Since you’ve told me so much, you must tell me all."

"The reason, my boy, is that you were born about three years after your mother married Monsieur de Plougastel. This was about eighteen months after Monsieur de Plougastel had left with the army, and about four months before he returned to his wife. It’s a matter Monsieur de Plougastel has never suspected, and for the most serious family reasons, he must never suspect. That’s why the utmost secrecy has been maintained. That’s why no one was ever allowed to know. Your mother came to Brittany early, and under an assumed name, she spent some months in the village of Moreau. You were born while she was there."

André-Louis thought it over. He had dried his tears. Now he sat rigid and composed.

"When you say that no one was ever allowed to know, you’re telling me, of course, that you, monsieur…"

"Oh, my God, no!" The denial burst out violently. Monsieur de Kercadiou sprang to his feet, pushed away from André’s side by the force of his emotions. It was as if the mere suggestion filled him with horror. "I was the only other person who knew. But it’s not as you think, André. You can’t imagine that I would lie to you, that I would deny you if you were my son?"

"If you say I am not, monsieur, that’s enough."

"You are not. I was Thérèse’s cousin and also, as she well knew, her truest friend. She knew she could trust me, and it was to me she came for help in her desperate situation. Once, years before, I would have married her. But, of course, I’m not the kind of man a woman could love. She trusted my love for her, however, and I have kept her trust."

"Then, who was my father?"

"I don’t know. She never told me. It was her secret, and I didn’t pry. It’s not in my nature, André."

André-Louis got up and stood silently facing Monsieur de Kercadiou.

"You believe me, André."

"Naturally, monsieur. And I’m sorry, I’m sorry that I’m not your son."

Monsieur de Kercadiou gripped his godson’s hand tightly, holding it for a moment without a word. Then, as they moved apart again:

"And now, what will you do, André?" he asked. "Now that you know?"

André-Louis stood for a moment, considering, then burst into laughter. The situation had its humorous side. He explained it.

"What difference should this knowledge make? Is a son’s devotion supposed to appear just because a relationship is announced? Am I to risk my neck by being careless on behalf of a mother who was so careful that she never intended to reveal herself? This discovery is based on pure chance, on a roll of the dice of Fate. Is that supposed to influence me?"

"The decision is yours, André."

"No, it’s beyond me. Let whoever can decide it, I cannot."

"You mean you refuse even now?"

"I mean that I consent. Since I can't decide what I should do, I'm left with only one option: to do what a son is supposed to do. It's grotesque, but all life is grotesque."

"You will never, never regret it."

Andre said, "I hope not. But I think it's very likely I will. Now I'd better see Rougane again right away and get the other two permits I need from him. Then perhaps it would be best if I took them to Paris myself in the morning. If you'll give me a bed, monsieur, I'd be grateful. I... I confess I'm hardly in a state to do anything else tonight."
