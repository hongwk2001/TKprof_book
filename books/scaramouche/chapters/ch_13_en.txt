Chapter 4. EXIT MONSIEUR PARVISSIMUS

At four o’clock on Monday afternoon, the curtain rose on "Figaro-Scaramouche." The market-hall was three-quarters full. Monsieur Binet credited this good turnout to the fair bringing people to Guichen, and to his company's grand parade through the town's streets during the busiest part of the day. André-Louis, however, believed it was entirely due to the title. That "Figaro" touch had attracted the wealthier middle class, who filled over half of the twenty-sous spots and three-quarters of the twelve-sous seats. The title had drawn them in. Whether it would continue to do so depended on how the company performed the *canevas*, the play's outline, which André-Louis had worked so hard on for Binet's glory. He had no doubt about the *canevas* itself. The authors he had drawn from for its elements were excellent, and he had taken their best work, which he felt was only fair to them.

The company outdid themselves. The audience eagerly followed Scaramouche's clever schemes. They delighted in Climene's beauty and freshness, and were almost moved to tears by the cruel fate that kept her from the longing arms of the handsome Leander through four long acts. They howled with laughter at Pantaloon's humiliation, the antics of his lively servant Harlequin, and the boastful swagger and roaring ferocity of the cowardly Rhodomont.

The Binet troupe's success in Guichen was undeniable. That night, the company drank Burgundy, paid for by Monsieur Binet. The evening's earnings totaled eight louis, which was the best business Monsieur Binet had ever done in his entire career. He was extremely pleased. Satisfaction seemed to radiate like steam from his stout body. He even went so far as to grudgingly give Monsieur Parvissimus some credit for the success.

"His suggestion was most valuable, as I perceived at the time," he said carefully, making sure to properly limit his praise.

"And his cutting of quills," Polichinelle growled. "Don't forget that. It's crucial to have someone around who knows how to cut a quill. I'll remember that when I become an author."

But not even that jab could rouse Monsieur Binet from his blissful contentment.

On Tuesday, the artistic success was repeated, and the financial gains increased. André-Louis, acting as doorkeeper, counted an enormous sum of ten louis and seven livres to Monsieur Binet after the performance. Monsieur Binet had never made so much money in a single evening before. A humble little village like Guichen was certainly the last place he would have expected such a windfall.

"Ah, but Guichen during the fair," André-Louis reminded him. "People are here from as far away as Nantes and Rennes to buy and sell. Tomorrow is the last day of the fair, so the crowds will be even bigger. We should surpass tonight's earnings."

"Surpass them? I'll be perfectly satisfied if we just do as well, my friend."

"You can count on that," André-Louis assured him. "Are we having Burgundy?"

And then the tragedy struck. It began with a series of bumps and thuds, ending in a crash outside the door that made them all jump to their feet in alarm.

Pierrot quickly opened the door and saw a man's crumpled body lying at the foot of the stairs. It was groaning, so it was alive. Pierrot stepped forward to turn the body over and discovered it was Scaramouche, his face wizened, grimacing, groaning, and twitching.

The entire company, crowding in behind Pierrot, burst into laughter.

"I always said you should switch roles with me," Harlequin cried. "You're such an excellent tumbler! Have you been practicing?"

"Fool!" Scaramouche snapped. "Must you laugh when I've almost broken my neck?"

"You're right. We should be weeping because you *didn't* break it. Come on, man, get up," Harlequin said, holding out a hand to the fallen rogue.

Scaramouche took the hand, clutched it, and pulled himself up from the ground. Then, with a scream, he dropped back down again.

"My foot!" he complained.

Binet pushed through the group of actors, scattering them left and right. Fear had quickly gripped him. Fate had played such tricks on him before.

"What's wrong with your foot?" he asked sourly.

"It's broken, I think," Scaramouche complained.

"Broken? Bah! Get up, man." Binet caught him under the armpits and hauled him upright.

Scaramouche landed howling on one foot. The other buckled beneath him when he tried to put it down, and he would have collapsed again if Binet hadn't supported him. He filled the room with his cries, while Binet swore loudly and creatively.

"Must you bellow like a calf, you fool? Be quiet! Someone, a chair here!"

A chair was pushed forward. Binet forced Scaramouche down into it.

"Let's look at this foot of yours."

Ignoring Scaramouche's howls of pain, Binet pulled off his shoe and stocking.

"What's wrong with it?" he asked, staring. "Nothing that I can see." He grabbed the foot, heel in one hand, instep in the other, and twisted it. Scaramouche screamed in agony until Climene caught Binet's arm and made him stop.

"My God, don't you have any feelings?" she scolded her father. "The boy has hurt his foot. Do you have to torture him? Will that make it better?"

"Hurt his foot!" Binet said. "I don't see anything wrong with his foot. There's nothing to justify all this fuss. Maybe he just bruised it..."

"A man with a bruised foot doesn't scream like that," Madame said, looking over Climene's shoulder. "Maybe he dislocated it."

"That's what I'm afraid of," Scaramouche whimpered.

Binet stood up, clearly disgusted.

"Take him to bed," he ordered them, "and get a doctor to see him."

They did as he said, and the doctor arrived. After examining the patient, he reported that nothing very serious had happened. However, it was clear that he had sprained his foot a little when he fell. A few days of rest, and he would be fine.

"A few days!" Binet cried. "Good heavens! Do you mean he can't walk?"

"It would be unwise, even impossible, for him to take more than a few steps."

Monsieur Binet paid the doctor's fee and sat down to think. He poured himself a glass of Burgundy, drank it in one gulp without a word, and then sat staring into the empty glass.

"Of course, this is exactly the kind of thing that always happens to me," he grumbled, not really speaking to anyone. The company members stood silently before him, sharing his disappointment. "I should have known that this, or something like it, would happen to ruin the first stroke of luck I've had in years. Oh well, it's over. Tomorrow we pack up and leave. It's the best day of the fair, right at the peak of our success. We could have made a good fifteen louis, and then this happens! Good heavens!"

"Are you saying we're canceling tomorrow's performance?"

Everyone, including Binet, turned to stare at André-Louis.

"Are we supposed to perform 'Figaro-Scaramouche' without Scaramouche?" Binet asked, sneering.

"Of course not." André-Louis stepped forward. "But surely, we can rearrange the parts. For example, Polichinelle is a fine actor."

Polichinelle gave him a sweeping bow. "Overwhelmed," he said, ever sardonic.

"But he already has his own part," Binet objected.

"It's a small part, which Pasquariel could play."

"And who will play Pasquariel then?"

"Nobody. We'll just cut it. The play won't suffer."

"He thinks of everything," Polichinelle sneered. "What a man!"

But Binet was far from agreeing. "Are you suggesting that Polichinelle should play Scaramouche?" he asked, incredulously.

"Why not? He's capable enough!"

"Overwhelmed again," Polichinelle interjected.

"Play Scaramouche with that physique?" Binet stood up, pointing an accusing finger at Polichinelle's sturdy, thick-set, short frame.

"For lack of anyone better," André-Louis said.

"More overwhelmed than ever." Polichinelle's bow was superb this time. "Honestly, I think I'll go get some fresh air to cool down after all this blushing."

"Go to the devil," Binet snapped at him.

"Better and better." Polichinelle headed for the door. He stopped on the threshold and struck a dramatic pose. "Understand me, Binet. I will not play Scaramouche now, under any circumstances." And he left. Overall, it was a very dignified exit.

André-Louis shrugged, threw out his arms, and then let them fall back to his sides. "You've ruined everything," he told Monsieur Binet. "This could have been easily arranged. Well, you're the master here, and since you want us to pack up and leave, I suppose that's what we'll do."

He went out as well. Monsieur Binet paused in thought for a moment, then followed him, his small eyes glinting with cunning. He caught up to André-Louis in the doorway. Monsieur Binet said very affably, “Let’s take a walk together, Monsieur Parvissimus.”

He linked his arm through André-Louis’s and led him out into the street, which was still quite busy. They walked past the booths lining the market and down the hill towards the bridge. Monsieur Binet said, “I don’t think we’ll pack up tomorrow. In fact, we’ll perform tomorrow night.”

“Not if I know Polichinelle. You’ve…”

“I’m not thinking of Polichinelle.”

“Of whom, then?”

“Of yourself.”

“I’m flattered, sir. And in what capacity are you thinking of me?” Binet’s voice had a sleek, oily quality that André-Louis disliked.

“I’m thinking of you in the part of Scaramouche.”

André-Louis said, “Daydreams. You’re amusing yourself, of course.”

“Not in the least. I’m quite serious.”

“But I’m not an actor.”

“You told me you could be.”

“Oh, on occasion… a small part, perhaps…”

“Well, here’s a big part—the chance to make a name for yourself in one go. How many people get an opportunity like that?”

“It’s a chance I don’t want, Monsieur Binet. Shall we change the subject?” André-Louis was very frosty, perhaps because he sensed something vaguely menacing in Monsieur Binet’s manner, as much as for any other reason.

Monsieur Binet said, “We’ll change the subject when I please.” A glimpse of steel glinted through his smooth demeanor. “Tomorrow night you play Scaramouche. You’re sharp-witted enough, your figure is ideal, and you have just the right kind of biting humor for the part. You should be a great success.”

“It’s much more likely that I’d be a spectacular failure.”

Binet said cynically, “That won’t matter.” He then explained himself. “The failure will be personal to you. The receipts will be safe by then.”

“Much obliged,” André-Louis said.

“We should take fifteen louis tomorrow night.”

André-Louis said, “It’s unfortunate that you’re without a Scaramouche.”

“It’s fortunate that I have one, Monsieur Parvissimus.”

André-Louis disengaged his arm. He said, “I’m beginning to find you tiresome. I think I’ll return.”

“A moment, Monsieur Parvissimus. If I’m to lose those fifteen louis… you won’t mind if I compensate myself in other ways?”

“That’s your own concern, Monsieur Binet.”

“Pardon, Monsieur Parvissimus. It might possibly be yours as well.” Binet took his arm again. “Do me the kindness of stepping across the street with me. Just as far as the post office there. I have something to show you.”

André-Louis went. Before they even reached the sheet of paper nailed to the door, he knew exactly what it would say. And indeed, just as he had supposed, it stated that twenty louis would be paid for information leading to the arrest of one André-Louis Moreau, a lawyer from Gavrillac, who was wanted by the King’s Lieutenant in Rennes on a charge of sedition.

Monsieur Binet watched him as he read. Their arms were linked, and Binet’s grip was firm and powerful.

He said, “Now, my friend, will you be Monsieur Parvissimus and play Scaramouche tomorrow, or will you be André-Louis Moreau of Gavrillac and go to Rennes to satisfy the King’s Lieutenant?”

André-Louis asked, his face a mask, “And what if you’re mistaken?”

"I'll take that risk," M. Binet grinned. "You mentioned, I think, that you were a lawyer. That was a slip-up, my dear. It's unlikely that two lawyers would be hiding in the same district at the same time. You see, I'm not so easily fooled. So, M. André-Louis Moreau, lawyer of Gavrillac, what's it going to be?"

André-Louis said, "We will talk it over as we walk back."

"What is there to talk over?"

"One or two things, I think," André-Louis replied. "I must know where I stand. Come, sir, if you please."

"Very well," M. Binet said. They turned up the street again. M. Binet kept a firm grip on his young friend's arm and stayed alert for any tricks the young gentleman might try. It was an unnecessary precaution. André-Louis was not the man to waste his energy uselessly. He knew that in physical strength, he was no match for the heavy and powerful Pantaloon.

André-Louis said sweetly, "If I yield to your most persuasive arguments, M. Binet, what guarantee do you give me that you won't sell me for twenty louis after I've served my purpose?"

"You have my word of honor for that," M. Binet said firmly.

André-Louis laughed. "Oh, we're going to talk about honor, are we? Really, M. Binet? It's clear you think I'm a fool."

In the dark, he did not see the flush that spread across M. Binet's round face. It was a few moments before he replied.

"Perhaps you are right," he growled. "What guarantee do you want?"

"I do not know what guarantee you can possibly give."

"I have said that I will keep faith with you."

"Until you find it more profitable to sell me."

"You can always make it more profitable for me to keep faith with you. It's because of you that we've done so well in Guichen. Oh, I admit it frankly."

"In private," André-Louis said.

M. Binet ignored the sarcasm.

"What you've done for us here with 'Figaro-Scaramouche,' you can do elsewhere with other things. Naturally, I won't want to lose you. That is your guarantee."

"Yet tonight you would sell me for twenty louis."

"Because—good heavens!—you enrage me by refusing a service well within your abilities. Don't you think, if I were entirely the scoundrel you think I am, I could have sold you last Saturday? I want you to understand me, my dear Parvissimus."

"I beg that you'll not apologize," André-Louis said. "You would be more tiresome than ever."

"Of course you will be mocking. You never miss a chance to mock. It'll bring you trouble before your life is over. Come; here we are back at the inn, and you have not yet given me your decision."

André-Louis looked at him. "I must yield, of course," he said. "I can't help myself."

M. Binet released his arm at last and slapped him heartily on the back. "Well said, my lad. You'll never regret it. If I know anything about the theater, I know that you have made the great decision of your life. Tomorrow night you'll thank me."

André-Louis shrugged and stepped out ahead towards the inn. But M. Binet called him back.

"M. Parvissimus!"

He turned. There stood the man's great bulk, the moonlight shining down on his round, fat face. He was holding out his hand.

"M. Parvissimus, no resentment. I don't allow that in my life. You will shake hands with me, and we will forget all this."

André-Louis looked at him for a moment with disgust. He was growing angry. Then, realizing this, he realized he was being ridiculous, almost as ridiculous as that sly, rascally Pantaloon. He laughed and took the outstretched hand. "No resentment?" M. Binet insisted.

"Oh, no resentment," André-Louis said.
