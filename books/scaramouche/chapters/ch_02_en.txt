[Book I - Chapter II: THE ARISTOCRAT]

CHAPTER II. THE ARISTOCRAT

The sleepy village of Gavrillac sat a half-league away from the main road to Rennes. This kept it safely hidden from the traffic of the outside world. The village lay in a curve of the River Meu. It started at the foot of a shallow hill and straggled halfway up the slope. A squat manor house sat at the top. The village paid tribute to its lord in money and service. It paid tithes to the Church and taxes to the King. By the time it finished paying, the village struggled to keep body and soul together with what was left. Still, conditions in Gavrillac were not as bad as in many other parts of France. They were certainly not half as bad as the lives of the wretched peasants working for the great Lord of La Tour d'Azyr. His massive estates were separated from this little village by the waters of the Meu.

The Chateau de Gavrillac looked like a lord's manor mostly because it sat high above the village. It had no special features of its own. It was built of granite like the rest of Gavrillac. It had stood for three centuries, which softened its harsh look. It was a squat, flat-fronted building with two stories. Each floor had four windows covered by external wooden shutters. Two square towers with cone-shaped roofs stood at either end. The house sat far back in a garden. The garden was bare now, but it was very pleasant in the summer. A fine sweep of balustraded terrace stretched across the front. It looked exactly like what it was. It had always been the home of unpretentious people who cared more about farming than adventure.

Quintin de Kercadiou was the Lord of Gavrillac. The vague title of Seigneur de Gavrillac was the only title he held. His forefathers had held it before him, and no one knew where it came from. He fit the impression his house gave perfectly. He was as rough as the granite itself. He had never visited the royal courts, and he had never served in the King's armies. He left it to his younger brother, Etienne, to represent the family in high society. His own interests had always been his woods and pastures. He hunted and farmed his land. On the surface, he looked like a simple peasant farmer. He kept no grand estate, at least not one that matched his position or the tastes of his niece, Aline de Kercadiou. Aline had spent two years in the royal court at Versailles with her uncle Etienne. She had very different ideas from her uncle Quintin about how a lord should live. She was the only child of a third Kercadiou brother and was orphaned at four years old. She had ruled over the Lord of Gavrillac like a tyrant ever since, as he was both father and mother to her. But she had never managed to break down his stubbornness about living grandly. She did not despair yet because persistence was a key part of her character. She had been working on him constantly since she returned from Versailles three months ago.

She was walking on the terrace when Andre-Louis and M. de Vilmorin arrived. The chill air made her wrap her slight body in a white cloak. Her head was covered in a close-fitting bonnet edged with white fur. A knot of pale blue ribbon tied it tight under her chin on the right side. A long ringlet of blonde hair escaped on the left. The cold air made her cheeks pink and seemed to make her dark blue eyes sparkle.

She had known Andre-Louis and M. de Vilmorin since childhood. The three of them used to play together. Andre-Louis was her uncle's godson, so she called him cousin. She kept calling him cousin long after Philippe de Vilmorin outgrew their childhood friendship. Philippe had become Monsieur de Vilmorin to her now.

She waved to them as they walked up. She stood at the end of the terrace waiting for them, looking like a beautiful painting and knowing it. 

"If you came to see my uncle, you picked a bad time, gentlemen," she said. She seemed a little restless. "He is very busy."

"We will wait, mademoiselle," M. de Vilmorin said. He bowed politely over her hand. "Who would rush to see the uncle when they can spend a moment with the niece?"

"Monsieur the abbe," she teased him. "When you become a priest, I will make you my confessor. You are so understanding."

"But he has no curiosity," Andre-Louis said. "You haven't thought of that."

"I wonder what you mean, Cousin Andre."

"You well might wonder," Philippe laughed. "No one ever knows what he means." 

Philippe looked across the terrace and saw a carriage parked in front of the chateau. It was the kind of carriage you saw in a big city, but rarely in the country. It was a beautiful two-horse cabriolet made of walnut. The varnish looked like glass, and tiny country scenes were painted perfectly on the doors. It held two people. It had a box in front for the driver and a stand in back for the footman. The stand was empty, but the footman was pacing in front of the door. He walked out from behind the carriage, and M. de Vilmorin saw his bright blue and gold uniform. It was the uniform of the Marquis de La Tour d'Azyr.

"Why, is M. de La Tour d'Azyr with your uncle?" he asked.

"He is, monsieur," she said. Her voice and eyes were full of mystery, but M. de Vilmorin did not notice.

"Ah, pardon me," he said. He bowed low with his hat in his hand. "Your servant, mademoiselle." He turned to walk toward the house.

"Shall I come with you, Philippe?" Andre-Louis called out.

"It would be rude to assume you would want to," M. de Vilmorin said, glancing at mademoiselle. "And I don't think it would help. If you will wait..."

M. de Vilmorin walked away. Mademoiselle paused for a moment and then laughed lightly. "Where is he going in such a hurry?"

"To see M. de La Tour d'Azyr as well as your uncle, I guess."

"But he cannot. They cannot see him. Did I not say they are very busy? You don't ask me why, Andre." She looked mysterious again. It could have been excitement or amusement, or maybe both. Andre-Louis could not tell.

"You clearly want to tell me, so why should I ask?" he said.

"If you are going to be sarcastic, I will not tell you even if you ask. Oh, yes, I will. It will teach you to treat me with proper respect."

"I hope I will always treat you with respect."

"You will have to when you learn that M. de La Tour d'Azyr is here because of me. I am the reason for his visit." She looked at him with sparkling eyes and a big smile.

"The rest seems obvious. But please treat me like an idiot, because it is not obvious to me."

"Why, stupid, he came to ask for my hand in marriage."

"Good God," Andre-Louis said. He stared at her in shock.

She stepped back from him. She frowned and tilted her chin up. "Does it surprise you?"

"It disgusts me," he said bluntly. "In fact, I don't believe it. You are playing a joke on me."

She put aside her annoyance for a moment to prove him wrong. "I am completely serious, monsieur. My uncle received a formal letter from M. de La Tour d'Azyr this morning. It announced his visit and the reason for it. It did surprise us a little..."

"Oh, I see," Andre-Louis said, looking relieved. "I understand. For a moment I almost feared..." He stopped, looked at her, and shrugged.

"Why did you stop? You almost feared that my time in Versailles was wasted. You thought I would let someone court me like a village peasant. You were being stupid. I am being courted properly, through my uncle."

"Does only his consent matter, according to Versailles?"

"What else matters?"

"Your own consent."

She laughed. "I am an obedient niece, when it suits me."

"Will it suit you to be obedient if your uncle accepts this monstrous proposal?"

"Monstrous!" She looked offended. "Why is it monstrous, please?"

"For a score of reasons," he answered irritably.

"Give me one," she challenged.

"He is twice your age."

"Hardly," she said.

"He is at least forty-five."

"But he looks thirty. He is very handsome. You have to admit that. You cannot deny that he is very rich and powerful. He is the greatest nobleman in Brittany. He will make me a great lady."

"God made you a great lady, Aline."

"Well, that is better. Sometimes you can almost be polite." She started walking along the terrace again, and Andre-Louis walked beside her.

"I can be more than polite. I can give you reasons why you should not let this beast ruin the beautiful thing God created."

She frowned, and her lips tightened. "You are speaking of my future husband," she scolded.

His lips tightened too, and his pale face grew paler. 

"Is it true then? Is it settled? Your uncle is going to agree? You are going to be sold into slavery to a man you don't even know, with no love at all? I dreamed of better things for you, Aline."

"Better than being the Marquise de La Tour d'Azyr?"

He made a frustrated gesture. "Are men and women just names? Do their souls mean nothing? Is there no joy or happiness in life? Are wealth and pleasure and empty titles the only things that matter? I thought so highly of you, Aline. I thought you were almost divine. You have a joyful heart and a smart mind. I thought you could see past the fake things and find real meaning. Yet you will give it all up for a fairy tale. You will sell your soul and your body to be the Marquise de La Tour d'Azyr."

"You are being rude," she said. She frowned, but her eyes were laughing. "And you jump to conclusions too fast. My uncle will only agree to let him try to win my consent. My uncle and I understand each other. I am not going to be traded like a vegetable."

He stopped and faced her. His eyes were burning, and his pale cheeks turned red. 

"You have been torturing me just to amuse yourself," he cried. "Well, I forgive you because I am so relieved."

"You are going too fast again, Cousin Andre. I let my uncle agree that the Marquis can court me. I like how he looks. I am flattered that he chose me, especially since he is so important. I might want to share his importance. The Marquis does not look boring. It should be interesting to have him court me. It might be even more interesting to marry him. When I think about it, I probably will decide to marry him."

He looked at her. He looked at her sweet, beautiful, childlike face framed in white fur. All the life seemed to drain from his face. 

"God help you, Aline," he groaned.

She stamped her foot. She thought he was being very annoying and a bit arrogant. 

"You are insolent, monsieur."

"It is never insolent to pray, Aline. I was only praying. I will keep praying. You will need my prayers, I think."

"You are insufferable." She was getting angry now. He could see her frown deepen and her face get red.

"That is because I am suffering. Oh, Aline, my little cousin. Think carefully about what you are doing. Think about the real things you are trading for these fake titles. You will never know those real things because the fake ones will block your way. When M. de La Tour d'Azyr comes to court you, study him well. Use your good instincts. Let your noble nature judge this animal. Consider that..."

"I consider that you are taking advantage of my kindness," she said. "You are abusing my tolerance. Who are you? What are you, that you have the nerve to speak to me like this?"

He bowed. He went back to being cold and detached, and he put back on his mocking attitude. 

"My congratulations, mademoiselle. You are adapting quickly to the great role you are going to play."

"You need to adapt too, monsieur," she snapped, turning her shoulder to him.

"I will adapt to being the dirt under the proud feet of Madame la Marquise. I hope I will know my place in the future."

His words stopped her. She turned back to him, and he saw that her eyes were watery. Instantly, his mocking tone changed to regret. 

"Lord, I am a beast, Aline," he cried, stepping toward her. "Forgive me if you can."

She was almost ready to ask him for forgiveness. But his apology meant she did not have to. 

"I will try," she said. "But only if you promise not to do it again."

"But I will," he said. "That is just how I am. I will fight to save you, even from yourself if I have to. It doesn't matter if you forgive me or not."

They stood there looking at each other. They were both breathing a little hard and looking a bit defiant. Then the others came out of the porch.

The Marquis of La Tour d'Azyr came first. He was the Count of Solz, a Knight of the Orders of the Holy Ghost and Saint Louis, and a Brigadier in the King's armies. He was a tall, graceful man. He stood straight like a soldier and held his head high and proud. He wore magnificent clothes. His mulberry velvet coat had wide skirts and gold lace. His velvet waistcoat was a golden apricot color. He wore black silk breeches and stockings. His red-heeled shoes were shiny and had diamond buckles. His powdered hair was tied back with a wide ribbon made of watered silk. He carried a small three-cornered hat under his arm. A thin, gold-hilted dress sword hung at his side.

Andre-Louis watched him carefully. He noticed how magnificent he looked and how smoothly he moved. The Marquis had a grand way about him that mixed pride with politeness in a strange way. Andre-Louis trembled for Aline. This man was an expert at winning women over. His romantic successes were famous. He drove mothers with marriageable daughters crazy, and he ruined the lives of husbands with pretty wives.

M. de Kercadiou followed right behind him. He was the exact opposite of the Marquis. The Lord of Gavrillac had very short legs. His body was starting to get fat at age forty-five. He had an enormous head, but he was not very smart. His face was pink and covered in spots from the smallpox that almost killed him when he was young. His clothes were so careless they were almost messy. He had never married, ignoring the first duty of a gentleman to produce an heir. Because of his looks and his bachelor life, the people in the countryside called him a woman-hater.

M. de Vilmorin came next. He was very pale and quiet. His lips were tight, and he looked worried.

A very elegant young gentleman stepped out of the carriage to meet them. It was the Chevalier de Chabrillane, the cousin of M. de La Tour d'Azyr. He had been waiting for the Marquis to return. He had watched Andre-Louis and mademoiselle talking with great interest, without them knowing he was there.

M. de La Tour d'Azyr saw Aline. He left the other men and walked straight across the terrace to her with long steps.

He nodded to Andre-Louis with a mix of politeness and superiority. The young lawyer had a strange place in society. Because of his birth, he was not considered a noble, but he was not a commoner either. He was somewhere in between. Neither class claimed him, but both classes treated him familiarly. Andre-Louis coldly returned the greeting and walked away to join his friend.

The Marquis took mademoiselle's hand. He bowed over it and kissed it. 

"Mademoiselle," he said. He looked deep into her blue eyes, which smiled back calmly. "Your uncle has done me the honor of allowing me to pay my respects to you. Will you do me the honor of receiving me tomorrow? I have something very important to tell you."

"Something important, M. le Marquis? You almost frighten me." But her calm little face in its fur hood showed no fear. She had learned how to act at Versailles for a reason.

"That is certainly not my goal," he said.

"But is it important to you, monsieur, or to me?"

"To both of us, I hope," he answered. His fine, passionate eyes were full of meaning.

"You make me curious, monsieur. And, of course, I am an obedient niece. I will be honored to receive you."

"Not honored, mademoiselle. You will give the honor. Tomorrow at this time, I will have the joy of visiting you."

He bowed again and kissed her fingers once more. She curtsied. With that polite and formal greeting finished, they parted.

She was breathing a little fast now. She was a little blinded by the man's beauty, his princely manner, and the power he seemed to show. Without thinking, she compared him to Andre-Louis. Andre-Louis was thin and rude, wearing a plain brown coat and steel-buckled shoes. She felt guilty for letting Andre-Louis say those presumptuous things. Tomorrow, the Marquis would offer her a great position and a high rank. She felt she had already lessened the dignity he was going to give her just by listening to the insult. She would not allow it again. She would not be so weak and childish as to let Andre-Louis make fun of a man who made him look like a servant.

Her vanity and ambition argued with her better self. To her great annoyance, her better self was not entirely convinced.

Meanwhile, M. de La Tour d'Azyr climbed into his carriage. He said goodbye to M. de Kercadiou. He also said a word to M. de Vilmorin, and M. de Vilmorin just bowed silently in agreement. The carriage drove away. The powdered footman in blue and gold stood stiffly on the back. M. de La Tour d'Azyr bowed to mademoiselle, and she waved back.

Then M. de Vilmorin put his arm through Andre-Louis's arm. "Come on, Andre," he said.

"But you will stay for dinner, both of you," the hospitable Lord of Gavrillac cried. "We will drink a special toast." He winked and looked toward mademoiselle as she walked up. He was a simple, good-hearted man.

M. de Vilmorin apologized. He said he had an appointment that stopped him from staying. He was very stiff and formal.

"And you, Andre?"

"Me? Oh, I share the appointment, godfather," he lied. "And I am superstitious about toasts." He did not want to stay. He was angry at Aline for smiling at M. de La Tour d'Azyr. He was angry at the dirty deal he saw her ready to make. He was suffering because he had lost an illusion.
