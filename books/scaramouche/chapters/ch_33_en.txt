Chapter 13. Sanctuary

It was late afternoon on that endless day of horror. The air was filled with constant alarms, volleys of musket fire, rolling drums, and the distant rumble of angry crowds. Inside their handsome house on the Rue du Paradis, Madame de Plougastel and Aline sat waiting. They were no longer waiting for Rougane. They realized that, whatever the reason—and by now many reasons surely existed—this friendly messenger would not return. They waited without knowing what for. They simply waited for whatever might happen.

Earlier in the afternoon, the roar of battle approached them. It raced swiftly in their direction, growing louder and more terrifying with each moment. It was the frenzied clamor of a mob, drunk with blood and intent on destruction. Close by, that fierce wave of humanity halted its turbulent advance. Then came the sound of pikes striking a door and imperious shouts to open it. After that, timbers splintered, glass shattered, and screams of terror mixed with screams of rage. Cutting through these shrill sounds was the deeper, booming sound of bestial laughter.

It was a hunt for two wretched Swiss guardsmen, blindly trying to escape. They were tracked down in a nearby house and cruelly put to death by that demonic mob. Once their gruesome task was done, the hunters, both men and women, formed a battalion. They came swinging down the Rue du Paradis, chanting the song of Marseilles—a song new to Paris in those days:

Allons, enfants de la patrie!
    Le jour de gloire est arrivé
    Contre nous de la tyrannie
    L’étendard sanglant est levé.

The song came nearer, raucously shouted by hundreds of voices. It was a dreadful sound. It had so suddenly replaced, at least temporarily, the merry, trivial tune of the "Ça ira!" which until then had been the revolutionary anthem. Instinctively, Madame de Plougastel and Aline clung to each other. They had heard the sacking of that other house in the neighborhood, without knowing the reason. What if it was now the Hotel Plougastel's turn? There was no real cause to fear it, except that amidst a turmoil they barely understood, and which was therefore all the more terrifying, the worst always had to be feared.

The dreadful song, so dreadfully sung, and the thunder of heavily booted feet upon the roughly paved street, passed by and faded into the distance. They breathed again, almost as if a miracle had saved them, only to be startled again an instant later. Madame's young footman, Jacques, the most trusted of her servants, burst into their presence unceremoniously with a scared face. He announced that a man who had just climbed over the garden wall claimed to be a friend of madame's and wished to be brought to her immediately.

“But he looks like a sansculotte, madame,” the loyal fellow warned her.

Her thoughts and hopes leapt at once to Rougane.

“Bring him in,” she commanded breathlessly.

Jacques went out, and soon returned with a tall man. He wore a long, shabby, and very ample overcoat and a wide-brimmed hat that was turned down all around, adorned with an enormous tricolor cockade. He removed this hat as he entered.

Jacques, standing behind him, noticed that his hair, though now somewhat disheveled, showed signs of having been carefully dressed. It was tied back in a club, and still had some lingering traces of powder. The young footman wondered what it was about the man's face, which was turned away from him, that made his mistress cry out and recoil. Then, with a gesture, he was abruptly dismissed.

The newcomer advanced to the middle of the drawing-room, moving like a man exhausted and breathing hard. There he leaned against a table, facing Madame de Plougastel across it. She stood regarding him, a strange horror in her eyes.

In the background, on a long bench at the far end of the drawing-room, sat Aline. She stared in bewilderment and some fear at a face that, though unrecognizable beneath the mask of blood and dust that smeared it, was still familiar. Then the man spoke, and she instantly recognized the voice as that of the Marquis de La Tour d’Azyr.

“My dear friend,” he said, “forgive me if I startled you. Forgive me if I forced my way in here without permission, at such a time, in such a manner. But you see my situation. I am a fugitive. During my frantic flight, not knowing where to turn for safety, I thought of you. I told myself that if I could just safely reach your house, I might find sanctuary.”

“You are in danger?”

“In danger?” He almost seemed to laugh silently at the unnecessary question. “If I were to show myself openly in the streets just now, I might with luck manage to live for five minutes! My friend, it has been a massacre. A few of us escaped from the Tuileries at the end, only to be hunted to death in the streets. I doubt if a single Swiss guard survives by this time. They had the worst of it, poor souls. And as for us, my God! They hate us more than they hate the Swiss. That's why I'm wearing this filthy disguise.”

He peeled off the shaggy greatcoat. He cast it from him and stepped forth in the black satin that had been the uniform of the hundred knights of the dagger who had rallied in the Tuileries that morning to defend their king.

His coat was torn across the back. His neckcloth and the ruffles at his wrists were torn and bloodstained. With his smeared face and disheveled hair, he was a terrible sight. Yet he managed to carry himself with his usual easy assurance, and remembered to kiss the trembling hand Madame de Plougastel extended to him in welcome.

“You did well to come to me, Gervais,” she said. “Yes, here is sanctuary for now. You will be quite safe, at least for as long as we are safe. My servants are completely trustworthy. Sit down and tell me all.”

He obeyed her, almost collapsing into the armchair she pushed forward. He was a man exhausted, either by physical exertion, by nerve-strain, or both. He drew a handkerchief from his pocket and wiped some of the blood and dirt from his face.

“It is soon told.” His tone was bitter, filled with despair. “This, my dear, is the end of us. Plougastel is lucky to be across the frontier at such a time. If I hadn't been foolish enough to trust those who today have proven themselves utterly unworthy of trust, that's where I would be myself. My remaining in Paris is the crowning folly of a life full of foolishness and mistakes. That I should come to you in my hour of most urgent need only emphasizes it.” He laughed in his bitterness.

“And now?” she asked him, moistening her dry lips.

“It only remains to get away as soon as possible, if it is still possible. Here in France, there is no longer any room for us—at least, not above ground. Today has proven it.” Then he looked up at her, standing beside him, so pale and timid, and he smiled. He patted the fine hand that rested upon the arm of his chair. “My dear Therese, unless your charity extends to giving me something to drink, you will see me perish of thirst before your very eyes, before the canaille even has a chance to finish me.”

She gasped. “I should have thought of it!” she cried, reproaching herself, and she turned quickly. “Aline,” she begged, “tell Jacques to bring something.”

“Aline!” he echoed, interrupting her, and swinging around in his turn. Then, as Aline rose into view, emerging from the background, and he finally noticed her, he abruptly heaved himself back to his weary legs. He stood there, stiffly bowing to her across the gleaming floor. “Mademoiselle, I had not suspected your presence,” he said, and he seemed extraordinarily ill at ease, like a man startled, as if caught in an illicit act.

“I noticed it, monsieur,” she answered, as she advanced to carry out madame's request. She paused before him. “From my heart, monsieur, I am deeply sorry that we should meet again in such painful circumstances.”

Not since the day of his duel with André-Louis, the day which had seen the death and burial of his last hope of winning her, had they stood face to face.

He paused as if about to answer her. His glance strayed to Madame de Plougastel, and, oddly quiet for someone who could be very eloquent, he bowed in silence.

"Please, monsieur, sit down. You are fatigued."

"It's gracious of you to notice," he said. "With your permission, then." He sat back down. She continued to the door and left to do her errand.

When she returned a short while later, they had almost inexplicably switched places. Madame de Plougastel was now seated in the brocade and gilt armchair. Monsieur de La Tour d'Azyr, despite his weariness, was leaning over the back of it, talking earnestly. His posture suggested he was pleading with her. As Aline entered, he immediately stopped and moved away. She felt as though she had intruded. She also noticed that the Countess was in tears.

The diligent Jacques followed her in a moment, carrying a tray laden with food and wine. Madame poured for her guest. He drank a long draught of the Burgundy. Then, holding out his grimy hands, he asked if he could tidy himself up before sitting down to eat.

Jacques led him away and helped him tidy up. When he returned, he had removed every last trace of the rough treatment he had endured. He looked almost like his usual self. His clothes were neat, and his bearing was calm, dignified, and courtly. However, his face was very pale and haggard. He seemed to have suddenly aged, appearing to be the age he actually was.

He ate and drank with appetite, explaining that he hadn't tasted food since early morning. As he did so, he recounted the dreadful events of the day in detail. He gave them the specifics of his own escape from the Tuileries. That was when all hope seemed lost, and the Swiss, having fired their last cartridge, were being massacred by the indescribably furious mob.

"Oh, it was all very poorly handled," he concluded critically. "We were timid when we should have been resolute, and then resolute when it was finally too late. That has been the story of our side since the beginning of this accursed struggle. We have lacked proper leadership throughout. Now, as I have already said, it is over for us. All that remains is to escape, as soon as we can figure out how to do it."

Madame told him about the hopes she had placed in Rougane.

This lifted him out of his gloom. He felt optimistic.

"You are wrong to have given up that hope," he assured her. "If this mayor is so well-disposed, he can certainly do as his son promised. But last night, it would have been too late for him to reach you. And today, even if he had come to Paris, it would have been almost impossible for him to cross the streets from the other side. It's most likely that he will still come. I pray that he does, because knowing that you and Mademoiselle de Kercadiou are out of this would comfort me above all."

"We should take you with us," Madame said.

"Ah! But how?"

"Young Rougane was supposed to bring me permits for three people: Aline, myself, and my footman, Jacques. You would take Jacques's place."

"Honestly, Madame, to get out of Paris, there's no man whose place I wouldn't take." He laughed.

Their spirits rose with his, and their flagging hopes revived. But as dusk fell upon the city again, with no sign of the deliverer they awaited, those hopes began to fade once more.

Monsieur de La Tour d'Azyr finally pleaded weariness. He asked to be allowed to withdraw so he could try to get some rest, preparing for whatever might have to be faced in the immediate future. When he had gone, Madame persuaded Aline to go and lie down.

"I will call you, my dear, the moment he arrives," she said. She bravely maintained that pretense of confidence, though it had by now entirely evaporated.

Aline kissed her affectionately and left. She was outwardly so calm and unperturbed that it left the Countess wondering. Did Aline realize the peril surrounding them? That danger was infinitely increased by the presence in the house of a man as widely known and detested as Monsieur de La Tour d'Azyr. His enemies were probably searching for him at this very moment.

Left alone, Madame lay down on a couch in the salon itself, ready for any emergency. It was a hot summer night. The glass doors opening onto the luxuriant garden stood wide to let in the air. On that air, intermittently from the distance, came sounds of the populace's continuing horrible activities, the aftermath of that bloody day.

Madame de Plougastel lay there, listening to those sounds for over an hour. She thanked Heaven that, for the moment at least, the disturbances were distant. She dreaded that at any moment they might occur closer by. She feared that this Bondy section, where her hotel was located, might become the scene of horrors similar to those whose echoes reached her ears from other sections to the south and west.

The couch where the Countess lay was in shadow. All the lights in that long salon had been extinguished, except for a cluster of candles. These were in a massive silver candle branch placed on a round marquetry table in the middle of the room. It was an island of light in the surrounding gloom.

The timepiece on the overmantel melodiously chimed ten o'clock. Then, startling in its suddenness, another sound broke the immediate silence and vibrated through the house. It brought Madame to her feet, a breathless mix of hope and dread. Someone was knocking sharply on the door below. Moments of agonizing suspense followed, culminating in the abrupt entrance of the footman, Jacques, into the room. He looked around, not seeing his mistress at first.

"Madame! Madame!" he panted, out of breath.

"What is it, Jacques!" Her voice was steady now that the need for self-control seemed forced upon her. She advanced from the shadows into that island of light around the table. "There is a man downstairs. He is asking... he is demanding to see you at once."

"A man?" she questioned.

"He... he seems to be an official. At least, he wears the sash of office. And he refuses to give any name. He says that his name would mean nothing to you. He insists that he must see you in person and at once."

"An official?" Madame said.

"An official," Jacques repeated. "I would not have let him in, but he demanded it in the name of the Nation. Madame, it is for you to say what should be done. Robert is with me. If you wish it... whatever it may be..."

"My good Jacques, no, no." She was perfectly composed. "If this man intended evil, surely he would not come alone. Bring him to me, and then ask Mademoiselle de Kercadiou to join me if she is awake."

Jacques departed, partly reassured himself. Madame seated herself in the armchair by the table, well within the light. She smoothed her dress with a mechanical hand. If, as it seemed, her hopes had been futile, so had her momentary fears. A man on any errand other than peace would have brought some followers with him, as she had said.

The door opened again, and Jacques reappeared. A slight man, wearing a wide-brimmed hat decorated with a tricolour cockade, stepped briskly past him. He wore a broad tricolour sash around the waist of his olive-green riding-coat. A sword hung at his side.

He took off his hat, and the candlelight shone on the steel buckle at the front. Madame was silently observed by a pair of large, dark eyes set in a lean, brown face. His eyes were incredibly focused and searching.

She leaned forward, and disbelief crossed her face. Then her eyes lit up, and color returned to her pale cheeks. She rose suddenly. She was trembling.

"Andre-Louis!" she exclaimed.
