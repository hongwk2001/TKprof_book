CHAPTER 10. THE RETURNING CARRIAGE

Monsieur de Kercadiou wrote a letter.

"Godson," he began, without any polite address, "I have learned with pain and anger that you have dishonored yourself again by breaking the promise you gave me to stay out of politics. With even greater pain and anger, I learn that your name has become a scandal in just a few days, that you have abandoned the weapon of false, deceptive arguments against my class—the class to which you owe everything—for an assassin's sword. It has come to my attention that you have an appointment tomorrow with my good friend, Monsieur de La Tour d’Azyr. A gentleman of his standing has certain obligations imposed by his birth, which do not permit him to back out of an engagement. But you have no such disadvantages. For a man of your background, refusing a duel of honor, or neglecting it once made, involves no sacrifice. Your peers will probably think you are showing commendable caution. Therefore, I beg you, and indeed, if I thought I still had any authority over you, which the favors you have received from me should grant me, I would command you, to let this matter go no farther, and to refrain from going to your appointment tomorrow morning. Since I have no such authority, as your past conduct now makes clear, and no reason to hope that a proper sense of gratitude to me will make you listen to this, my most earnest request, I am compelled to add that should you survive tomorrow’s encounter, I can in no circumstances ever again acknowledge your existence. If any spark of the affection you once expressed for me remains, or if you value the affection that, despite all you have done to forfeit it, is the main reason for this letter, you will not refuse to do as I am asking."

It was not a tactful letter. Monsieur de Kercadiou was not a tactful man. André-Louis, when the groom delivered it to him that Sunday afternoon in Paris, could only see concern for Monsieur de La Tour d’Azyr in the letter. Kercadiou called La Tour d’Azyr his good friend and prospective nephew-in-law.

He kept the groom waiting a full hour while composing his answer. Brief though it was, it cost him a great deal of effort and several unsuccessful attempts. He finally wrote this:

My dear godfather, you make refusal incredibly hard for me when you appeal to my affection. It is something for which I will always welcome the chance to give you proofs, and I am therefore more heartbroken than I can say that I cannot give you the proof you ask for today. There is too much between Monsieur de La Tour d’Azyr and me. Also, you do me and my class, whatever it may be, an injustice when you say that obligations of honor do not bind us. I consider them so binding that, even if I wanted to, I could not back out now.

If you persist in the harsh intention you express, I must accept it. Be assured that I will suffer.

Your affectionate and grateful godson,

André-Louis

He dispatched that letter by Monsieur de Kercadiou’s groom and considered this the end of the matter. It pained him deeply, but he bore the wound with the outward stoicism he always showed.

Next morning, at a quarter past eight, as he and Le Chapelier, who had come to have breakfast with him, were rising from the table to head out for the Bois, his housekeeper startled him by announcing Mademoiselle de Kercadiou.

He looked at his watch. Although his cabriolet was already at the door, he had a few minutes to spare. He excused himself to Le Chapelier and walked quickly to the anteroom.

She advanced to meet him, her manner eager, almost feverish.

"I won't pretend not to know why you're here," he said quickly, to get straight to the point. "But time is short, and I warn you that only the most compelling reasons are worth mentioning."

It surprised her. It was a rebuff right from the start, before she had uttered a word, and that was the last thing she had expected from André-Louis. Moreover, there was about him an air of aloofness that was unusual when he was with her, and his voice had been remarkably cold and formal.

It wounded her. She couldn't guess the conclusion he had jumped to. He made the same mistake with her, which was natural enough, that he had made with his godfather's letter yesterday. He believed the sole motivation for her visit was concern for Monsieur de La Tour d’Azyr. It never occurred to him that she might be concerned for *him*. He was so utterly convinced of the inevitable outcome of that meeting that he couldn't imagine anyone fearing for his safety.

What he assumed to be anxiety for the predestined victim had irritated him in Monsieur de Kercadiou; in Aline, it filled him with cold anger. He concluded from it that she hadn't been entirely honest with him, that ambition was pushing her to look favorably on Monsieur de La Tour d’Azyr's courtship. And nothing could have spurred him on more relentlessly in his purpose, since saving her was, in his eyes, almost as important as avenging the past.

She studied him intently, and his complete calm at such a time amazed her. She couldn't help but mention it.

"How calm you are, André!"

"I am not easily disturbed. It's a point of pride for me."

"But... Oh, André, this meeting can't happen!" She came close up to him, to set her hands upon his shoulders, and stood there, her face less than a foot from his.

"You know, of course, of some good reason why it should not?" he said.

"You may be killed," she answered him, and her eyes widened as she spoke.

It was so far from what he expected that he could only stare at her for a moment. Then he thought he had understood. He laughed as he removed her hands from his shoulders and stepped back. This was a shallow trick, childish and beneath her.

"Do you really think you can win by trying to scare me?" he asked, and almost sneered.

"Oh, you must be mad! Monsieur de La Tour d’Azyr is known as the most dangerous swordsman in France."

"Have you never noticed that most reputations are unearned? Chabrillane was a dangerous swordsman, and Chabrillane is dead and buried. La Motte-Royau was an even more dangerous swordsman, and now he's in a surgeon's care. So are the other hired swordsmen who dreamed of skewering a poor, defenseless provincial lawyer. And here today comes the leader, the finest example of these bully-swordsmen. He's here for long overdue wages. Be sure of that. So if you have no other reason to offer..."

His sarcasm mystified her. Could he possibly be serious in his confidence that he would win against Monsieur de La Tour d’Azyr? With her limited knowledge, and her mind full of her uncle's opposing conviction, it seemed to her that André-Louis was just acting. He would play a part until the very end.

Regardless, she changed her approach to answer him.

“You had my uncle’s letter?”

“And I answered it.”

“I know. But he will do what he said. Don't imagine he'll change his mind if you go through with this terrible plan.”

He said, “Now, that's a better reason than the other. If anything in the world could move me, it would be that. But there's too much between La Tour d’Azyr and me. I swore an oath on the dead hand of Philippe de Vilmorin. I never could have hoped God would give me such a great chance to keep it.”

“You haven't kept it yet,” she warned him.

He smiled at her. “True!” he said. “But nine o'clock will be here soon. Tell me,” he asked her suddenly, “why didn't you take this request of yours to Monsieur de La Tour d’Azyr?”

“I did,” she answered him, and she flushed as she remembered being rejected yesterday. He interpreted her blush quite differently.

“And he?” he asked.

“Monsieur de La Tour d’Azyr's obligations...” she began. Then she broke off to answer shortly, “Oh, he refused.”

“I see, I see. He had to, of course, no matter the cost to him. Yet, if I were in his shoes, I would have considered the cost nothing. But men are different, you see.” He sighed. “Also, if I were in your place, and that had happened, I think I would have dropped the matter there. But then...”

“I don't understand you, André.”

“I'm not that obscure. Not nearly as obscure as I can be. Think about it. It might help to comfort you soon.” He checked his watch again. “Please use this house as your own. I have to go.”

Le Chapelier poked his head in the door.

“Forgive the intrusion. But we'll be late, André, unless you...”

“Coming,” André answered him. “If you'll wait for my return, Aline, you'll do me a great favor. Especially considering your uncle's determination.”

She didn't answer him. She felt numb. He took her silence as agreement, and, bowing, he left her. Standing there, she heard his steps going down the stairs, along with Le Chapelier's. He was speaking to his friend, and his voice was calm and normal.

Oh, he was mad, blinded by self-confidence and vanity. As his carriage rattled away, she sank down limply, feeling exhausted and nauseous. She felt sick and faint with horror. André-Louis was going to his death. A conviction of it, an unreasoning conviction, perhaps the result of all Monsieur de Kercadiou's rantings, entered her soul. For a while, she sat there, paralyzed by hopelessness. Then she sprang up again, wringing her hands. She had to do something to prevent this horror. But what could she do? Following him to the Bois and intervening there would only create a scandal for no reason. All the rules of proper behavior were against her, forming a barrier that she couldn't cross. Was there no one who could help her?

Standing there, half-frenzied by her helplessness, she again heard the sound of vehicles and hooves on the cobblestones of the street below. A carriage was approaching. It pulled up with a clatter in front of the fencing academy. Could it be André-Louis returning? She passionately clutched at that sliver of hope. A loud, urgent knocking came from the door. She heard André-Louis' housekeeper, her wooden shoes clanking on the stairs, hurrying down to open it.

She rushed to the anteroom door, pulled it wide, and stood there, breathless, to listen. But the voice that drifted up to her was not the one she so desperately hoped to hear. It was a woman's voice, urgently asking for Monsieur André-Louis. The voice was vaguely familiar at first, then clearly recognized as that of Madame de Plougastel.

Excited, she ran to the top of the narrow staircase just in time to hear Madame de Plougastel exclaim in agitation:

“He has gone already! Oh, but how long ago? Which way did he go?”

This was enough to tell Aline that Madame de Plougastel's errand must be similar to her own. At that moment, with her mind in such general distress and confusion, her thoughts focused entirely on the one vital point. She found nothing astonishing in this. Madame de Plougastel's unusual regard for André-Louis seemed like a sufficient explanation to her then.

Without pausing to think, she ran down the steep staircase, calling:

“Madame! Madame!”

The plump, attractive housekeeper stepped aside, and the two ladies faced each other on the threshold. Madame de Plougastel looked pale and haggard, with a nameless dread staring from her eyes.

“Aline! You here!” she exclaimed. Then, with urgency sweeping aside all other thoughts, she asked, “Were you also too late?”

“No, madame. I saw him. I begged him. But he wouldn't listen.”

“Oh, this is horrible!” Madame de Plougastel shuddered as she spoke. “I only heard about it half an hour ago, and I came at once, to prevent it at all costs.”

The two women looked at each other, blank and despairing. In the sunshine-flooded street, one or two shabby idlers paused to stare at the handsome carriage with its magnificent bay horses, and at the two great ladies on the doorstep of the fencing academy. From across the way came the loud voice of a traveling bellows-mender, calling out his trade:

“A raccommoder les vieux soufflets!”

Madame swung to the housekeeper.

“How long is it since monsieur left?”

“Ten minutes, maybe; hardly more.” The good woman kept a calm, unreadable expression. She assumed these important ladies were friends of her master's latest victim, who was known for always winning.

Madame wrung her hands. “Ten minutes! Oh!” It was almost a moan. “Which way did he go?”

Aline informed her, “The meeting is set for nine o’clock in the Bois de Boulogne. Could we follow? And if we did, could we even make a difference?”

“Ah, my God!” Madame cried. “The question is, can we get there in time? At nine o’clock! It’s barely more than fifteen minutes away. My God! My God!” Madame clasped and unclasped her hands in anguish. “Do you know, at least, where in the Bois they are supposed to meet?”

“No, only that it’s in the Bois.”

“In the Bois!” Madame was thrown into a frenzy. “The Bois is nearly half the size of Paris!” But she swept breathlessly on, “Come, Aline, get in, get in!”

Then she spoke to her coachman. “To the Bois de Boulogne, by way of the Cours la Reine,” she commanded, “as fast as you can drive. You’ll get ten pistoles if we arrive in time. Whip up, man!”

She pushed Aline into the carriage and sprang in after her with the energy of a young girl. The heavy carriage, far too heavy for this race against time, was already moving before she had even sat down. It rocked and lurched, drawing curses from more than one pedestrian it narrowly missed crushing against a wall or trampling underfoot.

Madame sat back with closed eyes and trembling lips. Her face was very white and drawn. Aline watched her in silence. It almost seemed to her that Madame de Plougastel was suffering as deeply as she was, enduring an anguish of apprehension as great as her own.

Aline would wonder about this later. But at that moment, her half-numbed mind could only focus on their desperate mission.

The carriage finally rolled across the Place Louis XV and onto the Cours la Reine. Along that beautiful, tree-lined avenue between the Champs Elysees and the Seine, almost empty at this hour of the day, they picked up speed, leaving a cloud of dust behind them.

Even though they were driving dangerously fast, it felt too slow to the women in the carriage. As they reached the barrier at the end of the Cours, nine o'clock was striking in the city behind them. Every stroke seemed to sound a note of doom.

Yet here at the barrier, regulations forced a momentary halt. Aline asked the sergeant in charge how long it had been since a cabriolet like the one she described had passed that way. He replied that about twenty minutes ago, a vehicle had gone through the barrier. It carried the deputy, Monsieur le Chapelier, and the Paladin of the Third Estate, Monsieur Moreau. The sergeant was very well informed. He said with a grin that he could make a good guess about the business that took Monsieur Moreau that way so early in the day.

They left him and sped on through the open country, following the road that continued to run alongside the river. They sat back, silently despairing, staring hopelessly ahead, Aline's hand clasped tight in Madame's. In the distance, across the meadows on their right, they could already see the long, dark line of trees of the Bois. Soon, the carriage swung aside, following a branch of the road that turned right, away from the river, and headed straight for the forest.

Aline finally broke the hopeless silence that had settled between them since they passed the barrier.

“Oh, it’s impossible that we’ll get there in time! Impossible!”

“Don’t say it! Don’t say it!” Madame cried out.

“But it’s long past nine, Madame! André would be punctual, and these... affairs don’t take long. It... it will be all over by now.”

Madame shivered and closed her eyes. Soon, however, she opened them again and stirred. Then she put her head out the window. “A carriage is approaching,” she announced, her tone revealing her fear.

“Not already! Oh, not already!” Aline said, voicing the thought they both shared. She found it hard to breathe, feeling a sudden need for air. Something in her throat was throbbing as if it would suffocate her; a mist came and went before her eyes.

An open caleche was speeding towards them in a cloud of dust, coming from the Bois. They watched it, both pale. Neither dared to speak. Aline, in fact, had no breath to do so.

As it approached, it had to slow down, just as they did, to allow for a safe passage on the narrow road. Aline was at the window with Madame de Plougastel. With fearful eyes, both looked into the open carriage as it drew alongside them.

“Which of them is it, Madame? Oh, which of them?” Aline gasped, barely daring to look, her head spinning.

On the side closest to them sat a dark-haired young gentleman, unknown to either of the ladies. He was smiling as he spoke to his companion. A moment later, the man sitting beyond him came into view. He was not smiling. His face was white and grim, and it was the face of the Marquis de La Tour d'Azyr.

For a long moment, both women stared at him in speechless horror. Then, as he noticed them, a look of utter surprise spread across his stern face.

In that moment, with a long, shuddering sigh, Aline fainted and collapsed to the carriage floor behind Madame de Plougastel.
