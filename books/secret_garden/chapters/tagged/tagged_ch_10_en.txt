Chapter 10: Dickon

The sun shone down for nearly a week on the secret garden. "The Secret Garden" was what Mary called it when she was thinking of it. She liked the name, and she liked even more the feeling that when its beautiful old walls shut her in, no one knew where she was. It felt almost like being shut out of the world in some fairy place. The few books she had read and liked had been fairy tales, and she had read of secret gardens in some of the stories. In those stories, people sometimes fell asleep in them for a hundred years, which she had always thought was rather silly. She had no intention of falling asleep. In fact, she was becoming wider awake with every day that passed at Misselthwaite. She was beginning to enjoy being outdoors; she no longer hated the wind, but loved it. She could run faster and longer, and she could skip up to a hundred. The bulbs in the secret garden must have been quite surprised. Mary cleared such nice, clean spaces around them that they had all the breathing room they wanted. Even though Mary didn't know it, the bulbs began to cheer up under the dark earth and work incredibly hard. The sun could reach them and warm them, and when the rain came down, it could water them directly, so they began to feel very much alive.

Mary was an odd, determined little person, and now that she had something interesting to be determined about, she was completely absorbed. She worked, dug, and pulled up weeds steadily, growing more pleased with her work every hour instead of getting tired of it. It felt like a fascinating game. She found many more of the sprouting, pale green shoots than she had ever hoped to find. They seemed to be popping up everywhere, and every day she was sure she found tiny new ones—some so small they barely peeked above the soil. There were so many that she remembered Martha's words about "snowdrops by the thousands," and about bulbs spreading and making new ones. These had been left to themselves for ten years, and perhaps they had spread, just like the snowdrops, into thousands. She wondered how long it would be before they showed that they were flowers. Sometimes she stopped digging just to look at the garden, trying to imagine what it would look like when it was covered with thousands of lovely things in bloom.

During that week of sunshine, she became closer with Ben Weatherstaff. She surprised him several times by suddenly appearing beside him, as if she had sprung right out of the earth. The truth was, she was afraid he would pick up his tools and go away if he saw her coming, so she always walked toward him as silently as possible. But he did not actually object to her presence as strongly as he had at first. Perhaps he was secretly flattered by her obvious desire for his elderly company. Besides, she was much more polite now. He did not know that when she first saw him, she had spoken to him as if he were a native servant in India. She hadn't realized that a stubborn, sturdy old Yorkshireman was not used to bowing to his masters or being ordered around by them.

<ben>"You're like the robin,"</ben> he said to her one morning when he lifted his head and saw her standing by him. <ben>"I never know when I'll see you, or which direction you'll come from."</ben>

<mary>"He's friends with me now,"</mary> Mary said.

<ben>"That's just like him,"</ben> snapped Ben Weatherstaff. <ben>"Flirting with the womenfolk just for vanity and show. There's nothing he wouldn't do to show off and flutter his tail feathers. He's as full of pride as an egg is full of meat."</ben>

Ben seldom talked much and sometimes didn't even answer Mary's questions except with a grunt, but this morning he was more talkative than usual. He stood up and rested one heavy boot on the top of his spade while he looked her over.

<ben>"How long have you been here?"</ben> he jerked out.

<mary>"I think it's about a month,"</mary> she answered.

<ben>"You're beginning to do Misselthwaite credit,"</ben> he said. <ben>"You're a bit fatter than you were, and you're not quite so yellow. You looked like a young plucked crow when you first came into this garden. I thought to myself that I'd never set eyes on an uglier, sourer-faced child."</ben>

Mary was not vain, and since she had never thought much of her looks, she was not bothered by this.

<mary>"I know I'm fatter,"</mary> she said. <mary>"My stockings are getting tighter. They used to be all wrinkled. Look, there's the robin, Ben Weatherstaff."</mary>

There, indeed, was the robin, and she thought he looked nicer than ever. His red breast was as glossy as satin, and he fluttered his wings and tail, tilted his head, and hopped about with all kinds of lively, graceful movements. He seemed determined to make Ben Weatherstaff admire him. But Ben was sarcastic.

<ben>"Aye, there you are!"</ben> he said. <ben>"You can put up with me for a bit when you've got no one better. You've been reddening up your breast and polishing your feathers these last two weeks. <mary>"Oh, look at him!"</mary> Mary exclaimed.

The robin was clearly in a bold, charming mood. He hopped closer and closer, looking at Ben Weatherstaff more and more engagingly. He flew onto the nearest currant bush, tilted his head, and sang a little song right at him.

<ben>"You think you'll win me over by doing that,"</ben> said Ben, wrinkling his face in a way that made Mary sure he was trying not to smile. <ben>"You think no one can resist you—that's what you think."</ben>

The robin spread his wings. Mary could hardly believe her eyes. He flew right up to the handle of Ben Weatherstaff's spade and landed on the very top of it. Then the old man's face slowly wrinkled into a brand new expression. He stood completely still, as if he were afraid to breathe—as if he wouldn't have moved for the world, lest his robin should fly away. He spoke in a whisper.

<ben>"Well, I'm danged!"</ben> he said, as softly as if he were saying a gentle prayer. <ben>"You really do know how to get to a chap—you do! You're almost unearthly, you're so clever."</ben>

He stood without moving—almost without breathing—until the robin fluttered its wings again and flew away. Then he stood looking at the handle of the spade as if there might be magic in it. Finally, he began to dig again and said nothing for several minutes.

But because he kept breaking into a slow grin now and then, Mary was not afraid to talk to him.

<mary>"Do you have a garden of your own?"</mary> she asked.

<ben>"No. I'm a bachelor, and I lodge with Martin at the gate."</ben>

<mary>"If you had one,"</mary> said Mary, <mary>"what would you plant?"</mary>

<mary>"But if you wanted to make a flower garden,"</mary> Mary persisted, <mary>"what would you plant?"</mary>

<ben>"Bulbs and sweet-smelling things—but mostly roses."</ben>

<mary>"Do you like roses?"</mary> she asked.

<ben>"Well, yes, I do. I was taught to love them by a young lady I was gardener to. She had a lot of them in a place she was fond of, and she loved them like they were children—or robins. <mary>"What happened to the roses?"</mary> Mary asked again, more interested than ever.

<ben>"They were left to themselves."</ben>

<mary>"Did they die? Do roses completely die when they are left to themselves?"</mary> she ventured.

Mary felt her face turn red. She was almost afraid to answer.

<mary>"I—I want to play that—that I have a garden of my own,"</mary> she stammered. <mary>"I—there is nothing for me to do. I have nothing—and no one."</mary>

He said it in such a strange way that Mary wondered if he was actually a little sorry for her. She had never felt sorry for herself; she had only felt tired and cross because she disliked people and things so much. But now the world seemed to be changing and getting nicer. If no one found out about the secret garden, she would enjoy herself forever.

She stayed with him for ten or fifteen minutes longer, asking him as many questions as she dared. He answered every one of them in his usual grunting way, but he did not seem truly angry, and he did not pick up his spade and leave her. He mentioned something about roses just as she was going away, which reminded her of the ones he had said he loved.

<mary>"Do you go and see those other roses now?"</mary> she asked.

<ben>"Haven't been this year. My rheumatism has made me too stiff in the joints."</ben>

He said it in his grumbling voice, and then quite suddenly, he seemed to get angry with her, though she did not see why he should.

<ben>"Now look here!"</ben> he said sharply. <ben>"Don't you ask so many questions. You're the worst girl for asking questions I've ever come across. Go along now and play. I'm done talking for today."</ben>

He said it so crossly that she knew there was no point in staying another minute. She went skipping slowly down the outside walk, thinking about him. She said to herself that, strange as it was, here was another person she liked despite his grumpiness. She liked old Ben Weatherstaff. Yes, she did like him. She always wanted to try to make him talk to her. She also began to believe that he knew everything in the world about flowers.

There was a laurel-hedged path that curved around the secret garden and ended at a gate opening into a wood in the park. She decided to slip along this path and look into the wood to see if there were any rabbits hopping about. She enjoyed skipping very much, and when she reached the little gate, she opened it and went through because she heard a low, peculiar whistling sound and wanted to find out what it was.

It was a very strange sight indeed. She caught her breath as she stopped to look. A boy was sitting under a tree, leaning his back against it and playing a rough wooden pipe. He was a funny-looking boy, about twelve years old. He looked very clean, his nose turned up, and his cheeks were as red as poppies. Mary had never seen such round, blue eyes in any boy's face. Clinging to the trunk of the tree he leaned against was a brown squirrel, watching him. From behind a nearby bush, a cock pheasant was delicately stretching its neck to peek out, and quite close to him, two rabbits were sitting up, sniffing with trembling noses. It actually looked as if they had all gathered to watch him and listen to the strange, low little tune his pipe made.
