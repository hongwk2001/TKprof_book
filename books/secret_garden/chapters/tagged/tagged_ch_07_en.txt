Chapter 7: The Key to the Garden

Two days later, the moment Mary opened her eyes, she sat straight up in bed and called out to Martha.

<mary>"Look at the moor! Look at the moor!"</mary>

The rainstorm was over. During the night, the wind had swept away the gray mist and clouds, and then died down completely. Now, a brilliant, deep blue sky stretched high over the moorland. Mary had never imagined a sky could be so blue. In India, the skies were always hot and blazing. This sky was a deep, cool blue that seemed to sparkle like the water of a beautiful, bottomless lake. High up in the blue arch, small, snow-white clouds floated like fluffy fleece. The vast moor itself looked soft and blue now, instead of its usual gloomy purple-black or dreary gray.

<martha>"Yes,"</martha> Martha said with a cheerful grin. <martha>"The storm is over for now. It often does this at this time of year. It clears up in a single night, as if pretending it was never here and never planned to return. That is because spring is on its way. It is still a long way off, but it is coming."</martha>

<mary>"I thought maybe it always rained or looked dark in England,"</mary> Mary said.

<martha>"Oh, no!"</martha> Martha said, sitting up on her heels among her polishing brushes. <martha>"Nothing of the sort!"</martha>

<mary>"What does that mean?"</mary> Mary asked seriously. In India, the local people spoke different dialects that only a few understood, so she was not surprised when Martha used words she did not know.

Martha laughed just as she had on their first morning together.

<martha>"There now,"</martha> she said. <martha>"I am speaking broad Yorkshire again, even though Mrs. Medlock told me not to. 'Nowt of the sort' means 'nothing of the sort,'"</martha> she explained slowly and carefully, <martha>"but it takes so much longer to say it that way. Yorkshire is the sunniest place on earth when the sun does shine. I told you that you would like the moor after a while. Just wait until you see the golden gorse blossoms, the yellow broom, and the purple bells of the heather. There will be hundreds of butterflies fluttering, bees humming, and skylarks soaring up and singing. You will want to go out there at sunrise and spend the whole day on the moor, just like Dickon does."</martha>

<mary>"Could I ever go there?"</mary> Mary asked wistfully, looking out the window at the distant blue. It looked so new, vast, and wonderful, and the color was simply heavenly.

<martha>"I don't know,"</martha> Martha answered. <martha>"It seems to me you have never really used your legs since the day you were born. You couldn't walk five miles, and it is five miles to our cottage."</martha>

<mary>"I would like to see your cottage."</mary>

Martha stared at her curiously for a moment before picking up her polishing brush to rub the fireplace grate again. She noticed that Mary's small, plain face did not look nearly as sour now as it had on the first morning. It looked a little like her sister Susan Ann's face when she wanted something very much.

<martha>"I will ask my mother about it,"</martha> Martha said. <martha>"She is the kind of person who can almost always find a way to get things done. Today is my day off, and I am going home. Oh, I am so glad! Mrs. Medlock has a lot of respect for my mother. Maybe she can talk to her."</martha>

<mary>"I like your mother,"</mary> Mary said.

<martha>"I should think you would,"</martha> Martha agreed, polishing away.

<mary>"I have never seen her,"</mary> Mary said.

<martha>"No, you haven't,"</martha> Martha replied.

She sat back on her heels again and rubbed the tip of her nose with the back of her hand, looking puzzled for a moment, but then she spoke with absolute certainty.

<martha>"Well, she is so sensible, hard-working, good-natured, and clean that no one could help liking her, whether they have seen her or not. When I go home to her on my day off, I literally jump for joy while crossing the moor."</martha>

<mary>"I like Dickon, too,"</mary> Mary added. <mary>"And I have never seen him, either."</mary>

<martha>"Well,"</martha> Martha said stoutly, <martha>"I told you that the birds, the rabbits, the wild sheep, the ponies, and even the foxes like him. I wonder,"</martha> she added, staring at Mary thoughtfully, <martha>"what Dickon would think of you?"</martha>

<mary>"He wouldn't like me,"</mary> Mary said in her stiff, cold little way. <mary>"No one likes me."</mary>

Martha looked thoughtful again.

<martha>"How do you like yourself?"</martha> she asked, sounding genuinely curious.

Mary hesitated for a moment and thought about it.

<mary>"Not at all, really,"</mary> she answered. <mary>"But I never thought of that before."</mary>

Martha grinned a little, remembering a family story.

<martha>"My mother said that to me once,"</martha> Martha said. <martha>"She was working at her wash-tub, and I was in a terrible mood, complaining about everyone. She turned around and said to me, 'You young vixen! There you stand, saying you do not like this person and you do not like that person. How do you like yourself?' It made me laugh, and it brought me to my senses in an instant."</martha>

She left in high spirits as soon as she had served Mary breakfast. She was going to walk five miles across the moor to the cottage, help her mother wash clothes, do the weekly baking, and enjoy herself thoroughly.

Once Martha left, Mary felt lonelier than ever. She hurried out into the garden, and the first thing she did was run around the fountain flower garden ten times. She counted the laps carefully, and by the time she finished, she felt much better. The sunshine transformed the entire landscape. The high, deep blue sky arched over Misselthwaite Manor just as it did over the moor. She kept tilting her head back to look up at it, trying to imagine what it would feel like to lie on one of those fluffy white clouds and float away. She walked into the first kitchen garden and found Ben Weatherstaff working with two other gardeners. The beautiful weather seemed to have put him in a better mood, and he actually spoke to her first.

<ben>"Spring is coming,"</ben> he said. <ben>"Can't you smell it?"</ben>

Mary sniffed the air and thought she could.

<mary>"I smell something nice, fresh, and damp,"</mary> she said.

<ben>"That is the good, rich earth,"</ben> he answered, digging away. <ben>"It is in a good mood, getting ready to grow things. It is always glad when planting time comes. The earth gets bored in the winter when there is nothing to do. In the flower gardens out there, things are starting to stir down in the dark. The sun is warming them up. Before long, you will see little green spikes poking out of the black soil."</ben>

<mary>"What will they be?"</mary> Mary asked.

<ben>"Crocuses, snowdrops, and daffodils. Have you never seen them?"</ben>

<mary>"No. In India, everything is hot, wet, and green after the rains,"</mary> Mary said. <mary>"And I think things grow up overnight."</mary>

<ben>"These won't grow up overnight,"</ben> Weatherstaff said. <ben>"You will have to wait for them. They will poke up a little higher here, push out a new shoot there, and uncurl a leaf one day and another the next. Just watch them."</ben>

<mary>"I am going to,"</mary> Mary answered.

Soon, she heard the soft rustle of wings and knew instantly that the robin had returned. He was very lively and bold, hopping around close to her feet. He tilted his head to one side and looked at her so slyly that she decided to ask Ben Weatherstaff a question.

<mary>"Do you think he remembers me?"</mary> she asked.

<ben>"Remembers you!"</ben> Weatherstaff said indignantly. <ben>"He knows every cabbage stump in these gardens, let alone the people. He has never seen a little girl here before, and he is determined to find out all about you. There is no use trying to hide anything from him."</ben>

<mary>"Are things stirring down in the dark in the garden where he lives?"</mary> Mary asked.

<ben>"What garden?"</ben> grunted Weatherstaff, turning grumpy again.

<mary>"The one with the old rose trees."</mary> She could not help asking because she wanted to know so badly. <mary>"Are all the flowers dead, or do some of them come back in the summer? Are there ever any roses?"</mary>

<ben>"Ask him,"</ben> Ben Weatherstaff said, gesturing with his shoulders toward the robin. <ben>"He is the only one who knows. No one else has seen inside it for ten years."</ben>

Ten years was a long time, Mary thought. She had been born exactly ten years ago.

She walked away, thinking slowly. She had begun to like the garden, just as she had begun to like the robin, Dickon, Martha's mother, and even Martha herself. That seemed like a lot of people to like, especially for someone who wasn't used to liking anyone. She even counted the robin as one of those people. She went for a walk outside the long, ivy-covered wall where she could see the treetops. The second time she walked up and down, something incredibly exciting and interesting happened to her, and it was all because of Ben Weatherstaff's robin.

She heard a chirp and a twitter. Looking at the bare flowerbed to her left, she saw him hopping around, pretending to peck at the soil to convince her he hadn't followed her. But she knew he had, and the surprise filled her with so much joy that she actually trembled a little.

<mary>"You do remember me!"</mary> she cried out. <mary>"You do! You are prettier than anything else in the world!"</mary>

She chirped, talked, and coaxed him, while he hopped, flicked his tail, and twittered back. It felt exactly as if they were having a conversation. His red breast looked like bright satin, and he puffed it out, looking so grand, handsome, and proud. It was as if he were showing her just how important and human-like a robin could be. Mary completely forgot that she had ever been a cross or stubborn child. She stepped closer and closer, bending down to talk to him and trying to imitate his robin sounds.

Oh, to think that he would actually let her get that close! He knew she would never reach out to grab him or startle him in the slightest way. He knew it because he behaved like a real person—only much nicer than any human in the world. She was so happy she barely dared to breathe.

The flowerbed was not entirely bare. It had no flowers because the perennial plants had been cut back for the winter, but there were tall and short shrubs growing together at the back of the bed. As the robin hopped under them, Mary saw him hop over a small pile of freshly turned-up soil. He stopped there to look for a worm. The earth had been dug up because a dog had been trying to catch a mole, leaving a fairly deep hole in the ground.

Mary looked at the hole, not knowing why it was there, and noticed something half-buried in the fresh soil. It looked like a ring of rusty iron or brass. When the robin flew up into a tree nearby, she reached down and picked it up. She realized it was more than just a ring. It was an old key, and it looked as if it had been buried in the ground for a very long time.

Mary stood up, staring at the key hanging from her finger with a look of pure wonder and slight fear.

<mary>"Perhaps it has been buried for ten years,"</mary> she whispered. <mary>"Perhaps it is the key to the garden!"</mary>
