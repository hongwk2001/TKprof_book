Chapter 16: "I Won't!" Said Mary

They found a great deal to do that morning. Mary was late returning to the house, and she was in such a hurry to get back to her work that she completely forgot about Colin until the last minute.

"Tell Colin that I can't come to see him yet," she said to Martha. "I'm very busy in the garden."

Martha looked rather frightened.

"Oh, Miss Mary," she said, "it might put him in a terrible mood when I tell him that."

But Mary was not as afraid of him as other people were, and she was not a self-sacrificing person.

"I can't stay," she answered. "Dickon is waiting for me," and she ran away.

The afternoon was even lovelier and busier than the morning had been. Already, nearly all the weeds were cleared out of the garden, and most of the roses and trees had been pruned or dug around. Dickon had brought a spade of his own and had taught Mary how to use all her tools. By this time, it was clear that although the lovely, wild place was not likely to become a neat, formal garden, it would be a beautiful wilderness of growing things before the spring was over.

"There'll be apple blossoms and cherry blossoms overhead," Dickon said, working away with all his might. "And there'll be peach and plum trees in bloom against the walls, and the grass will be a carpet of flowers."

The little fox and the rook were just as happy and busy as they were, and the robin and his mate flew back and forth like tiny streaks of lightning. Sometimes the rook flapped his black wings and soared away over the treetops in the park. Each time he came back, he perched near Dickon and cawed several times as if he were sharing his adventures, and Dickon talked to him just as he had talked to the robin. Once, when Dickon was so busy that he did not answer right away, Soot flew onto his shoulder and gently tweaked his ear with his large beak. When Mary wanted to rest a little, Dickon sat down with her under a tree. Once, he took his pipe out of his pocket and played those soft, strange little notes, and two squirrels appeared on the wall to watch and listen.

"You're a good deal stronger than you were," Dickon said, looking at her as she dug. "You're beginning to look different, for sure."

Mary was glowing with exercise and good spirits.

"I'm gaining weight and getting healthier every day," she said excitedly. "Mrs. Medlock will have to get me some bigger dresses. Martha says my hair is growing thicker. It isn't so flat and stringy."

The sun was beginning to set, sending deep gold-colored rays slanting under the trees when they parted.

"It will be nice tomorrow," said Dickon. "I'll be at work by sunrise."

"So will I," said Mary.

She ran back to the house as fast as her feet could carry her. She wanted to tell Colin about Dickon's fox cub, the rook, and what the spring had been doing. She felt sure he would want to hear. So it was not very pleasant when she opened the door of her room and saw Martha waiting for her with a sad face.

"What's the matter?" she asked. "What did Colin say when you told him I couldn't come?"

"Oh!" Martha said. "I wish you had gone. He was close to throwing one of his tantrums. It's been a real struggle all afternoon to keep him quiet. He watched the clock the entire time."

Mary's lips tightened. She was no more used to considering other people than Colin was, and she saw no reason why an ill-tempered boy should interfere with what she liked doing best. She knew nothing about how pitiful it was for people who had been sick and nervous, and who did not realize they could control their tempers instead of making everyone else miserable too. When she had a headache in India, she had done her best to ensure that everyone else was just as miserable. She had felt she was completely in the right then, but of course, now she felt that Colin was completely in the wrong.

He was not on his sofa when she went into his room. He was lying flat on his back in bed, and he did not even turn his head toward her as she entered. This was a bad sign, and Mary marched up to him in her stiff, formal way.

"Why didn't you get up?" she asked.

"I did get up this morning when I thought you were coming," he answered without looking at her. "I made them put me back in bed this afternoon. My back and head ached, and I was tired. Why didn't you come?"

"I was working in the garden with Dickon," Mary said.

Colin frowned and condescended to look at her.

"I won't let that boy come here if you're going to stay with him instead of coming to talk to me," he said.

Mary flew into a silent rage. She could get incredibly angry without making a sound, becoming sour and stubborn, not caring what happened next.

"If you send Dickon away, I will never set foot in this room again!" she retorted.

"You'll have to if I want you to," Colin said.

"I won't!" Mary said.

"I'll make you," Colin said. "They will drag you in."

"Will they, Mr. Rajah?" Mary said fiercely. "They might drag me in, but they can't make me talk once I'm here. I'll sit and clench my teeth and never say a word to you. I won't even look at you. I'll just stare at the floor!"

They were a lovely, pleasant pair as they glared at each other. If they had been two street boys, they would have lunged at each other and had a rough-and-tumble fight. As it was, they did the next best thing.

"You are a selfish creature!" Colin cried.

"And what are you?" Mary retorted. "Selfish people always say that. You think anyone is selfish who doesn't do what you want. You're much more selfish than I am. You're the most selfish boy I've ever seen."

"I am not!" Colin snapped. "I'm not as selfish as your wonderful Dickon! He keeps you playing in the dirt when he knows I am all alone. He's the selfish one, if you ask me!"

Mary's eyes flashed with anger.

"He's nicer than any other boy who ever lived!" she said. "He's—he's like an angel!" It might sound a bit silly to say that, but she didn't care.

"A fine angel!" Colin sneered fiercely. "He's just a common cottage boy from the moor!"

"He's better than a common Rajah!" Mary retorted. "He's a thousand times better!"

Because she was the stronger of the two, she was beginning to win the argument. The truth was that he had never had a fight with anyone like himself in his life, and overall, it was actually good for him, though neither he nor Mary realized it. He turned his head on his pillow and closed his eyes, and a large tear squeezed out and ran down his cheek. He was starting to feel sorry for himself—but not for anyone else.

"I'm not as selfish as you, because I'm always sick, and I'm sure a lump is growing on my back," he said. "And besides, I am going to die."

"You are not!" Mary contradicted unsympathetically.

He opened his eyes wide with indignation. He had never heard anyone say such a thing to him before. He was both furious and strangely intrigued, if a person could be both at the same time.

"I'm not?" he cried. "I am! You know I am! Everybody says so."

"I don't believe it!" Mary said sourly. "You just say that to make people feel sorry for you. I think you're actually proud of it. I don't believe you! If you were a nice boy, it might be worth feeling sorry for you—but you're just too nasty!"

Despite his supposedly weak back, Colin sat up in bed in a perfectly healthy rage.

"Get out of the room!" he shouted, grabbing his pillow and throwing it at her. He was not strong enough to throw it far, and it only landed at her feet, but Mary's face became pinched with anger.

"I'm going," she said. "And I won't come back!"

She walked to the door, and when she reached it, she turned around and spoke again.

"I was going to tell you all kinds of wonderful things," she said. "Dickon brought his fox and his rook, and I was going to tell you all about them. Now I won't tell you a single thing!"

She marched out of the room and shut the door behind her. To her great astonishment, she found the nurse standing there as if she had been listening. Even more amazing, the nurse was laughing. She was a tall, attractive young woman who really shouldn't have been a nurse at all, since she couldn't stand sick people and was always making excuses to leave Colin with Martha or anyone else who would take over. Mary had never liked her, and she just stood there staring up at the woman, who was giggling into her handkerchief.

"What are you laughing at?" Mary asked her.

"At the two of you," the nurse said. "It's the best thing that could happen to that sickly, pampered boy—having someone stand up to him who is just as spoiled as he is." She laughed into her handkerchief again. "If he had a fiery sister to fight with, it would have been the saving of him."

"Is he going to die?"

"I don't know, and I don't care," the nurse replied. "His tantrums and bad temper are half of what is wrong with him anyway."

"What are hysterics?" Mary asked.

"You'll find out if you work him up into another tantrum—but at least you've given him something real to cry about, and I'm glad of it."

Mary went back to her room feeling very different from when she had come in from the garden. She was cross and disappointed, but she did not feel sorry for Colin at all. She had looked forward to telling him so many things, and she had meant to decide whether it was safe to trust him with their great secret. She had been starting to think she could, but now she had changed her mind completely. She would never tell him, and he could stay in his room, never get any fresh air, and die if he wanted to! It would serve him right! She felt so bitter and unforgiving that, for a few minutes, she almost forgot about Dickon, the green veil creeping over the world, and the soft wind blowing down from the moor.

Martha was waiting for her, and the worried look on her face had temporarily been replaced by interest and curiosity. There was a wooden box on the table, and its cover had been removed to reveal that it was full of neat packages.

"Mr. Craven sent this to you," Martha said. "It looks like there are picture books inside."

Mary remembered what he had asked her the day she went to his study: "Do you want anything—dolls, toys, books?" She opened the package, wondering if he had sent a doll, and wondering what she would do with it if he had. But he had not sent one. Instead, there were several beautiful books like the ones Colin had, and two of them were about gardens and full of pictures. There were also two or three games and a beautiful little writing case with a gold monogram, along with a gold pen and inkstand.

Everything was so lovely that her delight began to push the anger out of her mind. She had not expected him to remember her at all, and her cold little heart warmed up.

"I can write in cursive better than I can print," she said, "and the first thing I write with this pen will be a letter to tell him how grateful I am."

If she and Colin were still on good terms, she would have run to show him her presents right away. They could have looked at the pictures, read the gardening books, and perhaps tried playing the games. He would have enjoyed himself so much that he wouldn't have thought about dying once, or put his hand on his spine to feel for a lump. He had a habit of doing that which she could not stand. It made her feel uncomfortable and uneasy, because he always looked so terrified when he did it. He believed that if he felt even the tiniest bump one day, it would mean his back was beginning to grow crooked. Something he had overheard Mrs. Medlock whispering to the nurse had given him this idea, and he had brooded over it in secret until he was completely convinced. Mrs. Medlock had said that his father's back had started to show its crookedness in exactly that way when he was a child. Colin had never told anyone but Mary that most of his tantrums actually came from this hidden, terrifying fear. Mary had felt very sorry for him when he shared that with her.

"He always starts thinking about it when he's angry or tired," she said to herself. "And he has been very angry today. Perhaps—just perhaps—he has been worrying about it all afternoon."

She stood still, looking down at the carpet and thinking.

"I said I would never go back," she hesitated, frowning, "but maybe, just maybe, I'll go see if he wants me in the morning. He might try to throw his pillow at me again, but... I think I'll go."
