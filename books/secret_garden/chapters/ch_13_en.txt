Chapter 13: "I Am Colin"

Mary took the picture back to the house when she went to her supper, and she showed it to Martha.

"Oh!" Martha said with great pride. "I never knew our Dickon was as clever as that. That's a picture of a missel thrush on her nest, as large as life and twice as natural."

Then Mary realized that Dickon had meant the picture to be a message. He wanted her to be sure that he would keep her secret. Her garden was her nest, and she was like the missel thrush. Oh, how she liked that strange, unusual boy!

She hoped he would come back the very next day, and she fell asleep looking forward to the morning.

But you never know what the weather will do in Yorkshire, especially in the springtime. She woke up in the middle of the night to the sound of rain beating heavily against her window. It was pouring in torrents, and the wind was howling around the corners and through the chimneys of the huge old house. Mary sat up in bed, feeling miserable and angry.

"The rain is just as stubborn as I ever was," she said. "It only came because it knew I didn't want it."

She threw herself back on her pillow and buried her face. She did not cry, but she lay there hating the sound of the heavy, beating rain. She hated the wind and its mournful howling. She couldn't get back to sleep. The sad sound kept her awake because she felt so sad herself. If she had been happy, it probably would have lulled her to sleep. How the wind howled, and how the big raindrops poured down and beat against the glass!

"It sounds just like someone lost on the moor, wandering on and on and crying," she said.

She had been lying awake, tossing and turning for about an hour, when suddenly something made her sit up in bed and turn her head toward the door to listen. She listened intently.

"It isn't the wind now," she whispered loudly. "That isn't the wind. It is different. It's the same crying I heard before."

The door to her room was slightly open, and the sound came down the corridor—a distant, faint sound of fretful crying. She listened for a few minutes, and with each passing second, she became more certain. She felt as if she had to find out what it was. It seemed even stranger than the secret garden and the buried key. Perhaps her rebellious mood made her bold. She put her feet out of bed and stood on the floor.

"I am going to find out what it is," she said. "Everyone is in bed, and I don't care about Mrs. Medlock—I don't care at all!"

There was a candle by her bedside, so she picked it up and went quietly out of the room. The corridor looked very long and dark, but she was too excited to mind. She thought she remembered which corners to turn to find the short corridor with the tapestry-covered door—the one Mrs. Medlock had come through the day Mary got lost. The sound had come from that direction. She walked on with her dim light, almost feeling her way, her heart beating so loudly that she imagined she could hear it. The distant, faint crying continued and guided her. Sometimes it stopped for a moment or two and then started again. Was this the right corner to turn? She stopped and thought. Yes, it was. Down this hallway, then to the left, up two broad steps, and then to the right again. Yes, there was the tapestry door.

She pushed it open very gently and closed it behind her. Standing in the corridor, she could hear the crying quite clearly, though it was not loud. It came from the other side of the wall to her left, and a few yards farther down was a door. She could see a glimmer of light coming from underneath it. Someone was crying in that room, and it was a very young person.

So she walked to the door and pushed it open, and suddenly she was standing inside the room!

It was a large room with antique, elegant furniture. A low fire glowed faintly in the fireplace, and a nightlight burned beside a carved four-poster bed draped in brocade. Lying on the bed was a boy, crying fretfully.

Mary wondered if she was in a real place, or if she had fallen asleep again and was dreaming without realizing it.

The boy had a sharp, delicate face the color of ivory, with eyes that seemed much too large for it. He also had a lot of hair that tumbled over his forehead in thick locks, making his thin face look even smaller. He looked like a boy who had been sick for a long time, but he was crying more out of weariness and irritability than pain.

Mary stood near the door with her candle in her hand, holding her breath. Then she crept across the room. As she drew closer, the light caught the boy's attention. He turned his head on his pillow and stared at her, his gray eyes opening so wide that they looked huge.

"Who are you?" he said at last in a half-frightened whisper. "Are you a ghost?"

"No, I'm not," Mary answered, her own whisper sounding just as frightened. "Are you one?"

He stared and stared. Mary couldn't help noticing what strange eyes he had. They were agate gray, and they looked too big for his face because they were framed by thick black lashes.

"No," he replied after a moment. "I am Colin."

"Who is Colin?" she asked hesitantly.

"I am Colin Craven. Who are you?"

"I am Mary Lennox. Mr. Craven is my uncle."

"He is my father," said the boy.

"Your father!" Mary gasped. "No one ever told me he had a son! Why didn't they?"

"Come here," he said, keeping his strange eyes fixed on her with an anxious look.

She came close to the bed, and he reached out his hand to touch her.

"You are real, aren't you?" he said. "I have such realistic dreams so often. You might just be one of them."

Mary had slipped on a woolen robe before leaving her room, and she placed a fold of it between his fingers.

"Feel that and see how thick and warm it is," she said. "I'll pinch you a little if you want, to show you how real I am. For a minute, I thought you might be a dream, too."

"Where did you come from?" he asked.

"From my room. The wind was howling so loudly that I couldn't sleep. Then I heard someone crying and wanted to find out who it was. Why were you crying?"

"Because I couldn't sleep either, and my head ached. Tell me your name again."

"Mary Lennox. Did no one ever tell you that I had come to live here?"

He was still feeling the fabric of her robe, but he began to look a little more convinced that she was real.

"No," he answered. "They didn't dare."

"Why?" Mary asked.

"Because I would have been afraid of you seeing me. I won't let people look at me and gossip about me."

"Why?" Mary asked again, feeling more puzzled by the minute.

"Because I'm always like this, sick and forced to lie in bed. My father won't let people talk about me either. The servants are forbidden to speak of me. If I live, I might become a hunchback, but I won't live. My father hates to think that I might grow up to be like him."

"Oh, what a strange house this is!" Mary said. "What a strange house! Everything is some kind of secret. Rooms are locked up, gardens are locked up—and you! Have you been locked up, too?"

"No. I stay in this room because I don't want to be moved out of it. It exhausts me too much."

"Does your father ever come to see you?" Mary ventured.

"Sometimes. Usually when I'm asleep. He doesn't want to look at me."

"Why?" Mary couldn't help asking again.

An angry shadow passed over the boy's face.

"My mother died when I was born, and it makes him miserable to look at me. He thinks I don't know, but I've heard the servants talking. He almost hates me."

"He hates the garden because she died," Mary said, almost to herself.

"What garden?" the boy asked.

"Oh, just... just a garden she used to like," Mary stammered. "Have you always lived here?"

"Nearly always. Sometimes I've been taken to the seaside, but I won't stay there because people stare at me. I used to wear a metal brace to keep my back straight, but a famous doctor came from London to see me and said it was stupid. He told them to take it off and keep me out in the fresh air. But I hate fresh air, and I don't want to go outside."

"I didn't either when I first came here," Mary said. "Why do you keep staring at me like that?"

"Because of my dreams that seem so real," he answered rather fretfully. "Sometimes when I open my eyes, I can't believe I'm awake."

"We are both awake," Mary said. She looked around the room, with its high ceiling, shadowy corners, and dim firelight. "It does look a lot like a dream, and it's the middle of the night, and everyone else in the house is asleep—everyone but us. We are wide awake."

"I don't want it to be a dream," the boy said restlessly.

Suddenly, Mary had an idea.

"If you don't like people seeing you," she began, "do you want me to leave?"

He still held the fold of her robe, and he gave it a gentle pull.

"No," he said. "If you left, I would be sure you were just a dream. If you are real, sit down on that big footstool and talk to me. I want to hear about you."

Mary set her candle down on the table near the bed and sat on the cushioned stool. She didn't want to leave at all. She wanted to stay in this mysterious, hidden room and talk to this mysterious boy.

"What do you want me to tell you?" she asked.

He wanted to know how long she had been at Misselthwaite, which corridor her room was on, and what she had been doing. He asked if she disliked the moor as much as he did, and where she had lived before coming to Yorkshire. She answered all his questions and many more, while he lay back on his pillow and listened. He had her tell him all about India and her voyage across the ocean. She discovered that because he was always sick, he hadn't learned things the way other children did. One of his nurses had taught him to read when he was very young, and he spent his time reading and looking at pictures in beautiful books.

Even though his father rarely saw him when he was awake, he was given all kinds of wonderful things to keep himself amused. Yet he never seemed to enjoy any of them. He could have anything he asked for and was never forced to do anything he didn't want to do.

"Everyone has to do whatever pleases me," he said indifferently. "It makes me sick to get angry. And nobody believes I'll live to grow up."

He said it as if he was so used to the idea that it didn't even matter to him anymore. He seemed to like the sound of Mary's voice. As she kept talking, he listened in a sleepy but interested way. Once or twice, she wondered if he was drifting off to sleep. But at last, he asked a question that opened up a whole new topic.

"How old are you?" he asked.

"I am ten," Mary answered, forgetting herself for a moment, "and so are you."

"How do you know that?" he demanded, surprised.

"Because when you were born, the garden door was locked and the key was buried. It has been locked for ten years."

Colin sat halfway up, turning toward her and leaning on his elbows.

"What garden door was locked? Who did it? Where was the key buried?" he exclaimed, suddenly very interested.

"It... it was the garden Mr. Craven hates," Mary said nervously. "He locked the door. Nobody... nobody knew where he buried the key."

"What kind of garden is it?" Colin asked eagerly.

"Nobody has been allowed to go inside for ten years," Mary replied carefully.

But it was too late to be cautious. He was too much like her. He also had nothing to occupy his mind, and the idea of a hidden garden fascinated him just as it had fascinated her. He asked question after question. Where was it? Had she ever looked for the door? Had she ever asked the gardeners?

"They won't talk about it," Mary said. "I think they have been told not to answer any questions."

"I would make them," Colin said.

"Could you?" Mary asked hesitantly, beginning to feel frightened. If he could make people answer questions, who knew what might happen?

"Everyone has to please me. I told you that," he said. "If I live, this place will belong to me someday. They all know that. I would make them tell me."

Mary hadn't realized that she herself had been spoiled, but she could see very clearly that this mysterious boy was. He acted as if the whole world belonged to him. How strange he was, and how calmly he spoke of dying.

"Do you really think you won't live?" she asked, partly out of curiosity and partly hoping to make him forget about the garden.

"I don't suppose I will," he answered as indifferently as before. "For as long as I can remember, I've heard people say I won't live. At first, they thought I was too young to understand, and now they think I don't hear them. But I do. My doctor is my father's cousin. He is quite poor, and if I die, he will inherit all of Misselthwaite when my father dies. I don't think he wants me to live."

"Do you want to live?" Mary asked.

"No," he answered in a cross, tired voice. "But I don't want to die, either. When I feel sick, I lie here and think about it until I cry and cry."

"I've heard you crying three times," Mary said, "but I didn't know who it was. Were you crying about that?" She really wanted him to forget about the garden.

"Probably," he answered. "Let's talk about something else. Tell me about that garden. Don't you want to see it?"

"Yes," Mary answered in a very low voice.

"I do," he went on persistently. "I don't think I've ever really wanted to see anything before, but I want to see that garden. I want the key dug up, and the door unlocked. I would let them take me there in my wheelchair. That would be a way to get fresh air. I'm going to make them open the door."

He had become quite excited, and his strange eyes began to shine like stars, looking larger than ever.

"They have to do what I want," he said. "I will make them take me there, and I'll let you come, too."

Mary clenched her hands together. Everything would be ruined—everything! Dickon would never come back. She would never again feel like a missel thrush with a safe, hidden nest.

"Oh, don't—don't—don't—don't do that!" she cried out.

He stared at her as if he thought she had gone crazy.

"Why?" he asked. "You said you wanted to see it."

"I do," she answered, with a sob catching in her throat, "but if you make them open the door and carry you in like that, it will never be a secret again."

He leaned even farther forward.

"A secret," he said. "What do you mean? Tell me."

Mary's words rushed out, tumbling over one another.

"You see—you see," she panted, "if nobody knows but us—if there was a door, hidden somewhere under the ivy, and we could find it. And if we could slip through it together and shut it behind us, and nobody knew anyone was inside. We could call it our garden and pretend that we were missel thrushes and it was our nest. We could play there almost every day, digging and planting seeds and making it all come alive..."

"Is it dead?" he interrupted.

"It soon will be if nobody cares for it," she went on. "The bulbs will live, but the roses—"

He stopped her again, just as excited as she was.

"What are bulbs?" he asked quickly.

"They are daffodils and lilies and snowdrops. They are working in the earth right now, pushing up pale green shoots because spring is coming."

"Is spring coming?" he said. "What is it like? You don't see it in these rooms when you are sick."

"It is the sun shining on the rain, and the rain falling in the sunshine, and things pushing up and working under the earth," Mary said. "If the garden were a secret and we could get into it, we could watch everything grow bigger day by day and see how many roses are alive. Don't you see? Oh, don't you see how much better it would be if it was a secret?"

He fell back onto his pillow and lay there with a strange expression on his face.

"I've never had a secret," he said, "except the one about not living to grow up. They don't know I know, so it's a sort of secret. But I like this kind much better."

"If you promise not to make them take you to the garden," Mary pleaded, "I feel almost sure I can find a way to get in eventually. And then, if the doctor wants you to go out in your chair, and since you can do whatever you want, maybe we could find a boy to push you. We could go alone, and it would always be a secret garden."

"I would... like... that," he said very slowly, his eyes looking dreamy. "I would like that. I wouldn't mind fresh air if it was in a secret garden."

Mary began to catch her breath and feel safer, seeing that the idea of keeping the secret pleased him. She felt sure that if she kept talking and could help him picture the garden in his mind as she had seen it, he would love it too much to let everyone tramp into it whenever they pleased.

"I'll tell you what I think it looks like inside," she said. "Since it's been shut up so long, everything has probably grown into a wild tangle."

He lay completely still and listened as she described the roses that might have climbed from tree to tree, hanging down in loops, and the birds that must have built their nests there because it was so safe. Then she told him about the robin and Ben Weatherstaff. There was so much to say about the robin, and it felt so safe to talk about him, that she stopped feeling afraid. The story of the robin pleased Colin so much that he smiled until he looked almost handsome, though at first Mary had thought he was even plainer than she was, with his giant eyes and heavy hair.

"I didn't know birds could be like that," he said. "But if you stay cooped up in a room, you never see anything. You know so many things. I feel as if you've already been inside that garden."

She didn't know what to say, so she kept quiet. He clearly didn't expect an answer, and a moment later, he surprised her.

"I'm going to let you look at something," he said. "Do you see that rose-colored silk curtain hanging on the wall above the fireplace?"

Mary hadn't noticed it before, but she looked up and saw it. It was a curtain of soft silk covering what seemed to be a picture.

"Yes," she answered.

"There's a cord hanging from it," Colin said. "Go and pull it."

Mary stood up, very curious, and found the cord. When she pulled it, the silk curtain slid back on its rings, revealing a painting. It was a portrait of a girl with a laughing face. She had bright hair tied up with a blue ribbon, and her bright, lovely eyes were exactly like Colin's unhappy ones—agate gray and looking twice their actual size because of the dark lashes surrounding them.

"She is my mother," Colin said in a complaining tone. "I don't understand why she died. Sometimes I hate her for doing it."

"How strange!" Mary said.

"If she had lived, I don't think I would have been sick all the time," he grumbled. "I probably would have really lived. And my father wouldn't have hated to look at me. I'd probably have a strong back. Pull the curtain back over it."

Mary did as she was told and went back to her footstool.

"She is much prettier than you," Mary said, "but her eyes are just like yours—at least they are the same shape and color. Why is the curtain drawn over her portrait?"

He shifted uncomfortably.

"I made them cover it," he said. "Sometimes I don't like her looking at me. She smiles too much when I am sick and miserable. Besides, she is mine, and I don't want everyone to look at her."

There were a few moments of silence, and then Mary spoke.

"What would Mrs. Medlock do if she found out I was here?" she asked.

"She would do whatever I told her to do," he answered. "And I would tell her that I want you to come here and talk to me every day. I'm glad you came."

"So am I," Mary said. "I'll come as often as I can, but..." She hesitated. "...I still have to look for the garden door every day."

"Yes, you must," Colin agreed, "and you can tell me all about it afterward."

He lay thinking for a few minutes, just as he had done before, and then spoke again.

"I think you should be a secret, too," he said. "I won't tell them about you until they find out on their own. I can always send the nurse out of the room and say that I want to be left alone. Do you know Martha?"

"Yes, I know her very well," Mary said. "She takes care of me."

He nodded toward the outer corridor.

"She is the one sleeping in the other room. The nurse went away yesterday to stay overnight with her sister, and she always makes Martha look after me when she wants to go out. Martha can tell you when to come here."

Then Mary finally understood Martha's worried look whenever she had asked questions about the crying.

"So Martha knew about you all along?" she said.

"Yes, she often looks after me. The nurse likes to get away, and that's when Martha comes."

"I've been here a long time," Mary said. "Should I leave now? Your eyes look very sleepy."

"I wish I could fall asleep before you go," he said rather shyly.

"Shut your eyes," Mary said, pulling her footstool closer. "I'll do what my Ayah used to do in India. I'll pat and stroke your hand and sing something very low."

"I think I would like that," he said drowsily.

She felt sorry for him and didn't want him to lie there awake, so she leaned against the bed and began to stroke and pat his hand, singing a soft, low chant in Hindustani.

"That's nice," he murmured even more sleepily. She went on singing and stroking, and when she looked at him again, his dark lashes were resting against his cheeks. His eyes were closed, and he was fast asleep. She stood up quietly, picked up her candle, and crept out of the room without making a sound.
