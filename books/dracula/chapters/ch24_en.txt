CHAPTER XXIV

DR. SEWARD'S PHONOGRAPH DIARY, DICTATED BY VAN HELSING

This is a message for Jonathan Harker.

You must stay by your beloved Madam Mina's side. We are going out to conduct our search—if it can even be called a search. In truth, it is not a search but merely a confirmation of what we already know. Your most important and sacred duty is to remain behind and care for her today. The enemy will not be able to find her here today. Let me explain so that you will understand what the four of us already know. Our enemy has left; he has returned to his castle in Transylvania. I know this as clearly as if a giant hand of fire had written it on the wall. He had prepared for this eventuality, and that final box of native soil—which he needs to maintain his power—was ready to be shipped somewhere. This is why he gathered his money and why he rushed at the last minute, fearing we might catch him before sunset. His last hope was dashed when he could not hide in the tomb of poor Miss Lucy. With no time left and that plan ruined, he went straight for his final resource—his last earth-work, to use a double meaning. He is truly clever! He knew his game in London was over, so he decided to return home. He found a ship traveling the same route he took to get here, and he boarded it. We are now heading out to discover which ship it is and where it is bound. Once we find out, we will return and tell you everything. Then we can offer you and poor, dear Madam Mina the comfort of new hope. When you think about it, you will realize this is indeed hopeful: all is not lost. The monster we are pursuing took hundreds of years to reach London, yet we drove him out in a single day once we figured out how to deal with him. He may be powerful and immune to human suffering, but he is still finite. Meanwhile, we are strong in our shared purpose, and even stronger when we stand together. Take heart, dear husband of Madam Mina. This battle has only just begun, and we will ultimately win—as surely as God watches over His children from above. Therefore, please find great comfort until we return.

VAN HELSING.

Jonathan Harker's Journal.

October 4.—When I played Van Helsing's phonograph message for Mina, my poor wife's face brightened noticeably. The mere certainty that the Count has left the country has given her great comfort, and that comfort gives her strength. As for myself, now that the horrific threat is no longer right in front of us, it feels almost impossible to believe it ever happened. Even my own terrifying experiences in Castle Dracula feel like a long-forgotten dream, especially here in the crisp autumn air and bright sunlight.

Alas, how can I possibly deny it! In the midst of my thoughts, my eyes fell upon the red scar on my poor darling's white forehead—the burn mark left from her contact with the vampire. As long as that mark remains, there can be no denial. And in the future, the memory of it alone will keep our conviction crystal clear. Mina and I are too anxious to sit idle, so we have been reading through all the diaries over and over. Strangely, although the reality of the events feels stronger with each reading, the pain and fear seem to lessen. There appears to be a clear guiding purpose running through everything, which is comforting. Mina suggests that perhaps we are instruments of a greater good. That may be true! I will try to adopt her perspective. We have not yet spoken to each other about the future. It is best to wait until the Professor and the others return from their investigation.

The day is passing more quickly than I ever thought a day could again. It is now three o'clock.

Mina Harker's Journal.

October 5, 5 p.m.—Our briefing meeting. Present: Professor Van Helsing, Lord Godalming, Dr. Seward, Mr. Quincey Morris, Jonathan Harker, and Mina Harker.

Dr. Van Helsing detailed the steps taken during the day to discover which ship Count Dracula had used for his escape and where it was headed:—

"Since I knew he wanted to return to Transylvania, I was certain he would travel via the mouth of the Danube or somewhere along the Black Sea, as that was the route he took to get here. We were faced with a daunting blank slate. True to the Latin proverb 'Omne ignotum pro magnifico' (the unknown always appears greater), we set out with heavy hearts to find out what ships had left for the Black Sea last night. Madam Mina had mentioned seeing sails being raised, so he must have been on a sailing vessel. Such ships are too minor to be listed in the shipping logs of the Times, so at Lord Godalming's suggestion, we went to Lloyd's of London, where records of all departing ships, no matter how small, are kept. There, we discovered that only one ship bound for the Black Sea had left with the tide. It was the Czarina Catherine, which sailed from Doolittle's Wharf for Varna, intending to continue up the Danube to other ports. 'Aha!' I said. 'This is the ship the Count is on.' We hurried down to Doolittle's Wharf and found a man sitting in a wooden office so small he seemed larger than the building itself. We asked him about the Czarina Catherine's departure. He was a red-faced, loud-voiced fellow who swore constantly, but he was a decent sort. When Quincey pulled some rustling banknotes from his pocket and slipped them into a hidden pouch deep inside the man's clothing, he became even more agreeable and eager to help. He took us to question several rough, hot-tempered men along the docks, who also became much friendlier after we bought them a drink. They used a lot of slang and curses involving 'blood' and 'bloom' which I didn't entirely understand, but I grasped their meaning, and they gave us all the information we needed.

"They told us that around five o'clock yesterday afternoon, a man arrived in a great hurry. He was tall, thin, and pale, with a prominent nose, exceptionally white teeth, and eyes that seemed to burn. He was dressed entirely in black, except for a straw hat that suited neither him nor the hour. He was throwing money around as he urgently asked about ships sailing for the Black Sea. Someone escorted him to the office and then to the ship, but he refused to go aboard. Instead, he stood at the end of the gangplank and demanded to see the captain. The captain came down after being promised good pay; despite cursing at first, he agreed to the terms. The thin man then left after someone told him where to hire a horse and cart. He returned shortly after, driving a cart loaded with a massive box. He lifted the box down all by himself, even though it later took several men to hoist it onto a hand-truck for the ship. He gave the captain lengthy instructions on exactly how and where his box should be stored. The captain didn't like this and swore at him in several languages, telling him to come aboard and see the spot for himself if he was so concerned. But the man refused, saying he had too much else to do. The captain retorted—with plenty of curses—that he had better hurry back, because the ship was leaving before the tide turned. The thin man simply smiled and said he would board when he saw fit, but added he would be surprised if the ship left quite so soon. The captain swore again in multiple languages, and the thin man gave a polite bow, thanked him, and said he would intrude upon his kindness by boarding just before departure. Finally, the captain grew redder than ever and yelled—in more languages, heavily laced with curses—that he didn't want any foreigners on his ship. The thin man then asked where he might purchase shipping documents nearby, and departed.

"The dockworkers said no one knew where he went 'or bloomin' well cared,' because they soon had other things to worry about. It quickly became obvious that the Czarina Catherine wouldn't be sailing on schedule. A thin mist began to rise from the river, growing steadily until a dense fog completely swallowed the ship and the surrounding area. The captain swore furiously in his usual polyglot manner, but there was nothing he could do. The water level kept rising, and he began to fear they would miss the tide entirely. He was in a foul mood when, right at high tide, the thin man walked up the gangplank and asked to see where his box had been stored. The captain angrily told him that he and his old box could go to hell. The thin man wasn't offended; he went below deck with the mate to inspect the placement, then returned and stood quietly on the foggy deck for a while. He must have disembarked on his own, as no one saw him leave. In truth, no one was paying attention to him, because the fog soon began to dissipate and the air cleared. My drinking companions laughed as they recounted how the captain's swearing reached new, wildly creative heights when he asked other sailors on the river and discovered that almost no one else had seen any fog, except right around their wharf. Regardless, the ship departed on the ebb tide and was surely far down the river estuary by morning. By the time they told us all this, the ship was well out to sea.

"And so, my dear Madam Mina, we must rest for a while, as our enemy is out at sea, controlling the fog as he heads toward the Danube. Sailing takes time, no matter how fast a ship goes. If we travel over land, we will arrive much sooner and can intercept him there. Our best hope is to catch him in his box between sunrise and sunset; during those hours, he will be powerless to fight back, and we can deal with him properly. We have a few days to prepare our strategy. We know his exact destination because we met the ship's owner, who showed us the invoices and all the relevant paperwork. The box we are hunting is scheduled to be unloaded in Varna and handed over to an agent named Ristics, who will present his credentials. The merchant offered to send a telegram to Varna and have the authorities investigate if there was any foul play, but we declined. What we must do is not a matter for the police or customs officials. It must be handled by us alone, in our own way."

When Dr. Van Helsing finished speaking, I asked him if he was certain the Count was still aboard the ship. He replied, "We have the best possible proof: your own testimony, given under hypnotic trance this morning." I asked him again if it was truly necessary for them to hunt the Count down. The thought of Jonathan leaving me fills me with dread, and I know that if the others go, he will insist on going with them. The Professor answered, his voice growing in passion. He started quietly, but became more forceful and resolute as he spoke, until we could clearly see the commanding presence that had made him a leader among men for so long:—

"Yes, it is necessary—absolutely necessary! First and foremost for your sake, and secondly for the sake of all humanity. This monster has already caused tremendous harm, even while confined to a narrow space and struggling blindly in the dark to test his limited powers. I have already explained this to the others, and you, my dear Madam Mina, will hear it on my friend John's phonograph records, or your husband's. I explained to them how it took him centuries to plan his departure from his barren homeland—a land empty of people—to come to a new country where human life is as abundant as a crowded field of corn. If another vampire tried to replicate what he has done, perhaps all the centuries of history, past and future, would not be enough to help him succeed. But in his case, all the deep, strong, and mysterious forces of nature must have aligned in some miraculous way. The very region where he has existed as the Un-Dead for centuries is full of geological and chemical anomalies. There are bottomless caverns and fissures. There were once volcanoes, and some of their vents still emit waters with strange properties, and gases that can either kill or revive. Undoubtedly, some magnetic or electrical element in these mystical forces somehow sustains physical life in a bizarre way. Furthermore, he possessed extraordinary traits from the very beginning. During a brutal and warlike era, he was famous for having an iron will, a sharper intellect, and a braver heart than any other man. In him, some vital life force reached its absolute peak, and as his physical body grew strong and thrived, so did his brain. And all this happened without the diabolical aid he now possesses—because dark magic must ultimately yield to the forces of good. But this is the threat he now poses to us. He has infected you—please forgive me for saying this, my dear, but I speak only for your own good. He has infected you so cleverly that even if he does nothing else, you need only continue living your normal, sweet life. Eventually, when death claims you—as it does all mortals under God's law—you will become a creature just like him. We cannot allow this to happen! We have sworn to prevent it. We are the agents of God's will: He does not want this world, and the people His Son died to save, to be overrun by monsters whose very existence mocks Him. He has already permitted us to save one soul, and we ride out like the Crusaders of old to save more. Like them, we will journey toward the sunrise, and if we fall, we fall for a righteous cause." He paused, and I asked:—

"But won't the Count learn from this defeat? Now that he has been driven out of England, won't he simply avoid it in the future, just as a hunted tiger avoids the village that drove it away?"

"Aha!" he said. "Your tiger metaphor is excellent, and I will borrow it. When a man-eating tiger, as they are called in India, gets a taste for human blood, it ignores all other prey and prowls endlessly until it catches a person. This creature we have chased from our village is also a tiger, a man-eater, and he will never stop prowling. Moreover, it is simply not in his nature to retreat and stay away. During his mortal life, he crossed the Turkish border to attack his enemies on their own soil. He was driven back, but did he give up? No! He attacked again, and again, and again. Look at his incredible persistence and endurance. Even with his underdeveloped, child-like brain, he conceived the idea of infiltrating a great city long ago. And what did he do? He identified the most promising location in the world for his needs. Then, he patiently and methodically prepared for the task. He took his time to test his strength and understand his abilities. He studied new languages. He learned about modern society—the politics, the laws, the finance, the science, and the customs of a new country and a people that didn't exist when he was alive. The glimpse he got of our world only whetted his appetite and sharpened his ambition. In fact, it helped his brain evolve, because it proved that all his initial theories were correct. And he accomplished all this entirely on his own, from a ruined tomb in a forgotten land! Imagine what else he might do once the broader world of modern knowledge is open to him. He can laugh in the face of death, and he thrives amidst diseases that wipe out entire populations. Oh, if only such a being had come from God rather than the Devil, what a tremendous force for good he could have been in our world. But we are sworn to rid the world of him. Our work must be done in silence and complete secrecy. In this enlightened age, when people refuse to believe even what is right in front of their eyes, the skepticism of rational men would become his greatest weapon. Their doubt would serve as his shield, his armor, and his sword against us—his enemies, who are willing to risk our very souls to save the woman we love, for the good of humanity, and for the glory of God."

After a group discussion, we decided not to finalize any plans tonight. We agreed to sleep on the facts and try to arrive at the proper conclusions independently. Tomorrow at breakfast, we will meet again, share our thoughts, and agree on a concrete course of action.

* * * * *

I feel a wonderful sense of peace and rest tonight. It feels as though some haunting presence has been lifted from me. Perhaps...

My hopeful thought was left unfinished, and could never be finished, because I caught sight of the red mark on my forehead in the mirror. I instantly knew that I was still unclean.

Dr. Seward's Diary.

October 5.—We all woke up early, and I believe the sleep did us all a great deal of good. When we met for an early breakfast, the mood was generally more cheerful than any of us had ever expected to feel again.

It is truly remarkable how resilient human nature can be. The moment any obstacle or source of distress is removed, regardless of how it happens—even if through death—we immediately bounce back to our baseline state of hope and joy. While sitting around the table, I widened my eyes in wonder on more than one occasion, questioning whether the events of the past few days had all just been a bad dream. It was only when I glanced at the red blotch on Mrs. Harker's forehead that I was snapped back to reality. Even now, as I carefully think through our situation, it is almost impossible to fully grasp that the source of all our torment is still out there. Even Mrs. Harker seems to forget her troubles for long stretches; she only thinks about her terrible scar when something triggers the memory. We are scheduled to meet in my study in half an hour to finalize our plan of action. I sense only one immediate problem, and it is based more on instinct than reason: we will all need to speak entirely frankly, yet I fear that poor Mrs. Harker's tongue has somehow been mysteriously tied. I know she is drawing her own conclusions, and given everything that has happened, I can guess how brilliant and accurate those conclusions must be. However, she either will not or cannot voice them. I mentioned this concern to Van Helsing, and we plan to discuss it privately. I assume it is the effect of the horrific poison that has entered her veins and is beginning to work. The Count definitely had an ulterior motive when he forced her to endure what Van Helsing calls "the Vampire's baptism of blood." Well, perhaps a poison can be distilled even from good things. In an era where the existence of toxic ptomaines in decaying matter is still a mystery, we shouldn't be surprised by anything! But I do know this: if my instinct regarding poor Mrs. Harker's silence is correct, then a terrible complication—an unknown danger—lies ahead of us. The very same power that forces her to stay silent might also force her to speak. I dare not follow that line of thought any further, because doing so feels like I am mentally dishonoring a noble woman!

Van Helsing is coming to my study shortly before the others arrive. I will try to bring up the subject with him then.

* * * * *

Later.—When the Professor arrived, we discussed the current state of affairs. I could tell something was weighing on his mind that he wanted to say, but he seemed hesitant to bring it up. After beating around the bush for a moment, he said abruptly:—

"Friend John, there is something you and I must discuss privately, at least for now. Later on, we may need to bring the others into our confidence." He paused, so I waited patiently. He then continued:—

"Madam Mina, our poor, dear Madam Mina is changing." A cold shiver ran through my body as my worst fears were confirmed. Van Helsing went on:—

"Given our tragic experience with Miss Lucy, we must heed the warning signs this time before things go too far. Our mission is now far more difficult than ever, and this new complication makes every single hour critically important. I can already see the traits of the vampire emerging in her face. It is very, very slight at the moment, but it is visible if you look closely and without prejudice. Her teeth are slightly sharper, and at times her eyes grow harder. But that is not all; she also falls into frequent silences now, just as Miss Lucy did. Miss Lucy wouldn't speak, even when she wrote down things she wanted us to know later. Here is my fear. If we can put her in a hypnotic trance to see and hear what the Count is doing, isn't it highly likely that the Count—who hypnotized her first, drank her blood, and forced her to drink his—could compel her mind to reveal whatever she knows to him?" I nodded in agreement, and he continued:—

"Therefore, we must prevent this from happening. We must keep her completely ignorant of our plans, so she cannot reveal what she does not know. It is a terribly painful task! Oh, it breaks my heart to even think about it, but it must be done. When we meet today, I must tell her that, for reasons we cannot disclose, she can no longer be part of our council and must simply remain under our protection." He wiped his forehead, which had broken out in a heavy sweat at the thought of the pain he was about to inflict on a poor soul who had already suffered so much. I knew it would bring him some comfort if I admitted that I had reached the exact same conclusion; at the very least, it would spare him the agony of doubting his own judgment. I told him so, and it had exactly the calming effect I anticipated.

It is now nearly time for our general meeting. Van Helsing has stepped away to prepare himself for the gathering and the painful duty he must perform. I genuinely believe he left so he could find a moment to pray alone.

* * * * *

Later.—At the very beginning of our meeting, both Van Helsing and I experienced a huge sense of personal relief. Mrs. Harker had sent a message via her husband, stating that she would not be joining us for the time being. She felt it would be better for us to freely discuss our strategy without her presence complicating matters. The Professor and I glanced at each other for a moment, and we both seemed visibly relieved. I personally felt that if Mrs. Harker had recognized the danger herself, it spared us a great deal of emotional pain and averted significant risk. Under the circumstances, we agreed with a questioning look, a nod, and a finger to the lips to keep our suspicions to ourselves until we could speak privately again. We immediately began outlining our Plan of Campaign. Van Helsing summarized the key facts first:—

"The Czarina Catherine departed the Thames yesterday morning. Even traveling at her absolute fastest speed, it will take her at least three weeks to reach Varna. We, on the other hand, can travel there overland in just three days. Now, if we subtract two days from the ship's voyage to account for any favorable weather the Count might summon, and add a full day and night for any delays we might encounter on our end, we still have a buffer of nearly two weeks. Therefore, to be completely safe, we must leave here no later than the 17th. This ensures we will arrive in Varna at least a day before the ship docks, giving us time to make all necessary preparations. Naturally, we will all go armed—armed against both supernatural and physical threats." Quincey Morris chimed in here:—

"Since the Count comes from a country full of wolves, there's a chance he might have allies waiting there before we arrive. I suggest we add Winchester rifles to our arsenal. I'm a firm believer in a Winchester whenever that kind of trouble is around. Do you remember when the wolf pack chased us at Tobolsk, Art? What we wouldn't have given to have a repeating rifle each back then!"

"Excellent!" said Van Helsing. "Winchesters it is. Quincey is level-headed at all times, but especially when there is hunting to be done. It's better to insult science with metaphors than to underestimate the danger wolves pose to men. In the meantime, there is nothing more we can accomplish here. And since none of us are familiar with Varna, why not go sooner rather than later? Waiting there is the same as waiting here. We can get our things ready tonight and tomorrow, and then, if everything goes well, the four of us can set off on our journey."

"The four of us?" Harker asked questioningly, looking from one face to the next.

"Of course!" the Professor answered quickly. "You must stay behind to take care of your lovely wife!" Harker was silent for a moment before replying in a hollow voice:—

"Let's discuss that part of the plan in the morning. I need to consult with Mina." I thought this was the perfect moment for Van Helsing to warn him not to share our plans with her, but he ignored the opportunity. I looked at him pointedly and cleared my throat. In response, he simply pressed his finger to his lips and turned away.

Jonathan Harker's Journal.

October 5, afternoon.—For quite a while after our meeting this morning, I couldn't process anything. The new turn of events has left my mind in a state of bewilderment, leaving no room for active reasoning. Mina's sudden decision not to participate in the discussion made me think heavily; and since I couldn't argue with her about it, I could only guess at her reasons. I am no closer to understanding it now. The way the others simply accepted her absence also puzzled me; the last time we discussed this topic, we all agreed there would be no more secrets among us. Mina is sleeping right now, looking as calm and sweet as a little child. Her lips are curved in a slight smile, and her face beams with genuine happiness. Thank God she can still experience moments like this.

* * * * *

Later.—How strange everything is. I sat there watching Mina sleep happily, and I felt as close to true happiness as I imagine I will ever get. As evening approached and the setting sun cast long shadows over the earth, the silence in the room grew increasingly solemn to me. Suddenly, Mina opened her eyes, looked at me tenderly, and said:—

"Jonathan, I want you to promise me something on your word of honor. A promise made to me, but sworn sacredly before God, and one that must not be broken even if I fall to my knees and beg you with bitter tears. Quickly, you must promise me right now."

"Mina," I replied, "I cannot make a promise like that on the spot. I may not even have the right to make it."

"But, my love," she pleaded, with such intense spiritual fervor that her eyes shone like the North Star. "I am the one asking for it, and it isn't for my own sake. You can ask Dr. Van Helsing if I am right. If he disagrees, you may do as you wish. Furthermore, if you all collectively agree later on, you will be released from the promise."

"I promise!" I said, and for a fleeting moment, she looked supremely happy—though to me, the red scar on her forehead seemed to deny her any true happiness. She continued:—

"Promise me that you will not tell me anything about the plans being made for the campaign against the Count. Not a single word, hint, or implication; not at any point while I still bear this mark!" She pointed solemnly to the scar. Seeing how deeply serious she was, I answered just as solemnly:—

"I promise!" And as I spoke the words, I felt as though a heavy door had instantly been slammed shut between us.

Later, midnight.—Mina was bright and cheerful all evening. So much so that the rest of us seemed to draw courage from it, as if we were infected by her good mood. As a result, even I felt as though the heavy blanket of gloom weighing us down had lifted slightly. We all went to bed early. Mina is currently sleeping as peacefully as a little child. It is truly remarkable that she is still able to sleep normally in the midst of this terrible ordeal. Thank God for that, because at least while she sleeps, she can forget her troubles. Perhaps her peaceful example will help me sleep as well as her cheerfulness helped my mood tonight. I am going to try. Oh, how I long for a deep, dreamless sleep.

* * * * *

October 6, morning.—Another unexpected development. Mina woke me up early, around the same time as yesterday, and asked me to fetch Dr. Van Helsing. I assumed she needed to be hypnotized again, so I went to get the Professor without asking any questions. He was fully dressed in his room, clearly expecting a summons. He had left his door slightly ajar so he could hear our bedroom door open. He came with me immediately, and as he stepped into our room, he asked Mina if he should bring the others in as well.

"No," she said very simply. "That won't be necessary. You can tell them exactly what I'm about to say. I must go with you on your journey."

Dr. Van Helsing was just as shocked as I was. After a moment of silence, he asked:—

"But why?"

"You have to take me with you. I will be safer with you, and you will be safer with me."

"But why, my dear Madam Mina? You know that protecting you is our most sacred duty. We are heading into extreme danger—danger that you are more vulnerable to than any of us, because of... because of what has happened." He trailed off, looking embarrassed.

As she answered, she raised a finger and pointed to her forehead:—

"I know. That is exactly why I must go. I can speak freely now while the sun is coming up, but I might not be able to later. I know that if the Count commands me to go to him, I must obey. I know that if he orders me to come secretly, I will use any trick or deception—even against Jonathan—to get to him." God witnessed the look she gave me as she said those words, and if there is a Recording Angel, that look will surely be noted to her eternal honor. I could only squeeze her hand. I couldn't speak; my emotions were too overwhelming for even tears to relieve. She continued:—

"You men are brave and strong. You have strength in numbers, so you can resist the forces that would easily break a single person trying to fight alone. Besides, I might be useful. You can hypnotize me and discover things that even I am not consciously aware of." Dr. Van Helsing replied very seriously:—

"Madam Mina, as always, you are incredibly wise. You will come with us, and together we will finish what we are setting out to do." After he spoke, Mina remained silent for a long time, so I looked over at her. She had fallen back onto her pillow, fast asleep. She didn't even stir when I pulled up the blinds and let the morning sunlight flood the room. Van Helsing signaled for me to follow him quietly. We went to his room, and within a minute, Lord Godalming, Dr. Seward, and Mr. Morris joined us. He relayed what Mina had told us, and then added:—

"We will leave for Varna in the morning. We now have to manage a new variable: Madam Mina. Oh, but her soul is so pure. It was agonizing for her to confess what she just did, but it was absolutely the right thing to do, and it gave us warning just in time. We cannot afford to miss any chances, and we must be ready to strike the very second that ship arrives in Varna."

"What exactly is our plan?" Mr. Morris asked bluntly. The Professor paused for a moment before answering:—

"First, we will board the ship. Once we identify the correct box, we will place a branch of wild rose on top of it. We will tie it down securely, because folklore says a vampire cannot emerge as long as the rose is there. At this stage, we have to put our trust in superstition. Mankind's earliest faith was based on superstition, and superstition still has its roots in faith. Then, when we get the right opportunity—when no one else is around to see us—we will pry the box open, and... and everything will be resolved."

"I'm not going to wait for an opportunity," Morris declared. "The moment I see that box, I will rip it open and destroy the monster inside, even if a thousand people are watching! And if I get killed for it the very next second, so be it!" I instinctively grabbed his hand and found it gripped as firm as a piece of steel. I think he understood the gratitude in my eyes; I certainly hope he did.

"Good man," Dr. Van Helsing said approvingly. "Brave man. Quincey is a true man. God bless him for his courage. My boys, believe me when I say that not one of us will hesitate or hold back out of fear. I am simply outlining what we can do—what we must do. But the truth is, we have no idea exactly what actions we will take. There are far too many unpredictable variables, and the path ahead is so uncertain that we cannot say for sure until the actual moment arrives. We will be fully armed in every sense of the word, and when the final showdown comes, we will not hold back. Now, let us use today to put all our earthly affairs in order. Settle any matters concerning your loved ones and dependents, because none of us knows how, when, or what our end may be. I have already settled my own affairs, and since I have nothing else pending, I will go make the travel arrangements. I will purchase the tickets and handle everything we need for the journey."

There was nothing left to discuss, so we went our separate ways. I must now settle all my worldly affairs and prepare myself for whatever may happen....

* * * * *

Later.—It is done. My will is drawn up, and everything is settled. If Mina survives me, she is my sole heir. If she does not, then the rest of our friends who have been so good to us will inherit whatever is left.

It is now nearing sunset, and Mina's growing anxiety has drawn my attention. I am certain there is something weighing on her mind that the exact moment of sunset will bring out. These transitions between day and night have become agonizing times for all of us, as every sunrise and sunset brings a new threat or a fresh pang of suffering. However, perhaps this is part of God's will to achieve a good end. I am recording all of this in my journal because my darling wife must not hear these thoughts right now, but if the time ever comes when she can safely read them again, the record will be ready for her.

She is calling for me.
