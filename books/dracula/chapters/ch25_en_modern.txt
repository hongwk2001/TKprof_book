CHAPTER 25

DR. SEWARD’S DIARY

October 11, Evening.—Jonathan Harker has asked me to record this. He feels he hardly has the strength for the task himself, but he wants an accurate account to be kept.

I don’t think any of us were surprised when Mrs. Harker asked to see us just before sunset. We’ve recently realized that sunrise and sunset grant her a specific kind of freedom. During these times, her true self can emerge without any dark force controlling, restraining, or manipulating her actions. This state begins about half an hour before the actual sunrise or sunset and lasts until the sun is high in the sky or while the clouds are still glowing with light above the horizon. At first, she experiences a sort of detached state, as if some invisible bond has been loosened, and then complete freedom quickly follows. However, when this window of freedom closes, she quickly relapses into her oppressed state, preceded only by a brief, ominous silence.

When we gathered tonight, she seemed somewhat stiff and clearly showed signs of an intense internal struggle. I assumed she was making a desperate effort to seize control of herself at the earliest possible moment. Within a few minutes, however, she gained complete self-control. Motioning for her husband to sit beside her on the sofa where she was half-reclining, she asked the rest of us to pull our chairs in close. Taking her husband’s hand in hers, she began:

"We are all gathered here together in freedom, perhaps for the last time! I know, my dear; I know you will stay with me until the very end." She said this to her husband, whose grip on her hand visibly tightened. "Tomorrow morning we set out on our mission, and God only knows what awaits any of us. You are kind enough to bring me along. I know that you will do everything that brave, dedicated men can possibly do for a poor, weak woman whose soul might be lost—no, not lost yet, but certainly in grave danger. However, you must remember that I am not like you. There is a poison in my blood and my soul that may destroy me—that absolutely will destroy me unless we find some way to break the curse. Oh, my friends, you know as well as I do that my soul is hanging in the balance. While I know there is one immediate way out for me, neither you nor I must take it!" She looked pleadingly at each of us, starting and ending with her husband.

"What is that way out?" asked Van Helsing in a rough voice. "What is the path that we must not—cannot—take?"

"It is that I could die right now, either by my own hand or someone else's, before the ultimate evil is fully accomplished. I know, and you know, that if I were dead, you could and would free my immortal soul, just as you did for my poor friend Lucy. If death, or the fear of death, were the only thing stopping me, I would gladly choose to die right here and now, surrounded by the friends who love me. But death isn't the end of it. I cannot believe it is God's will for me to die when there is still hope before us and a difficult mission to finish. Therefore, I am willingly giving up the guarantee of eternal peace right now, choosing instead to walk into the darkness where the most horrific things of this world or the underworld may be waiting!" We all remained silent, instinctively knowing this was just an introduction. The others looked tense, and Harker’s face turned completely gray; he likely guessed what she was going to say better than the rest of us. She went on:

"This is everything I can put into the communal pot." I couldn't help but notice her use of the old legal term 'hotch-pot' in such a grim situation, though she spoke with complete seriousness. "What will each of you give? I know you will offer your lives," she said quickly, "which is easy enough for brave men. Your lives belong to God, and you can return them to Him. But what will you give to me?" She looked at us questioningly again, but this time she avoided making eye contact with her husband. Quincey seemed to catch her meaning; he nodded, and her face brightened. "Then I will tell you exactly what I want, because we can't afford to have any hesitation or uncertainty between us now. You must all promise me—every single one of you, even you, my beloved husband—that if the time comes, you will kill me."

"When exactly is that time?" Quincey asked, his voice low and tight.

"It is when you are absolutely convinced that I have changed so much that it is better for me to die than to live. When my flesh is dead in that way, you must immediately drive a stake through my heart and cut off my head, or do whatever else is required to give me true peace!"

Quincey was the first to stand after a brief pause. He knelt in front of her, took her hand in his, and said with deep solemnity:

"I’m just a rough guy who probably hasn't lived an honorable enough life to earn a distinction like this, but I swear to you on everything I hold sacred that if that time ever comes, I will not hesitate to do the duty you've asked of us. And I also promise you that I will make absolutely sure, because if I have even the slightest doubt, I will assume the time has come!"

"My true friend!" was all she could manage to say through her falling tears as she leaned forward and kissed his hand.

"I swear the same, my dear Madam Mina!" said Van Helsing.

"And I!" said Lord Godalming. Each of them knelt before her in turn to take the oath. I did the same. Then her husband turned to her, his eyes hollow and his skin a sickly green color that made his snow-white hair stand out even more, and asked:

"Must I make such a promise too, my wife?"

"You too, my dearest," she replied, her voice and eyes filled with deep, desperate pity. "You must not hesitate. You are the closest and most precious person to me; you are my entire world. Our souls are permanently bonded together for all of life and eternity. Remember, my love, that there have been times in history when brave men had to kill their own wives and female loved ones to prevent them from falling into the hands of a brutal enemy. Their hands didn't waver just because the women they loved begged to be killed. In times of terrible trial, it is a man's duty to do this for those he loves! And oh, my dear, if I must face death at someone's hand, let it be the hand of the man who loves me most. Dr. Van Helsing, I haven't forgotten how merciful you were in poor Lucy’s case by allowing the man who loved her—" she paused, blushing quickly, and corrected herself, "—by allowing the man who had the best right to give her peace to be the one to do it. If that time comes for me, I am counting on you to make sure my husband has the comforting memory that his own loving hand was the one to free me from this horrifying curse."

"I swear it again!" the Professor's booming voice rang out. Mrs. Harker smiled—she genuinely smiled—and let out a sigh of relief as she leaned back and said:

"And now, I must give you one final warning—a warning you must never forget. If this time ever comes, it may happen quickly and completely out of nowhere, and in that situation, you must not waste a single second. You have to take the opportunity immediately. When that moment arrives, I might be—no, if it comes, I absolutely will be—allied with your enemy against you."

"I have one more request," she said, her voice becoming very serious. "It isn't a matter of life and death like the other promise, but I would like you to do this one thing for me, if you're willing." We all silently agreed; there was no need for anyone to say a word.

"I want you to read the Burial Service for me." She paused as her husband let out a deep groan of anguish. Taking his hand in hers and holding it against her heart, she went on: "You must read it over me someday. No matter how this terrible situation ends, it will bring comfort to all of us, or at least to some of us. My dearest, I hope you will be the one to read it, because then I will always remember it in your voice, no matter what happens!"

"But oh, my darling," he pleaded, "death is still so far away from you."

"No, it isn't," she replied, holding up a hand to stop him. "I am closer to death right now than I would be if I were buried deep under the heavy earth!"

"Oh, my wife, do I really have to read it?" he asked, hesitating before he began.

"It would bring me peace, my husband!" was her only answer. Once she had the book ready for him, he started to read.

How could I—or anyone, for that matter—possibly describe that bizarre scene? The solemnity, the intense gloom, the profound sadness, the sheer horror of it... and yet, mixed in with all of that, a strange sweetness. Even the most hardened skeptic, someone who sees nothing but a cruel joke in anything religious or emotional, would have been moved to tears if they had seen this small group of fiercely loyal friends kneeling around this suffering, heartbroken woman. Or if they had heard the raw, tender passion in her husband’s voice as he read the simple, beautiful words of the Burial of the Dead, his voice breaking so often with emotion that he constantly had to stop. I—I can't even write about it anymore. The words... my voice... they just fail me!

*       *       *       *       *

Her intuition had been entirely correct. As strange as the whole thing was—and as bizarre as it will probably seem to us looking back on it later—it brought us a tremendous amount of comfort. When the silence finally fell, signaling that Mrs. Harker’s window of freedom was closing and she was relapsing, it didn't feel nearly as hopelessly desperate as we had feared it would.

Jonathan Harker’s Journal.

October 15, Varna.—We left Charing Cross station on the morning of the 12th, arrived in Paris that same night, and took our reserved seats on the Orient Express. We traveled continuously, day and night, and finally arrived here around five o'clock. Lord Godalming went straight to the Consulate to check for telegrams, while the rest of us came here to our hotel, "The Odessus." There might have been interesting sights or events during the trip, but I was in too much of a hurry to get here to notice or care about any of them. Until the ship Czarina Catherine docks in this port, absolutely nothing else in the world matters to me. Thank God! Mina is doing well and seems to be regaining her strength; her healthy color is finally returning. She sleeps a massive amount; she slept through almost the entire train ride. Right before sunrise and sunset, however, she becomes wide awake and sharply alert. It's become part of our routine for Van Helsing to hypnotize her during these specific windows of time. Initially, it took some effort and he had to use many hand gestures, but now she slips into a trance immediately, almost out of habit, and he barely has to do anything. During these moments, he seems able to control her simply by willing it, and her mind instantly obeys him. He always asks her what she can see and hear. To the first question, she answers:

"Nothing; it's completely dark." And to the second question:

"I can hear waves splashing against the side of a ship and water rushing past. The canvas sails and ropes are straining, and the wooden masts and beams are creaking. The wind is blowing hard—I can hear it whistling through the rigging, and the front of the ship is throwing up sea foam." It’s obvious that the Czarina Catherine is still out on the open ocean, hurrying toward Varna. Lord Godalming just got back. He received four telegrams, one for every day since we left London, and they all said exactly the same thing: the Czarina Catherine hasn't been reported to Lloyd’s shipping registry from any port. Before we left London, he arranged for his agent to wire him every single day with an update. The agent was instructed to send a message even if there was no news, just so Godalming would know for sure that someone was actively watching on the other end.

We ate dinner and went to bed early. Tomorrow, we plan to meet with the Vice-Consul and figure out a way to get on board the ship the exact moment it arrives. Van Helsing believes our best chance is to board the vessel between sunrise and sunset. The Count, even if he shape-shifts into a bat, cannot cross running water on his own, which means he'll be trapped on the ship. And since he wouldn't dare transform back into a human without raising suspicion—which he clearly wants to avoid—he'll be forced to stay hidden inside his earth-box. If we can get on board during the day, he will be completely at our mercy. We can pry open the box and deal with him—just like we did with poor Lucy—before he even wakes up. And we certainly won't be showing him any mercy. We don't anticipate having much trouble with the local officials or the ship's crew. Thank God we're in a country where you can buy your way out of anything with a bribe, and we have plenty of cash. We just need to make absolutely certain that the ship doesn't sneak into port during the night without us knowing, and then we'll be completely fine. A fat wallet will solve this problem for us, I'm sure of it!

*       *       *       *       *

October 16.—Mina’s hypnotic report remains exactly the same: splashing waves, rushing water, total darkness, and strong, helpful winds. We definitely arrived in plenty of time, and whenever we finally hear news of the Czarina Catherine, we will be ready. Since the ship has to pass through the Dardanelles strait, someone is bound to spot it and send a report.

*       *       *       *       *

October 17.—I believe everything is now firmly in place to "welcome" the Count when he returns from his travels. Godalming told the shipping company that he suspected the box loaded on board might contain property stolen from a friend. Through this explanation, he managed to secure a tentative agreement allowing him to open it at his own risk. The ship's owner provided him with a letter instructing the Captain to give Godalming full cooperation to do whatever he needed while on the vessel, and also sent a similar letter of authorization to their local agent in Varna. We've met with this agent, who was thoroughly impressed by Godalming’s polite and gracious manner. We are all confident that he will do absolutely everything in his power to help us. We have already planned our exact course of action once the box is open. If the Count is inside, Van Helsing and Seward will immediately sever his head and drive a stake through his heart. Morris, Godalming, and I will be standing by to prevent anyone from interfering, and we are fully prepared to use our weapons if necessary. The Professor assures us that if we treat the Count’s body this way, it will crumble into dust very quickly. If that happens, there won't be any physical evidence left to incriminate us, even if the authorities somehow suspect a murder took place. But even if evidence were left behind, we are prepared to face the consequences of our actions together. Perhaps someday this very diary will serve as the evidence needed to save one of us from the hangman's noose. Personally, I would gladly take that risk if the opportunity arises. We are determined to leave no stone unturned in executing our plan. We've even made arrangements with certain port officials to ensure a special messenger notifies us the precise second the Czarina Catherine is spotted.

*       *       *       *       *

October 24.—We have waited an entire week. Godalming receives telegrams every day, but the message is always exactly the same: "Not yet reported." Mina’s hypnotic reports every morning and evening also never change: she hears splashing waves, rushing water, and creaking masts.

Telegram, October 24th.

From Rufus Smith, Lloyd’s registry in London, to Lord Godalming, care of the British Vice-Consul in Varna.

"Czarina Catherine was reported this morning passing through the Dardanelles."

Dr. Seward’s Diary.

October 25.—I miss my phonograph so much! Writing a diary out by hand with a pen is incredibly tedious, but Van Helsing insists I have to do it. Yesterday, when Godalming received his telegram from Lloyd’s, we were all absolutely buzzing with excitement. I finally understand what soldiers feel right before a battle when the call to action is given. Mrs. Harker was the only person in our group who didn't show any emotion. Looking back, her calmness makes perfect sense; we had been extremely careful to keep the news a secret from her, and we all tried our hardest to hide our excitement while we were around her. In the past, I'm certain she would have picked up on our mood, no matter how hard we tried to conceal it. But she has changed significantly over the last three weeks. This deep lethargy continues to weigh her down. Even though she appears physically strong and well, and her natural color is starting to return, Van Helsing and I remain deeply concerned. We discuss her condition frequently, though we haven't breathed a word of it to the others. It would completely shatter poor Harker’s heart—and definitely destroy his nerves—if he even suspected we were worried about this. Van Helsing tells me that he meticulously examines her teeth while she is under hypnosis. He believes that as long as they don't start becoming pointed, there is no immediate danger of her actively transforming. But if that change does begin... we will have to take action! Neither of us has explicitly stated what those actions would be, but we both know exactly what needs to be done. And despite how horrifying it is to even think about, neither of us will back down from the task. "Euthanasia" is such a brilliant and comforting word! I am deeply grateful to whoever invented it.

Based on the speed the Czarina Catherine made from London to the Dardanelles, it's only about a 24-hour sail from the strait to our location. Therefore, the ship should arrive sometime tomorrow morning. Since it's impossible for her to dock before then, we have all decided to go to bed early. We plan to wake up at one o'clock in the morning so we are fully prepared.

*       *       *       *       *

October 25, Noon.—There is still no news of the ship arriving. Mrs. Harker’s hypnotic report this morning was identical to the others, meaning we could receive an update at literally any moment. The men are all pacing with nervous energy—except for Harker, who is remarkably calm. However, his hands are as cold as ice, and about an hour ago, I caught him sharpening the edge of the massive Gurkha knife he now carries everywhere. It will be a very bad day for the Count if the blade of that "Kukri" ever finds his throat, swung by that cold, relentless hand!

Van Helsing and I grew somewhat alarmed about Mrs. Harker today. Around noon, she slipped into a strange sort of lethargy that unsettled us. We didn't mention it to the others, but neither of us liked the look of it. She had been fidgety and restless all morning, so initially, we were just relieved to hear she had fallen asleep. But when her husband casually mentioned that she was sleeping so deeply he couldn't even wake her, we immediately went to her room to check on her ourselves. She was breathing normally and looked so healthy and peaceful that we decided the sleep was probably the best thing for her right now. Poor girl, she has so many awful memories to escape from; it's no wonder that this deep, oblivious sleep is doing her good.

*       *       *       *       *

Later.—Our assessment was correct. When she finally woke up after several hours of deep sleep, she seemed brighter and healthier than she had in days. At sunset, she gave her usual hypnotic report. Wherever he is in the Black Sea right now, the Count is rushing toward his destination. And, I hope, rushing straight toward his ultimate doom!

*       *       *       *       *

October 26.—Another day has passed with no word of the Czarina Catherine. She definitely should have arrived by now. It's clear that she is still sailing somewhere, because Mrs. Harker’s hypnotic report at sunrise was unchanged. It's possible the ship is occasionally dropping anchor due to heavy fog; a few steamships that arrived last night reported thick patches of fog both north and south of our port. We have to stay vigilant, as the ship's arrival could be signaled at any moment.

*       *       *       *       *

October 27, Noon.—This is extremely strange; we still have no news of the ship we're waiting for. Mrs. Harker gave her usual report last night and this morning: "splashing waves and rushing water," though she did mention that "the sound of the waves is very faint." The telegrams from London have all been identical: "no further updates." Van Helsing is incredibly anxious. He just told me he's afraid the Count might be slipping through our fingers. He then added, with deep meaning:

"I really don't like Madam Mina’s lethargy. Souls and memories can do very strange things when a person is in a trance." I was just about to ask him to explain further, but Harker walked in at that exact moment, and Van Helsing quickly held up a hand to warn me to be quiet. We need to try to get her to speak more thoroughly tonight at sunset when she's hypnotized.

*       *       *       *       *

October 28.—Telegram. From Rufus Smith, London, to Lord Godalming, care of the British Vice Consul in Varna.

"Czarina Catherine reported entering port of Galatz at one o'clock today."

Dr. Seward’s Diary.

October 28.—When the telegram arrived announcing the ship was in Galatz, I don't think any of us were as shocked as you might expect. True, we didn't know where, how, or when the curveball would come, but I think we all had a gut feeling that something bizarre was going to happen. The fact that the ship was overdue in Varna had already convinced each of us privately that things wouldn't go according to our plan; we were just waiting to find out exactly what would change. Even so, it was still a surprise. I suppose human nature is built on such a foundation of hope that we stubbornly believe things will work out the way they *should*, even when logic tells us they won't. This kind of idealistic thinking might be a guiding light for angels, but for humans, it's often a deceptive illusion. It was a strange moment, and we all reacted differently. Van Helsing threw his hand over his head for a second, almost as if he were protesting to God, but he didn't say a single word. A few seconds later, he stood up, his face set like stone. Lord Godalming turned deathly pale and sat there breathing heavily. I was half-stunned myself and just stared from one person to the next in sheer disbelief. Quincey Morris tightened his belt with a swift motion I recognized perfectly; back in our traveling days, that meant "time for action." Mrs. Harker turned a sickly, ghost-like white, making the scar on her forehead look like it was glowing red, but she simply folded her hands quietly and looked upward in prayer. Harker smiled—he actually smiled—but it was the dark, bitter smile of a man who has lost all hope. Yet his actions contradicted his despair, because his hands instinctively reached for the hilt of his massive Kukri knife and stayed there. "When does the next train leave for Galatz?" Van Helsing asked the room in general.

"At 6:30 tomorrow morning!" We all jumped, because the answer came from Mrs. Harker.

"How on earth do you know that?" Arthur asked.

"You must have forgotten—or maybe you never knew, though Jonathan and Dr. Van Helsing do—that I'm a train fanatic. Back home in Exeter, I always used to memorize the train schedules so I could help my husband with his work. It proved so useful that I've made a habit of studying timetables wherever I go. I figured that if we ever needed to get to Castle Dracula, we'd have to go through Galatz, or at least Bucharest, so I memorized those times very carefully. Unfortunately, there aren't many options to memorize, since the only train tomorrow leaves at the time I said."

"What an incredible woman!" the Professor murmured.

"Can’t we charter a private train?" Lord Godalming asked. Van Helsing shook his head. "I'm afraid not. This country operates very differently from yours or mine. Even if we managed to hire a special train, it probably wouldn't get us there any faster than the regular one. Besides, we have preparations to make. We need to think. Let's get organized now. Arthur, my friend, go to the station, buy the tickets, and make sure everything is ready for us to leave in the morning. Jonathan, my friend, go see the shipping agent here and get letters from him introducing us to their agent in Galatz, granting us the same authority to search the ship that we had here. Quincey Morris, go to the Vice-Consul and ask him to contact his counterpart in Galatz so we can get all the diplomatic help we need to make our journey smooth and avoid any delays crossing the Danube. John will stay here with Madam Mina and me, and we will strategize. If your tasks take a long time, you might be delayed, but it won't matter if you miss sunset, because I am here to take Madam's hypnotic report."

"And I," Mrs. Harker said brightly, looking more like her old self than she had in a very long time, "will try to be useful in any way I can. I will think and write for you all, just like I used to. Something is shifting inside me in a very strange way, and I feel more free than I have in a while!" The three younger men instantly looked happier, seeming to grasp the hopeful significance of her words. But Van Helsing and I turned to look at each other, and we both shared a grave, deeply troubled expression. We kept our mouths shut at the time, however.

Once the three men had left to run their errands, Van Helsing asked Mrs. Harker to get her copies of the typed diaries and find the section containing Harker’s journal from his time at the Castle. She left the room to get it. The moment the door clicked shut behind her, he turned to me:

"We're thinking the exact same thing! Say it!"

"There's been some kind of change. And the hope it gives me makes me feel sick to my stomach, because it might just be a trick."

"Exactly. Do you know why I asked her to go get the manuscript?"

"No," I replied, "unless it was just an excuse to get me alone."

"You're partially right, friend John, but only partially. I need to tell you something. And oh, my friend, I am taking a massive—a terrifying—risk, but I firmly believe it's the right thing to do. The moment Madam Mina said those words that stopped us both in our tracks, I had a sudden realization. During her trance three days ago, the Count sent his spirit to read her mind. Or, more likely, he pulled her mind to him, into his earth-box on the ship surrounded by rushing water, during those windows at sunrise and sunset when he has that freedom. In that moment, he learned that we are here waiting for him. Because out here in the real world, with her eyes to see and ears to hear, she has far more information to give him than he could ever gather while locked inside his coffin. So now, he's throwing all his effort into escaping us. Right now, he doesn't want anything to do with her.

"He's arrogant enough, with his vast knowledge, to believe she will always come whenever he eventually calls for her. But for now, he has cut her off—pushed her away and released her from his immediate control, as much as he is able to, so she can't connect with him. Ah! And that is where I find hope. I hope that our human brains—which have been human for so long and have not lost the grace of God—will prove superior to his childish brain. His mind has lain stagnant in a tomb for centuries; it hasn't grown to our level of maturity, and it only operates on selfish, and therefore narrow, motives. Here comes Madam Mina now. Do not say a single word to her about her trance! She doesn't know about it, and telling her would overwhelm her and plunge her into despair right when we desperately need her hope and courage. We need her brilliant brain—which is trained like a man's but possesses the sweet intuition of a woman—and we need that special psychic connection the Count accidentally gave her. He may not be able to take that connection away completely, even though he thinks he can. Hush! Just let me do the talking, and you'll see what I mean. Oh, John, my friend, we are in a truly terrifying situation. I am more afraid now than I have ever been in my life. All we can do is trust in a good God. Be quiet! Here she is!"

I was afraid the Professor was going to have a total nervous breakdown, just like he did when Lucy died, but through an incredible effort of will, he managed to control himself. By the time Mrs. Harker walked lightly into the room, he had completely regained his composure. She looked bright and cheerful, seemingly having forgotten her miserable situation now that she had a task to focus on. As she came in, she handed Van Helsing a stack of typewritten pages. He read through them with a serious expression, but his face gradually lit up as he went on. Then, holding the pages between his thumb and forefinger, he said:

"Friend John, for a man with as much experience as you have—and for you too, dear Madam Mina, even though you are young—there is a lesson here: never be afraid to follow a thought to its end. A half-formed idea has been buzzing around in my head for a while now, but I was afraid to let it take flight. But now, armed with this new information, I've traced that half-thought back to its source, and I realize it wasn't a half-thought at all. It was a complete, fully formed idea; it was just too young and weak to use its little wings yet. To borrow from my friend Hans Christian Andersen's *The Ugly Duckling*, it wasn't a duck-thought at all, but a magnificent swan-thought, just waiting for the right moment to spread its massive wings and fly. Look here at what Jonathan wrote:

'In a later era, another member of his bloodline repeatedly led his armies across the Great River into Turkish territory. Every time he was beaten back, he returned again, and again, and again. Even when he had to flee alone from a bloody battlefield where his entire army was being slaughtered, he kept coming back, because he possessed the absolute conviction that he alone would eventually be victorious.'

"What does this passage tell us? Not much, you say? Wrong! The Count’s childish brain couldn't see the significance, which is why he spoke about it so freely to Jonathan. Your adult brain didn't see it, and my adult brain didn't see it either, until just a moment ago. No! The breakthrough came from someone else dropping a single word without even realizing its importance—because she, too, didn't know what it meant, or what it *could* mean. It’s like when dormant chemical elements drift along naturally, and then they suddenly touch—and *boom!* A massive explosion of light fills the sky, blinding and killing some, but illuminating the entire landscape for miles around so everyone else can see clearly. Isn't that right? Well, let me explain. First of all, have you ever studied the psychology of criminals? Yes and no. You have, John, because it's essentially the study of madness. You have not, Madam Mina, because crime has never touched your life—except for this one terrible time. But your mind is sharply logical; you don't jump to generalized conclusions from a single isolated incident. Now, there is a specific trait common to all true criminals. It is so consistent, across all countries and all eras, that even police officers who know nothing about psychology recognize it from sheer experience. That is what it means to be empirical. A true criminal—someone who seems biologically destined for a life of crime and incapable of anything else—will always obsess over one specific type of crime. This kind of criminal does not possess a fully developed adult brain. He may be clever, cunning, and highly resourceful, but mentally, he has not reached full maturity. In many ways, he still has the brain of a child. 

"Now, *our* criminal is also destined for crime; he, too, operates with a childish brain, and his actions perfectly reflect that immaturity. A baby bird, a little fish, or a young animal doesn't learn through abstract principles; they learn through direct experience. And once they successfully learn how to do something, that success becomes the foundation they use to try to do more. Archimedes famously said, 'Give me a fulcrum, and I shall move the world!' Doing something successfully once is the fulcrum that allows a child's brain to develop into an adult's. But until a child develops the ambition to attempt something new, they will simply repeat the exact same successful action, over and over again! Oh, my dear, I can see your eyes widening; that flash of lightning just lit up the landscape for you, didn't it?" Mrs. Harker had started clapping her hands, her eyes sparkling with realization. He continued:

"Now it's your turn to speak. Tell us two dry men of science exactly what you see with those bright eyes of yours." He gently took her hand and held it while she spoke. I noticed that his thumb and forefinger were resting directly on her pulse, likely an instinctive and completely unconscious gesture on his part. 

"The Count is a criminal, a textbook criminal type," she began. "Criminologists like Nordau and Lombroso would classify him exactly that way, and as a criminal, his mind is imperfectly developed. Because of this, whenever he faces a difficult situation, he instinctively falls back on his past habits. His own history is the clue. The one page of his history that we actually know about—which Jonathan heard directly from his own lips—tells us that once before, when he was in what Mr. Morris would call a 'tight spot,' he retreated from the country he was trying to invade, went back to his homeland, and then, without ever losing sight of his ultimate goal, prepared himself for a brand new assault. He came back better equipped for the job, and that time, he won. 

"So, he came to London to invade a new territory. He was defeated. When all hope of success was gone and his very existence was in jeopardy, he fled back across the sea to his home—exactly like he fled back across the Danube River from Turkey all those centuries ago."

"Excellent, excellent! Oh, you are such a brilliantly clever woman!" Van Helsing said enthusiastically, leaning down to kiss her hand. A moment later, he turned to me and said, as calmly as if we were discussing a patient on a hospital ward:

"Her pulse is only seventy-two. Even with all this excitement. I have hope." Turning back to her, he said with eager anticipation:

"Please, keep going. Go on! There's more to this theory, if you're willing to share it. Don't be afraid; John and I understand. I certainly do, and I'll tell you if you're on the right track. Speak your mind without fear!"

"I will try, but you have to forgive me if I sound incredibly egotistical."

"No! Don't worry about that. You *must* be egotistical right now, because you are exactly what we need to focus on."

"Alright. Since he is a criminal, he is deeply selfish. And because his intellect is limited and his actions are driven entirely by selfishness, he restricts himself to a single, narrow goal. That goal is absolutely relentless. Just as he fled back across the Danube, abandoning his troops to be slaughtered without a second thought, he is now entirely focused on saving his own skin, completely careless of everything else. And because he is so selfishly focused on his own survival, it has actually loosened the terrifying grip he gained over my soul on that dreadful night! I felt it! Oh, I truly felt it! Thank God for His infinite mercy! My soul feels freer now than it has at any point since that awful hour. The only thing that still haunts me is the terrible fear that he might have used my knowledge for his own plans while I was in a trance or asleep." The Professor stood up abruptly:

"He *did* use your mind! And by using it, he intentionally stranded us here in Varna, while the ship carrying him sailed right past us, hidden in the fog, all the way to Galatz. He undoubtedly made preparations there to ensure his escape from us. But his childish mind could only plan that far ahead. And perhaps, as is so often the case with God’s Providence, the very trick the villain relied on most for his own selfish benefit will turn out to be the thing that destroys him. As the great Psalmist wrote, the hunter is caught in his own snare. 

"Right now, he believes he has completely shaken us off. He thinks he has escaped with a massive head start, so his selfish, childish brain will tell him it's safe to go to sleep. He also assumes that because he deliberately severed his connection to your mind, you can no longer read *his* mind. And that is exactly where he miscalculated! That horrific baptism of blood he forced upon you actually grants your spirit the freedom to seek him out—just as you have already been doing during those windows of freedom at sunrise and sunset. During those times, you go to him through *my* will, not his. You've forged this incredible power to help yourself and others out of the very suffering he inflicted upon you. And this weapon is even more precious now because he has no idea you have it! In his rush to protect himself, he even blocked himself from knowing our current location. We, however, are not selfish. We believe that God is standing with us through all this darkness and these many difficult hours. We are going to track him down, and we will not hesitate, even if we have to risk our own souls and risk becoming monsters like him in the process. Friend John, this has been an incredibly productive hour; it has pushed us miles ahead in our mission. You need to act as the scribe and write all of this down immediately. When the others get back from their errands, you can give it to them to read, so they will understand the situation exactly as we do."

And so, I have written all this down while we wait for the others to return. Mrs. Harker has also used her typewriter to document everything that has happened since she brought the manuscript into the room.
