May 5. The Castle. The gray dawn has cleared, and the sun has risen high above the distant horizon. I can't even tell if the jagged silhouette of the horizon is made of trees or mountain ridges. The distance is so great that large and small shapes just blur into one solid mass. I don't feel particularly sleepy. Since no one will wake me until I get up naturally, I plan to keep writing until my eyes close. There are mountains of strange events to record. Just in case anyone reading this thinks I was drunk when I left Bistritz, let me write down exactly what I ate last night.

I filled up on a dish called "Robber's Skewers." It consists of pieces of bacon, onion, and beef heavily coated in red pepper, strung on a stick, and roasted over a charcoal fire. It tastes just like the cheap meat given to cats on the streets of London! I paired it with a wine called "Golden Mediasch," which had a strange, slightly stinging taste on the tongue, but it wasn't bad. I had exactly two glasses and didn't touch any other alcohol.

As I was about to board the stagecoach, the driver, who hadn't taken his seat yet, was having a serious conversation with the landlady. Seeing them glance at me from time to time, I was sure they were talking about me. People sitting on a bench outside the inn, known as the "gossip seat," also crept closer to eavesdrop. Most of them clicked their tongues and looked at me with pity. Since the crowd was a mix of different nationalities, strange and harsh-sounding words kept catching my ear. I quietly pulled out my multi-language dictionary to look up their meanings. Unfortunately, not a single word made me feel any better. There was "Ordog" (Satan), "Pokol" (hell), "Stregoica" (witch), as well as "Vrolok" and "Vlkoslak." The last two words mean werewolf or vampire in Slovak and Serbian, respectively. (Memo: Don't forget to ask the Count about these superstitions.)

By the time we were ready to leave, quite a crowd had gathered in front of the inn. They all crossed themselves at once and pointed two fingers at me. I had to nudge the passenger next to me and ask what the gesture meant. He hesitated, but realizing I was English, he whispered that it was a charm against the "evil eye." For someone heading into an unknown land to meet a stranger, this explanation was incredibly unsettling. However, their faces were so full of genuine pity and sympathy that my heart warmed despite the circumstances.

I will never forget the final scene in the inn's courtyard. Beautiful oleanders and orange trees sat in green pots in the middle of the yard, and people in all kinds of clothing stood under the wide archway, crossing themselves at me. Our driver wore baggy linen pants (called "gotza") that covered the entire front seat. As he cracked his long whip over the four small ponies, our bumpy journey finally began.

As the beautiful scenery unfolded, my vague fears melted away. Of course, if I had understood the language my fellow passengers were speaking, I probably wouldn't have been able to relax and enjoy the view. Before us rolled sloping green hills covered in forests, and on the rugged mountain peaks, farmhouses were scattered sparsely, facing away from the trees and roads. The whole world was brightly colored with cherry, plum, pear, and apple blossoms. Every time the carriage drove by, white petals fluttered down onto the fresh green grass beneath the branches.

The road wound its way through these green hills (called "Mittel-land"). It seemed to disappear over gentle grassy slopes, then hid itself in pine forests that spilled down the mountain ridges like flickering flames. Because it was a dirt road, the wheels rattled terribly, but we flew along at a frantic pace. At the time, I couldn't understand why we were in such a hurry, but the driver seemed determined not to lose a single second before reaching the Borgo Pass. I heard it was a great road for traveling in the summer, but it seemed they hadn't repaired it yet after the winter snows melted.

Such rough road conditions are actually rare in the Carpathian Mountains. It is an old custom in this region to purposely leave the roads in bad shape. They say the old lords (called "Hospodars") deliberately avoided building good roads because they constantly worried that foreign enemies, especially the Turkish army, would use them to invade and start wars.

As we crossed the gentle hills of the Mittel-land, the true majesty of the Carpathian Mountains revealed itself with massive forests and steep cliffs. The late afternoon sun shone directly on the grand ridges on both sides, bringing out all the brilliant colors of this beautiful mountain range. The deep shadows of the peaks were dyed dark blue and purple, while the areas where grass and rocks mixed were colored green and brown. Jagged rocks and sharp cliffs continued layer after layer, finally disappearing beyond the distant horizon alongside giant snow-capped peaks. Whenever the sun began to set, white waterfalls crashed and gleamed through massive, gaping cracks in the mountains. As the carriage rounded the base of the mountain, a snow-capped peak that seemed to tower right in front of our noses showed off its grandeur. The passenger sitting next to me gently tapped my arm.

"Look there! It's Isten Szek!" he said, reverently crossing himself. "It means the Seat of God!"

As we endlessly traveled the snake-like winding road, the sun slowly sank behind us, and a thick evening mist wrapped around us. Only the snow-capped mountain peaks held onto the last rays of the sunset, turning a cold, soft pink. From time to time, Czechs and Slovaks in beautiful traditional clothing passed by on the road. Unfortunately, quite a few of them had bulging necks due to swollen thyroids. Whenever we sped past a cross set up by the road, every single passenger crossed themselves. I also frequently saw peasant men and women kneeling and praying earnestly in front of the crosses. They seemed completely oblivious to the world, not even flinching at the loud noise of our carriage.

Everything looked fascinating to me, from the haystacks tilted precariously in the trees to the clusters of weeping birches flashing their silver bark through light green leaves. We also passed several traditional wagons called "Leiterwagen." Shaped like the long spine of a snake, these wagons were perfect for traveling on bumpy roads. Inside the wagons, there were usually peasants heading home. There was a mix of Czechs in pure white sheepskin coats and Slovaks in brightly colored sheepskin coats; the latter held long staffs with axes on the end, grasping them like spears. As the day ended, the air turned freezing cold. In the deepening twilight, the oak, beech, and pine forests transformed into one massive, eerie curtain of darkness.

In the deep valleys cut between the mountains, the snow hadn't fully melted yet, and the dark fir trees towering over it stood out sharply. Whenever the road cut through a dense pine forest, the darkness on both sides pressed in on us as if it would swallow us at any moment. The gloomy atmosphere created by the gray masses of trees kept pulling me into ominous thoughts. As the setting sun lit up the bizarre clouds that endlessly swirled through the valleys of the Carpathian Mountains, my wild imagination only grew worse. On the steep uphill roads, no matter how much the driver rushed, the horses' steps naturally slowed down. Just like back home in England, I wanted to get out of the carriage and walk up, but the driver jumped at the idea and stopped me.
