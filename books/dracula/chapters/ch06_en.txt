[P001] CHAPTER 6

[P002] MINA MURRAY'S JOURNAL

[P003a] July 24. Whitby.—Lucy came to meet me at the station, looking even sweeter and more lovely than before, and we took a carriage up to the Crescent, a curved residential street where they are renting rooms. This is truly a beautiful place. The little river Esk flows through a deep valley, widening as it approaches the harbor. A massive viaduct with high piers crosses it, making the view through the arches seem somehow farther away than it actually is.

[P003b] The valley is a lush green, and it is so steep that when you stand on the high ground on either side, you look directly across unless you gaze straight down. The houses in the old town, on the side opposite to us, all have red roofs and seem stacked haphazardly on top of one another, much like paintings of Nuremberg in Germany. Right above the town lie the ruins of Whitby Abbey, which was plundered by the Danes (Vikings).

[P003c] It also serves as the setting for a scene in the poet Walter Scott's epic "Marmion," where a girl is punished by being walled up alive. It is a highly noble ruin, massive in scale and full of beautiful, romantic fragments. There is a legend that a ghost of a lady in white can be seen in one of the windows.
[P003b] Between the abbey and the town sits another church, the parish one, surrounded by a large graveyard filled with tombstones.

[P003d] To my mind, this is the finest spot in Whitby, as it sits right above the town, offering a commanding view of the harbor and the entire bay out to a headland called Kettleness stretching into the sea. The ground descends so steeply toward the harbor that a portion of the hillside has collapsed, destroying some of the graves. In one spot, part of the gravestones' masonry juts out far above the sandy path below. There are walking paths with benches winding through the churchyard, and people sit there all day taking in the beautiful scenery and enjoying the breeze.

[P003e] I intend to come here often to sit and work. In fact, I am writing this right now with my notebook on my lap, listening to the conversation of three old men sitting beside me. They seem to do nothing all day but sit up here and talk.

[P004] The harbor is visible below me. On the far side, a long granite breakwater stretches out into the sea, curving outward at its end, with a lighthouse situated in the middle. Outside of it, a heavy sea wall runs along the length. On the near side, the sea wall bends backward like a crooked elbow, and it too has a lighthouse at its end. Between the two breakwaters lies a narrow entrance to the harbor, which then suddenly opens up.

[P005a] The view is nice at high tide, but when the tide goes out, the water recedes to leave the bottom exposed, leaving just the stream of the Esk flowing between sandbars dotted with rocks. Outside the harbor on this side, a great reef rises for about half a mile, its sharp edge extending straight out from behind the south lighthouse. At the end of the reef is a buoy with a bell that tosses about in bad weather, carrying a mournful sound on the wind.

[P005b] The locals have a legend that whenever a ship is wrecked, you can hear bells ringing out at sea. I should ask the old man coming this way about it.

[P006] He is an amusing old man. He must be incredibly old, for his face is as wrinkled and gnarled as tree bark. He tells me he is nearly a hundred, and that he was a sailor in the Greenland whaling fleet back in 1815 when the Battle of Waterloo was fought. Unfortunately, he seems to be a very skeptical person. When I asked him about the bells at sea and the White Lady at the abbey, he answered rather bluntly:

[P007a] "I wouldn't trouble myself with those things, miss. Those stories are all worn out. Mind you, I'm not saying they never happened, but they certainly didn't happen in my time. Those tales might work on outsiders and day-trippers, but they aren't fit for a fine young lady like yourself. Those foolish folks from York and Leeds who come here eating salted herring, drinking tea, and looking to buy cheap jet jewelry will believe anything.

[P007b] I wonder who even bothers to go to the trouble of telling them lies—even those newspapers, which are just full of nonsense." I thought he would be a good source of interesting tales from his old whaling days, so I asked if he would mind telling me something about catching whales. Just as he was settling in to start his story, the clock struck six, at which point he struggled to his feet and said:

[P008] "I must head back home now, miss. My granddaughter doesn't like to be kept waiting when supper is ready. Since there are so many steps, it takes me a while to hobble up them. Besides, miss, judging by the clock, my stomach is quite empty."

[P009a] He hobbled away, and I could see him hurrying down the steps as fast as he could. Those steps are a major attraction here. There are hundreds of them leading from the town up to the church—I don't know exactly how many—winding their way up in an elegant curve. The incline is so gentle that even a horse could easily walk up and down. I imagine they originally had something to do with the abbey. I should go home, too. Lucy went out making visits with her mother, and since they were just formal duty calls, I didn't go along.

[P009b] They should be back home by now.

[P010] *       *       *       *       *

[P011a] 11. August 1.—I came up here an hour ago with Lucy, and we had a very interesting conversation with my old friend and the two other men who always join him. He is clearly the sage or oracle among them, and I suspect he must have been quite dictatorial in his younger days. He never concedes a point and browbeats everyone. If he cannot win an argument with logic, he resorts to bullying, and then takes their silence as agreement. Lucy was wearing a white lawn dress and looked absolutely sweet and lovely.

[P011b] Her complexion has also become beautiful since she arrived here. I noticed that as soon as we sat down, the old men wasted no time coming over to sit near her. She is so gentle with the elderly; I think they all fell in love with her on the spot. Even my stubborn old man surrendered and didn't contradict her, choosing instead to snap at me twice as much. When I brought up the subject of legends, he immediately launched into a sort of sermon. I must try to remember his words and write them down:

[P012a] 12. "It's all nonsense, from start to finish. Nothing more, nothing less. All those stories about curses, ghosts, monsters, and goblins are only good for making children and silly women cry. They are just empty bubbles floating in the air. Those things, along with all the omens and warnings, were invented by parsons, spiteful scholars, and railway ticket touts just to scare gullible people and make them do things they wouldn't normally do. It makes my blood boil just thinking about it.

[P012b] Why, not satisfied with printing lies on paper and preaching them from the pulpit, they now insist on carving those lies onto gravestones. Look all around you in any direction you please. Look at those tombstones, trying to stand tall but leaning sideways. They are simply collapsing under the weight of the lies written on them. Every single one says 'Here lies' or 'Sacred to the memory,' yet nearly half of them don't even have a body underneath. And nobody cares a bit about their memory, let alone considers it sacred. It's all lies, one kind of lie or another!

[P012c] Good heavens, it will be quite a spectacle on Judgment Day when the dead come pouring out in their shrouds, scrambling and dragging their tombstones along just to prove how good they were! Some of them have been lying in the sea for so long that their hands are swollen and slippery, and they'll be shivering, unable to even get a proper grip on their own gravestones."

[P013] 13. I could tell from the old man's smug expression and the way he looked around for his friends' approval that he was showing off, so I offered a word to keep him going:

[P014] 14. "Oh, Mr. Swales, you can't be serious. Surely all these tombstones aren't fake?"

[P015a] 15. "Nonsense! Aside from those who make themselves out to be far better than they were, there might be a very small few that aren't lies. There are always folks who think a tiny bowl is as big as the sea, as long as it belongs to them. But the whole thing is just a pack of lies. Now look here. You are a stranger here, miss, and you see this churchyard." I didn't completely understand his dialect, but I thought it best to agree. I knew it had something to do with the church.

[P015b] He continued: "And you suppose that all these stones sit above people who are buried here nicely and peacefully?" I agreed again. "Well, that is exactly where the lie begins. There are dozens of these graves that are as empty as old Dun's tobacco box on a Friday night." He nudged one of his friends with his elbow, and they all laughed. "And my goodness! How could it be otherwise? Look at that tombstone over there, the furthest one past the bier path. Read it!" I walked over and read:

[P016] 16. "Edward Spencelagh, master mariner, murdered by pirates off the coast of Andres, April, 1854, age 30." When I returned, Mr. Swales continued:

[P017a] 17. "Who do you suppose brought him home to bury him here? Murdered off the coast of Andres, and you thought his body was lying underneath! Why, I could name a dozen men whose bones are up in the Greenland seas"—he pointed north—"or washed away by the currents. The tombstones all around you are just like that. With your young eyes, you can read the lies written in small print even from here. Take Braithwaite Lowrey—I knew his father—lost on the Lively off Greenland in '20. Or Andrew Woodhouse, drowned in the same waters in 1777.

[P017b] John Paxton, drowned off Cape Farewell a year later. And old John Rawlings, whose grandfather sailed with me, drowned in the Gulf of Finland in '50. Do you really think all these men have to rush back to Whitby when the trumpet sounds? I seriously doubt it! I tell you, when they get here, they will be pushing and shoving each other so much it will be like the old days fighting on the ice.

[P017c] Back then, we fought each other from dawn to dusk, bandaging our cuts by the light of the aurora borealis." This was clearly a local joke, because the old man chuckled as he spoke, and his friends enthusiastically agreed.

[P018] 18. "But," I said, "you are not entirely correct, because you are basing this on the assumption that all the poor people, or their spirits, will have to take their tombstones with them on Judgment Day. Do you really think that will be necessary?"

[P019] 19. "Well, what else are tombstones for? Answer me that, miss!"

[P020] 20. "To comfort their families, I suppose."

[P021] 21. "To please their relatives, you suppose!" he said with intense scorn. "How will it comfort their relatives to know that lies are written above them, and that everybody in town knows they are lies?" He pointed to a stone at our feet that served as a flat slab supporting the bench, right near the edge of the cliff. "Read the lies on that flat gravestone," he said. The letters were upside down from where I sat, but Lucy was facing them directly, so she leaned over and read:

[P022] 22. "Sacred to the memory of George Canon, who died, in the hope of a glorious resurrection, on July 29, 1873, after falling from the rocks at Kettleness. This tomb was erected by his sorrowing mother to her dearly beloved son. 'He was the only son of his mother, and she was a widow.' Really, Mr. Swales, I don't see anything very funny about that!" She spoke her comment very gravely and somewhat severely.

[P023a] 23. "You don't see anything funny! Ha! ha! That's because you don't know that the sorrowing mother was an awful old hag who hated him because he was crippled—a total hunchback he was—and he hated her so much that he committed suicide just so she wouldn't collect the life insurance she took out on him. He blew off almost the top of his head with an old musket they kept for scaring crows. It wasn't scaring crows after that, because it only brought the horseflies and maggots to his corpse. That's how he really fell off the rocks.

[P023b] And as for hoping for a glorious resurrection, I've heard him say himself many times that he hoped he would go to hell. His mother was so religious that she was sure to go to heaven, and he didn't want to end up anywhere near her. Now, isn't that gravestone"—he pounded it with his stick as he spoke—"a complete pack of lies? And won't the angel Gabriel chuckle when crippled little George comes panting up the steps with that tombstone balanced on his hump, asking for it to be accepted as evidence!"

[P024] 24. I didn't know what to say, but Lucy changed the subject as she stood up:

[P025] 25. "Oh, why did you have to tell us this? This is my favorite seat, and I don't want to leave it; but now I find I must keep sitting over the grave of a suicide."

[P026a] 26. "That won't harm you, my pretty; and it might make poor George happy to have such a tidy lass sitting on his lap. It won't hurt you. Goodness, I've sat here on and off for nearly twenty years, and it hasn't done me any harm. Don't you worry about who lies beneath you, or who doesn't lie there either! It will be time enough to get scared when you see all the tombstones run away and the place stripped as bare as a harvested field. There's the clock, and I must be going.

[P026b] Good day to you, ladies!" And he hobbled away.

[P027a] 27. Lucy and I sat for a while. Everything before us was so beautiful that we held hands as we sat. She told me all over again about Arthur and their upcoming marriage. It made me just a little heart-sick, because I haven't heard from Jonathan for a whole month.

[P027b] 28. * * * * *

[P028] *       *       *       *       *

[P029a] 29. The same day. I came up here alone because I am very sad. There was no letter for me. I hope nothing is wrong with Jonathan. The clock has just struck nine. I can see the lights scattered all over the town, sometimes in rows along the streets, and sometimes on their own; they run right up the river Esk and fade away in the curve of the valley. To my left, the view is blocked by the black roofline of the old house next to the abbey.

[P029b] The sheep and lambs are bleating in the fields far behind me, and I can hear the clatter of a donkey's hooves on the paved road below. The band on the pier is playing a loud waltz keeping good time, and further down the quay, a Salvation Army meeting is going on in a back street. Neither group can hear the other, but up here I can hear and see them both. I wonder where Jonathan is and if he is thinking of me! I wish he were here.

[P030] 30. Dr. Seward's Diary.

[P031a] 31. June 5.—The case of Renfield grows more interesting the more I come to understand the man. He has certain strongly developed traits: selfishness, secrecy, and purpose. I wish I could figure out the ultimate object of his purpose. He seems to have some definite scheme of his own, but I do not yet know what it is. His one redeeming quality is a love of animals, although he takes such bizarre turns with it that I sometimes think he is simply abnormally cruel. His pets are of an odd sort. Right now, his hobby is catching flies.

[P031b] He currently has such a massive swarm of them that I had to firmly object. To my surprise, he didn't fly into a rage as I expected, but took the matter quite seriously. He thought for a moment and then asked, "May I have three days? I will clear them away." I told him that would be fine, of course. I must keep a close eye on him.

[P031c] 32. * * * * *

[P032] *       *       *       *       *

[P033a] 33. June 18.—He has now shifted his attention to spiders, and has managed to keep several very large ones in a box. He feeds them his flies, and the number of flies is visibly decreasing, even though he uses half his own food to attract more flies from outside into his room.

[P033b] 34. * * * * *

[P034] *       *       *       *       *

[P035a] 35. July 1.—His spiders are now becoming as much of a nuisance as his flies were, and today I told him he had to get rid of them. He looked very sad at this, so I compromised and said he must at least clear out some of them. He cheerfully agreed to this, and I gave him the same three-day deadline as before to reduce their numbers. He thoroughly disgusted me while I was with him. A horrible blowfly, bloated from feeding on something rotten, buzzed into the room.

[P035b] He snatched it out of the air, held it triumphantly between his thumb and finger for a few seconds, and then, before I could stop him, popped it into his mouth and ate it. I scolded him for doing it, but he calmly argued that it was very good and wholesome; he claimed it was life, strong life, and that it transferred that life to him. This gave me an idea, or at least the beginnings of one. I must watch carefully to see how he gets rid of his spiders.

[P035c] He clearly has some complex problem working in his mind, because he keeps a small notebook where he is constantly jotting things down. Whole pages are filled with masses of figures—usually single numbers added up in batches, and then those totals added up again in larger batches, as if he were auditing some kind of morbid ledger.

[P035d] 36. * * * * *

[P036] *       *       *       *       *

[P037a] 37. July 8.—There is a method to his madness, and the germ of the idea in my mind is growing. It will soon be a complete theory, and then my unconscious mind will have to hand the reins over to conscious thought. I stayed away from my patient for a few days so I could observe if there were any changes. Things remain much as they were, except he has disposed of some of his old pets and acquired a new one. He managed to catch a sparrow and has already partially tamed it.

[P037b] His method of taming it is simple enough, as his spider population has already plummeted. The spiders that do remain, however, are very well fed, since he continues to lure in flies using his own food.

[P037c] 38. * * * * *

[P038] *       *       *       *       *

[P039] 39. July 19.—We are making progress. My patient now has a whole flock of sparrows, and his flies and spiders have been nearly wiped out. When I entered his room, he ran up to me and said he wanted to ask a great favor—a very, very great favor. As he spoke, he fawned over me like a begging dog. I asked him what it was, and he replied with a look of pure rapture:

[P040] 40. "A kitten! A nice, sleek, playful little kitten that I can play with, and teach, and feed—and feed—and feed!" I wasn't entirely unprepared for this request, as I had noticed the steady progression in the size and vitality of his pets. However, I didn't want his pretty little family of tame sparrows to be wiped out the same way the flies and spiders were. I told him I would think about it, and asked if he wouldn't rather have a full-grown cat instead of a kitten. His eagerness gave him away as he answered:

[P041a] 41. "Oh, yes, I would love a cat! I only asked for a kitten in case you refused to give me a cat. No one would refuse me a kitten, would they?" I shook my head and told him that I was afraid it wasn't possible at the moment, but that I would look into it. His face fell, and I saw a clear warning of danger in his expression—a sudden, fierce, sideways glare that was murderous. The man is a developing homicidal maniac.

[P041b] I plan to test him by denying this current craving to see how he reacts; that will tell me more.

[P041c] 42. * * * * *

[P042] *       *       *       *       *

[P043a] 43. 10 p.m.—I visited him again and found him sulking in a corner. When I entered, he threw himself on his knees in front of me and begged me to let him have a cat, claiming his very salvation depended on it. I stood firm, however, and told him no. At that, he got up without a word, retreated to the corner where I had found him, and sat down to gnaw on his fingers. I will check on him early tomorrow morning.

[P043b] 44. * * * * *

[P044] *       *       *       *       *

[P045a] 45. July 20.—I visited Renfield very early, before the ward attendant made his rounds. I found him awake and humming a tune. He was scattering sugar he had saved on the windowsill, clearly preparing to start catching flies again—and doing so cheerfully and in good spirits. I looked around for his birds, and when I didn't see them, I asked where they had gone. Without even turning around, he replied that they had all flown away. However, there were a few feathers scattered around the room and a single drop of blood on his pillow.

[P045b] I didn't say anything to him, but I went to the attendant and told him to report back if he noticed anything unusual about Renfield during the day.

[P045c] 46. * * * * *

[P046] *       *       *       *       *

[P047a] 47. 11 a.m.—The attendant just came to tell me that Renfield was violently sick and threw up a huge pile of feathers. "If you ask me, Doctor," he said, "I think he ate his birds, and he just swallowed them down raw!"

[P047b] 48. * * * * *

[P048] *       *       *       *       *

[P049a] 49. 11 p.m.—I gave Renfield a heavy dose of opiates tonight—enough to put even him to sleep—and took his notebook to examine it. The idea that has been bouncing around in my head recently has finally taken shape, and my theory is proven correct. My homicidal maniac is of a very specific variety. I will have to create a new psychiatric classification for him: a "zoophagous" (life-eating) maniac. His goal is to absorb as many lives as possible, and he has methodically set out to achieve this by accumulating them.

[P049b] He fed many flies to a single spider, and many spiders to a single bird, and then he wanted a cat to consume all those birds. What would his next step have been? It would almost be worth letting the experiment play out. It could be done if there were a compelling enough reason. People used to condemn animal dissection, but look at the medical advances it has brought us!

[P049c] Why shouldn't we push the boundaries of science in its most difficult and crucial area: understanding the human brain? If I could unlock the secret of even one mind like this—if I held the key to the inner workings of just one lunatic—I could advance my field of science to a level that would make the groundbreaking work of modern physiologists look like child's play. If only I had a good enough reason! I must stop dwelling on this, or I might give in to the temptation.

[P049d] A noble cause might just convince me, for who's to say I don't also possess a naturally exceptional brain?

[P050] 50. The man's logic is flawless; lunatics are always perfectly logical within the confines of their own delusions. I wonder how many lower lives he thinks a human life is worth, or if he counts a human as just one life. He perfectly balanced his morbid ledger and began a new account today. How many of us actually start with a clean slate each day of our lives?

[P051] 51. It feels like only yesterday that my hopes were dashed by Lucy's rejection, and I truly began a new chapter in my life. And so it will be until God, the Great Recorder, balances my ledger of life and closes my account, for better or worse. Oh, Lucy, Lucy, I cannot be angry with you, nor can I resent my friend who has found happiness with you. I must simply work and endure this hopeless waiting. Work! I must work!

[P052] 52. If only I had a driving purpose as strong as my poor, mad patient's—a noble, unselfish cause to devote my energy to—that would be true happiness.

[P053] 53. Mina Murray's Journal.

[P054a] 54. July 26.—I am feeling anxious, and writing here brings me comfort; it's like whispering my worries to myself and being my own listener. Using shorthand also makes it feel more private than regular writing. I am worried about both Lucy and Jonathan. I hadn't heard from Jonathan in a while and was growing very concerned, but yesterday, dear Mr. Hawkins, who is always so thoughtful, forwarded me a letter from him. I had written to Mr. Hawkins to ask if he had any news, and he replied that the enclosed letter had just arrived.

[P054b] It was just a single sentence sent from Castle Dracula, stating that he was on his way home. That isn't like Jonathan at all. I don't understand it, and it makes me uneasy.
[P054b] Furthermore, even though Lucy is in good health, she has recently started sleepwalking again. Her mother mentioned it to me, and we agreed that I should lock our bedroom door every night. Mrs. Westenra has this terrifying fear that sleepwalkers always wander out onto roofs or cliff edges, suddenly wake up, and plunge to their deaths with a scream.

[P054c] The poor woman is naturally worried about her daughter. She told me that her late husband, Lucy's father, had the same habit; if no one stopped him, he would get up in the middle of the night, get dressed, and leave the house. Lucy is getting married in the autumn, and she is already busy planning her dresses and imagining how to decorate her new home. I share her excitement, as I am dreaming of the same things, though Jonathan and I will be starting out much more modestly and will have to stick to a tight budget. Mr.

[P054d] Holmwood—the Honorable Arthur Holmwood, Lord Godalming's only son—is coming to visit very soon. He will leave London as soon as he can, as his father hasn't been well, and I know dear Lucy is counting the minutes until he arrives. She wants to bring him up to this bench on the churchyard cliff to show him how beautiful Whitby is. I expect it's the waiting that is making her restless; she should be fine once he gets here.

[P055] *       *       *       *       *

[P056a] 55. * * * * *

[P056b] 56. July 27.—Still no news from Jonathan. I am growing quite worried about him, though I couldn't say exactly why. I just wish he would write, even if it were only a single line. Lucy is sleepwalking more than ever, and her moving around the room wakes me up every night. Luckily, the weather is so hot that she isn't in danger of catching a cold, but the constant anxiety and broken sleep are starting to take their toll on me. I am becoming nervous and insomniac myself. Thank God Lucy's health remains strong. Mr.

[P056c] Holmwood was suddenly called away to Ring because his father fell seriously ill. Lucy is upset about the delay in seeing him, but it hasn't affected her appearance; she has gained a little weight, and her cheeks have a lovely rosy glow. She has completely lost that pale, anemic look she used to have. I pray she stays this well.

[P056d] 57. * * * * *

[P057] *       *       *       *       *

[P058a] 58. August 3.—Another week has passed, and still no word from Jonathan, not even to Mr. Hawkins, whom I checked with. Oh, I desperately hope he isn't sick. If he were well, he surely would have written. I keep re-reading his last short letter, but something about it bothers me. It doesn't sound like him, even though it is definitely his handwriting. There is no mistaking that. Lucy hasn't sleepwalked much this past week, but she has developed a strange, intense focus that I don't understand; even in her sleep, she seems to be watching me.

[P058b] She tries the door, and when she finds it locked, she searches the room for the key.

[P059a] 59. August 6.—Another three days, and still no news. This suspense is becoming unbearable. If I only knew where to write or where to go, I would feel better, but no one has heard a word from Jonathan since that brief note. I can only pray to God for patience. Lucy is more high-strung than ever, though physically fine. The weather looked very threatening last night, and the local fishermen are predicting a storm. I should try to observe it and learn how to read the weather signs. Today is incredibly gloomy.

[P059b] As I write this, the sun is hidden behind thick clouds high over Kettleness. Everything is painted in shades of gray—except the green grass, which stands out like emeralds. There is gray rock, and gray clouds, edged with a faint sunburst in the distance, hanging over the gray sea. The sandy points stretching into the water look like gray fingers. The sea is crashing over the shallows and sand flats with a dull roar that is muffled by the sea mist rolling inland. The horizon has vanished entirely in the haze.

[P059c] The landscape feels vast and overwhelming; the clouds are stacked up like massive boulders, and there is a low rumbling over the sea that sounds like a warning of impending doom. Dark figures dot the beach, sometimes half-hidden in the mist, looking like trees walking around. The fishing boats are racing back to port, pitching violently in the heavy swells as they steer into the harbor, leaning over so far their decks are nearly touching the water. Here comes old Mr. Swales.

[P059d] He is walking straight toward me, and I can tell by the way he tips his hat that he wants to speak with me....

[P060] 60. I was deeply moved by the change in the poor old man. He sat down next to me and spoke very gently:

[P061] 61. "I have something I need to tell you, miss." I could see he was uneasy, so I gently held his poor, wrinkled old hand in mine and encouraged him to speak his mind. Leaving his hand resting in mine, he said:

[P062a] 62. "My dear, I'm afraid I must have shocked you these past few weeks with all the wicked things I've said about the dead and everything else. But I didn't truly mean them, and I want you to remember that when I'm gone. Us old folks, with one foot already in the grave, don't much like thinking about death, and we certainly don't want to feel terrified of it. That's why I made a habit of making light of it—just to cheer my own heart up a bit. But God knows, miss, I am not afraid of dying.

[P062b] Not one bit. It's just that I don't want to die if I can avoid it. But my time must be near now. I am very old, and a hundred years is more than any man has a right to expect. I am so close to the end that I can almost hear the Grim Reaper sharpening his scythe. You see, I couldn't just drop the habit of joking about it all at once; my jaw just kept moving the way it was used to.

[P062c] Someday soon, the Angel of Death will blow his trumpet for me.
[P062b] But please, my dear, don't weep or mourn for me!"—he said this because he saw that I had started crying—"If he comes for me this very night, I will answer his call without hesitation. Because, after all, life is really just a waiting room for whatever comes next. Death is the only thing we can truly rely on. And I am at peace, my dear, because it is coming for me, and coming quickly. It might be coming right now, while we sit here wondering.

[P062d] Maybe it's hidden in that wind blowing in off the sea—the one that brings loss, shipwrecks, terrible distress, and broken hearts. Look! Look!" he cried suddenly. "There is something in that wind, and in the cough of the waves out there, that sounds, looks, tastes, and smells like death. It is in the air. I can feel it coming. Lord, let me answer cheerfully when my time comes!" He devoutly raised his arms and tipped his hat. His lips moved silently, as if in prayer.

[P062e] After a few minutes of silence, he stood up, shook my hand, gave me his blessing, said goodbye, and hobbled away. The whole encounter moved me deeply and left me very upset.
