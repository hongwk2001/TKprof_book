Chapter 1

Jonathan Harker's Journal

(Kept in shorthand.)

May 3. Bistritz. - I left Munich at 8:35 PM on May 1 and arrived in Vienna early the next morning. We were supposed to arrive at 6:46 AM, but the train was delayed by an hour. Budapest looked like a beautiful city from what I could see from the train and around the station. Because we arrived late and I didn't know when we would leave, I was afraid to wander too far. It felt as if I was leaving the West and stepping into the East. Crossing the grand, modern bridges over the Danube River, I could really feel that this was a land with traces of old Turkish rule. The Danube seemed unusually wide and deep.

We left mostly on time and reached Klausenburg by nightfall. I decided to stay overnight at the Hotel Royale. For dinner, or rather a late supper, I had a chicken dish cooked with plenty of red pepper. It was incredibly tasty, but it made me very thirsty. (Memo: Make sure to get the recipe for Mina.) I asked the waiter, and he said it was called "Paprika Hendl." He told me it was a traditional local dish, so I should be able to find it anywhere along the Carpathian Mountains. My basic German skills turned out to be quite useful. Honestly, if I didn't know German, I can't imagine how I would have managed here.

Whenever I had free time in London, I visited the British Museum library to look up books and maps about Transylvania. I figured that learning about the country beforehand would certainly help when dealing with a nobleman from there. The area the Count mentioned was in the extreme east, right where the borders of Transylvania, Moldavia, and Bukovina meet. It is located right in the middle of the Carpathian Mountains, making it one of the wildest and most remote regions in Europe.

Unfortunately, I couldn't find any detailed maps comparable to our British maps, so I couldn't find any documents pointing out the exact location of Castle Dracula. However, I did confirm that Bistritz, the town where the Count was supposed to receive his mail, was quite well-known. I am writing down my notes here so I can refresh my memory when I tell Mina about my travels later.

Four distinct groups of people live in Transylvania. In the south are the Saxons, mixed with the Wallachians, who are descendants of the ancient Dacians. The Magyars from Hungary live in the west, and the Szekelys live in the east and north. I am heading straight into Szekely territory. They firmly believe they are the descendants of Attila and the Huns. This might actually be true, since the Huns were already there when the Magyars conquered the region in the 11th century.

I once read that all the superstitions in the world are gathered in the horseshoe-shaped region of the Carpathian Mountains. It is as if this place is the center of a giant whirlpool of imagination. If that is true, my stay here should be quite interesting. (Memo: I must ask the Count about all these superstitions.)

The bed was comfortable, but I couldn't sleep because of strange dreams. It might have been because a dog was barking under my window all night. Or maybe it was that paprika dish. I drank an entire pitcher of water, but my throat still felt like it was burning. I finally fell asleep around dawn, only to be startled awake by someone banging on my door. I must have fallen into a deep sleep. I had paprika again for breakfast. I also had a cornmeal porridge called "Mamaliga" and a dish of stuffed eggplant with minced meat called "Impletata." It was absolutely delicious.

(Memo: Don't forget to get this recipe too.) Since the train was scheduled to leave a little before 8 o'clock, I rushed through my breakfast. I ran to the station at 7:30, but to my surprise, I had to sit in the carriage for over an hour before the train finally started moving. It seems that the further east you go, the less reliable the train schedules become. At this rate, I can't even imagine how late the trains must be in China!

We traveled slowly all day through picture-perfect countryside. Whenever I saw old castles and small villages perched on steep hills, they reminded me of illustrations from old religious books. Sometimes we ran alongside a wide river, and the large piles of round stones spread out on both sides clearly showed the history of frequent floods. It would take a massive amount of fast-flowing water to sweep the riverbanks so clean. At every station where the train stopped, crowds of people in all sorts of clothing were gathered.

Some people wore short jackets, round hats, and clumsily patched pants, much like the peasants I used to see back home in England, France, or Germany. On the other hand, there were quite a few people who looked as exotic as a painting. From a distance, the women looked quite pretty, but up close, their waists looked very thick. They wore wide white sleeves and large belts with fluttering pieces of cloth, which made them look like ballet dancers. Of course, under all that, they were wearing layers of petticoats. But the most bizarre outfits belonged to the Slovaks.

They looked much rougher than the others. They wore large cowboy hats, baggy white pants that looked quite dirty, and heavy leather belts that were nearly a foot wide and studded with brass. They tucked their pant legs into heavy boots that reached their knees, showing off long black hair and thick black mustaches. They certainly stood out, but their appearance was too intimidating to approach easily. If they were on stage, they would perfectly fit the role of ancient eastern bandits. However, I was told that despite their looks, they are actually quite gentle and lack assertiveness.
