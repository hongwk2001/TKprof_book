[P001] Chapter 18

[P002] Dr. Seward's Diary

[P003] September 30.—I arrived home at five in the afternoon. I found that Godalming and Morris had not only arrived but had already finished reading and studying the transcript of the various diaries and letters organized by Mr. Harker and his excellent wife. Mr. Harker had not yet returned from his meeting with the carriers, about whom Dr. Hennessey had written to me. Mrs. Harker offered us a cup of tea, and I can honestly say that for the first time since I moved into this old house, it truly felt like a home. Once we finished our tea, Mrs. Harker spoke up.

[P004] "Dr. Seward, may I ask you a favor? I would like to see your patient, Mr. Renfield. Please let me meet him. Everything you have written about him in your diary has caught my interest immensely!" She asked with such an earnest and beautiful expression that I could not possibly refuse, nor was there any good reason to do so. I took her with me. When I entered the room and told the man that a lady wished to see him, he simply replied, "Why?"

[P005] "She is touring the house and wants to meet everyone in it," I answered. "Oh, I see," he said. "Then by all means, let her come in. But please wait a minute until I tidy up the place." His way of tidying up was incredibly bizarre. Before I could stop him, he swallowed all the flies and spiders in his boxes. It was very clear that he was either afraid of some interference or extremely guarded. Only after finishing his repulsive task did he say in a bright voice, "Let the lady come in." He then sat on the edge of the bed with his head down, but kept his eyelids raised so he could watch her enter. For a moment, I tensed up, thinking he might harbor murderous intentions. I remembered how quiet he had been right before he attacked me in my own study. I positioned myself carefully so I could immediately subdue him if he tried to lunge at her. The lady entered the room with a natural grace that would instantly command the respect of any lunatic. People with mental illness tend to respect a comfortable, natural demeanor above all else. She smiled kindly, approached him, and offered her hand.

[P006] "Good evening, Mr. Renfield," she said. "As you can see, I know who you are. Dr. Seward has told me about you." He didn't answer immediately; instead, he stared her up and down with a deep frown. Soon, his expression shifted to surprise, which then mixed with suspicion. He then uttered something that completely astonished me.

[P007] "You're not the lady the doctor wanted to marry, are you? You couldn't be. She is dead." Mrs. Harker smiled warmly and replied.

[P008] "Oh, no! I already have a husband whom I married before I ever met Dr. Seward. I am Mrs. Harker."

[P009] "Then what are you doing here?"

[P010] "My husband and I are staying as guests at Dr. Seward's house."

[P011] "Then you shouldn't stay."

[P012] "But why not?" I felt that this conversation was becoming unpleasant not only for me but also for Mrs. Harker, so I intervened.

[P013] "How did you know I wanted to marry anyone?" He paused, shifting his gaze from Mrs. Harker to me, then immediately back to her, and gave a purely contemptuous reply.

[P014] "What an incredibly foolish question!"

[P015] "I don't think so at all, Mr. Renfield," Mrs. Harker said, immediately jumping to my defense. He responded to her with as much polite courtesy as the contempt he had just shown me.

[P016] "Mrs. Harker, you will surely understand that when a host is as loved and respected as ours, everything about him becomes a matter of great interest to those of us in this small community. Dr. Seward is loved by his family, friends, and even his patients. However, because some of these patients lack complete mental balance, they tend to distort cause and effect. Having lived as an inmate in this asylum, I cannot help but notice that the sophistical tendencies of some patients lean toward the logical fallacies of 'false cause' or 'missing the point.'" I opened my eyes wide at this new development. My own special patient, the most severe case of his kind I had ever encountered, was discussing basic philosophy with the manners of a cultured gentleman. I wondered if Mrs. Harker's presence had triggered some emotion in his memory. If this sudden change was spontaneous or somehow due to her unconscious influence, she undoubtedly possessed a very special gift or power.

[P017] We continued to converse for a while. Seeing that he appeared quite rational, she carefully broached his favorite topic, casting a questioning glance at me as she began. I was astonished once again, for he discussed the subject with the complete objectivity of a perfectly sane person. He even used himself as an example when explaining certain concepts.

[P018] "You see, I myself am a prime example of a man consumed by an eccentric belief. It is no wonder my friends were horrified and insisted on putting me under supervision. I used to operate under the delusion that life is a dynamic and perpetual force, and that by consuming a large number of living creatures—no matter how low they rank in the hierarchy of creation—one could extend their life indefinitely. Sometimes, this belief grew so powerful that I actually attempted to take human lives. The doctor here can attest that on one occasion, I tried to kill him. My goal was to strengthen my own vitality by absorbing his life through his blood. I was, of course, taking the biblical phrase 'the blood is the life' quite literally. Although, I must admit, a certain peddler of fraudulent elixirs has cheapened that profound truth to a despicable degree. Isn't that right, Doctor?" I was so shocked that I barely knew what to think or say, and merely nodded in agreement. It was impossible to believe that just five minutes ago, I had watched him gobble down flies and spiders. Glancing at my watch, I realized it was time to meet Professor Van Helsing at the train station. I informed Mrs. Harker that we needed to go. She promptly followed, saying kindly to Mr. Renfield, "Goodbye. I hope the next time we meet, it will be under much more pleasant circumstances for you." To my absolute surprise, he replied:

[P019] "Goodbye, Madam. I will pray to God that I never see your beautiful face again. May God bless and protect you!"

[P020] When I headed to the station to meet Van Helsing, I left the younger men at the house. Poor Arthur seemed more upbeat than he had been since Lucy first fell ill, and Quincey finally looked like his old, cheerful self after a very long time.

[P021] Van Helsing stepped out of the carriage with the energetic agility of a young boy. As soon as he spotted me, he hurried over and said:

[P022] "Ah, my friend John. How is everything? All well? Excellent! I have been very busy, as I intend to stay here if necessary. I have tied up all my affairs back home, and I have much to tell you. Is Madam Mina with you? Good. And her excellent husband? Are Arthur and my friend Quincey here as well? Perfect!"

[P023] On the carriage ride back to the house, I filled him in on what had happened and explained how my diary had proven useful thanks to Mrs. Harker's suggestion. The Professor interrupted me:

[P024] "Ah, the incredible Madam Mina! She possesses the brilliant mind of a highly gifted man, combined with the warm heart of a woman. Believe me, the good Lord had a purpose when He created such a wonderful combination. Friend John, fate has favored us so far by allowing her to help us. However, after tonight, she must have absolutely nothing to do with this horrific ordeal. It is too dangerous for her to take such risks. We men have sworn to destroy this monster, but it is no place for a woman. Even if she remains physically unharmed, witnessing so much terror could break her spirit. She might suffer from nervous exhaustion during the day and terrible nightmares at night. Besides, she is a young woman, newly married. She will have other things to focus on in her future, even if not right now. You mentioned she compiled all the records, so we must consult with her. But starting tomorrow, she will step away from this work, and we will proceed alone." I wholeheartedly agreed with him. I then told him what we had discovered while he was away: the house Dracula had purchased was right next door to mine. He was completely stunned and looked deeply troubled. "Oh, if only we had known that sooner!" he lamented. "We could have reached him in time to save poor Lucy. Still, as your saying goes, there is no use crying over spilled milk. We must stop dwelling on the past and see our mission through to the end." He then fell completely silent until we pulled up to my front gate. Before we went to prepare for dinner, he addressed Mrs. Harker:

[P025] "Madam Mina, my friend John tells me that you and your husband have perfectly organized a chronological record of everything that has happened up to this point."

[P026] "Not up to this point, Professor," she replied spontaneously, "but up to this morning."

[P027] "But why not until now? We have seen how even the smallest details can provide valuable clues. We have shared our deepest secrets, and no one has suffered because of it."

[P028] Mrs. Harker blushed slightly, pulled a piece of paper from her pocket, and said:

[P029] "Dr. Van Helsing, would you mind reading this and telling me if it should be included? It is my journal entry for today. I also recognize the need to record everything right now, no matter how trivial. But this entry contains little more than my personal feelings. Is it necessary to include it?" The Professor read it with a serious expression and handed it back, saying:

[P030] "You do not have to include it if you would prefer not to. However, I strongly suggest that you do. It will only make your husband love you more, and it will cause all of us, your friends, to respect and cherish you even more deeply." She blushed again, smiled brightly, and took the paper back.

[P031] And so, our records are completely up to date and perfectly organized as of this very hour. After dinner, the Professor took a copy to study before our meeting, which is scheduled for nine o'clock. The rest of us have already read through everything. When we gather in the study, we will all be fully informed of the facts and ready to strategize our battle against this terrifying and mysterious enemy.

[P032] Mina Harker's Journal.

[P033] September 30.—When we gathered in Dr. Seward's study two hours after our six o'clock dinner, we unintentionally arranged ourselves like a board or committee. Dr. Seward gestured for Professor Van Helsing to take the head of the table as he entered the room. The Professor had me sit on his right and asked me to act as the secretary. Jonathan sat next to me. Across from us were Lord Godalming, Dr. Seward, and Mr. Morris. Lord Godalming sat next to the Professor, with Dr. Seward in the middle. The Professor began:

[P034] "I assume I can take it for granted that we are all familiar with the facts detailed in these documents." We all nodded in agreement, and he continued:

[P035] "In that case, I believe it would be wise to first discuss the nature of the enemy we are facing. Then, I will share some information regarding this man's history, which I have managed to uncover. Once we have done that, we can discuss our course of action and make the necessary preparations.

[P036] "Vampires are real, and some of us have proof of their existence. Even if we did not have the tragic evidence of our own experiences, the teachings and historical records of the past provide more than enough proof for any rational mind. I admit that I was initially a skeptic. Had I not spent many years training myself to keep an open mind, I would never have believed it until the facts were thundering in my ears, crying, 'Look! Look! I am the proof!' Alas, if only I had known from the beginning what I know now—or even suspected him—we might have spared the precious life of the woman we all loved so dearly. But she is gone. Now, we must work quickly so that no more innocent souls perish while we still have the power to save them. The 'nosferatu' (vampire) does not die after striking once, like a bee. Instead, he grows stronger, and with that strength comes an even greater capacity for evil. The vampire hiding among us possesses the physical strength of twenty men. His cunning far exceeds human intelligence, as it has been honed over centuries. Furthermore, he has access to the dark arts of necromancy—which, as the word implies, is the magic of communicating with the dead—and he can command any deceased spirits within his reach. He is a beast, and worse than a beast. He is a heartless devil. He can, within certain limits, appear whenever and wherever he wants, taking whatever form he chooses. Within his territory, he can control the elements: storms, fog, and thunder. He can command the lower creatures: rats, owls, bats, moths, foxes, and wolves. He can alter his size, growing larger or shrinking, and can vanish or appear without a sound. So, how do we launch our first strike to destroy him? How will we locate his lair, and once we find it, how do we eliminate him? My friends, this is a monumental challenge. The task we are undertaking is terrifying, and the consequences may cause even the bravest among us to shudder. If we fail in this fight, he will undoubtedly win, and where will that leave us? I do not care about losing my own life. But failing here is not simply a matter of life and death. It means that we would become exactly like him—foul creatures of the night, devoid of heart and conscience, preying on the bodies and souls of the people we love most. The gates of heaven would be closed to us forever, for who would ever open them for us again? We would endure for eternity as abominations, a stain upon God's sunlight, an arrow in the side of Him who died for humanity. We are now face to face with our duty. Should we shrink away from it? Speaking for myself, I say absolutely not. But I am an old man. The joys of life—the sunshine, beautiful places, the singing of birds, music, and love—are far behind me. The rest of you are young. Some of you have known deep sorrow, but you still have many beautiful days ahead of you. What do you say?"

[P037] While the Professor was speaking, Jonathan took my hand. When I saw him reach out, I was terrified that the horrific nature of our situation was overwhelming him. But feeling his touch—so strong, confident, and resolute—was a lifeline to me. A brave man's hand speaks for itself; its comforting music can be felt deeply, even without a woman's love.

[P038] When the Professor finished speaking, my husband looked into my eyes, and I looked into his. We didn't need to exchange a single word.

[P039] "I answer for both Mina and myself," he stated.

[P040] "Count me in, Professor," said Mr. Quincey Morris, as concise as ever.

[P041] "I am with you," said Lord Godalming. "For Lucy's sake, if for no other reason."

[P042] Dr. Seward simply nodded. The Professor stood up, placed his golden crucifix on the table, and extended his hands to either side. I took his right hand, and Lord Godalming took his left. Jonathan held my right hand with his left and reached across to take Mr. Morris's hand. As we all joined hands, our solemn pact was forged. My heart felt as cold as ice, but the thought of backing down never even crossed my mind. We returned to our seats, and Dr. Van Helsing resumed speaking with a slightly more cheerful demeanor, signaling that the serious work had begun. This mission had to be approached with the same gravity and businesslike efficiency as any other major undertaking in life.

[P043] "Excellent. You all know what we are up against. However, we are not without our own strengths. We possess the power of unity—a power completely denied to vampires. We have the resources of science, and we are free to act and think as we choose. The hours of both day and night are equally ours to utilize. Essentially, within the scope of our human abilities, we are unrestricted and free to use them. We have self-sacrifice for a noble cause, and our goal is not a selfish one. These advantages are significant.

[P044] "Now, let us examine the limitations of the forces aligned against us, both the general weaknesses of vampires and the specific vulnerabilities of this one.

[P045] "Our only sources of information are myths and superstitions. In a matter of life and death—or worse—these might initially seem insignificant. Yet, we must rely on them. First, because we have no other choice. Second, because ultimately, these myths and superstitions are the foundation of everything. Isn't the belief in vampires held by others based entirely on these legends? Even a year ago, which of us would have entertained such a possibility in our scientific, skeptical, and fiercely logical nineteenth century? We refused to believe even what we witnessed with our own eyes. Therefore, we must accept that the existence of the vampire, its limitations, and its cures all rest on this same foundation. Let me tell you, this monster is known everywhere humanity has existed. He was in ancient Greece and ancient Rome. He thrived throughout Germany, France, India, and even the Chersonese (the Malay Peninsula). He is present even in China, a land so distant from us in every way, and the people there fear him to this day. He has followed in the footsteps of the berserker Icelanders, the Huns (believed to be born of demons), the Slavs, the Saxons, and the Magyars. This history is all we have to guide us. And I can assure you, many of these ancient beliefs have been validated by our own tragic experiences. The vampire lives on and does not die simply from the passage of time. He thrives as long as he can gorge himself on the blood of the living. Furthermore, we have seen firsthand that he can actually grow younger. His vitality surges and he seems to renew himself when his preferred food is abundant. But he cannot survive without blood; he eats no normal food. Our friend Jonathan, who lived with him for weeks, never once saw him eat. He casts no shadow and has no reflection in a mirror, as Jonathan also noted. He possesses the physical strength of many men, which Jonathan witnessed when the Count forced the doors shut against the wolves and assisted with the carriage. He can transform into a wolf, as we deduced from the ship's arrival in Whitby when the dog's throat was torn open. He can become a bat, as Madam Mina saw at the window in Whitby, as friend John saw flying from the house next door, and as my friend Quincey saw at Miss Lucy's window. He can travel within a mist that he conjures—the noble ship's captain proved this. However, to our knowledge, the distance he can project this mist is limited, and it only forms immediately around him. He can travel on moonlight as elemental dust, just as Jonathan saw those three sisters do in Dracula's castle. He can shrink himself down to a tiny size; we saw Miss Lucy slip through a hair-thin crack in the tomb door before she found peace. Once he finds a path, he can enter or exit any space, no matter how tightly sealed or soldered shut with fire. He can see in the dark, which is a massive advantage in a world that is half-deprived of light. But please, listen closely. He can do all of these things, yet he is not free. In fact, he is more of a prisoner than a galley slave or a madman in a cell. He cannot simply go wherever he pleases. Despite being an unnatural creature, he is still bound by certain laws of nature—though we do not fully understand why. He cannot enter any house for the first time unless someone inside explicitly invites him in, though once invited, he can enter at will thereafter. Like all evil creatures, his powers vanish when daylight arrives. He only has limited freedom at specific times. If he is not in his designated sanctuary, he can only change his form exactly at noon, sunrise, or sunset. We have heard these legends, and our own records support them through logical deduction. While he can do as he pleases within his limits when he is in his unholy sanctuaries—his earth-boxes, his coffins, or a desecrated place like the suicide's grave in Whitby—outside of those places, he can only change form at those specific times. It is also said that he can only cross running water at slack tide or flood tide. Moreover, certain things repel and paralyze him, such as the garlic we are familiar with. As for sacred objects, like the crucifix we used when making our vow, they strip him of all power, forcing him to stand far away in silent reverence. There are other deterrents as well, which I will share in case we need them during our hunt. Placing a branch of wild rose on his coffin traps him inside. Firing a blessed bullet into his coffin will grant him true death. We already know the peace brought by driving a stake through the heart or severing the head. We have witnessed it with our own eyes.

[P046] "Thus, once we locate this monster's lair, we can confine him to his coffin and destroy him using the knowledge we have gathered. But he is exceptionally cunning. I asked my friend Arminius at the University of Budapest to investigate, and using every available resource, he uncovered the truth about this man's past. He must be the infamous Voivode Dracula, who earned his fearsome reputation fighting the Turks across the great river on the borders of Turkey. If this is true, he was no ordinary man. During his time and for centuries after, he was known as the bravest, smartest, and most cunning son of the 'land beyond the forest' (Transylvania). That brilliant mind and iron will followed him into the grave and are now deployed against us. According to Arminius, the Draculas were a great and noble bloodline, though occasionally some descendants were suspected by their contemporaries of making deals with the Devil. They supposedly learned dark secrets at the Scholomance, a mythical school of black magic in the mountains above Lake Hermanstadt, where the Devil claimed every tenth student as his payment. The historical records contain words like 'stregoica' (witch), 'ordog', and 'pokol' (Satan and hell). In one specific manuscript, this very Dracula is referred to as a 'wampyr'—a term we understand all too well. This same bloodline has produced great men and noble women, and their graves have made the earth sacred, providing the only soil where this foul creature can find rest. It is a terrifying irony that this pure evil is deeply rooted in goodness. He cannot rest in barren soil devoid of holy memories."

[P047] While they were talking, Mr. Morris had been staring intently out the window. He quietly stood up and left the room. After a brief pause, the Professor continued:

[P048] "Now, we must decide on our next move. We have gathered a great deal of data, and we must begin laying out our strategy. Thanks to Jonathan's investigation, we know that fifty boxes of earth were shipped from the castle to Whitby and delivered to Carfax. We also know that at least some of those boxes have been moved. I believe our first step should be to determine if the remaining boxes are still in the house beyond the wall we looked at today, or if more have been transported elsewhere. If they have been moved, we must track them down—"

[P049] We were suddenly interrupted in a very startling manner. A pistol shot rang out outside the house, and a bullet shattered the window glass, ricocheted off the top of the frame, and struck the far wall of the room. I must be a coward at heart, because I screamed. The men all leaped to their feet. Lord Godalming rushed to the window and threw it open. As he did, we heard Mr. Morris's voice from outside:

[P050] "I am so sorry! I fear I have alarmed you all. I will come inside and explain." A minute later, he walked in and said:

[P051] "That was incredibly foolish of me. I sincerely beg your pardon, Mrs. Harker; I must have given you a terrible fright. The truth is, while the Professor was speaking, a massive bat flew up and perched on the windowsill. The recent events have left me with such a paralyzing horror of those damned beasts that I simply cannot tolerate them. Lately, whenever I see one in the evenings, I go out and take a shot at it. You used to laugh at me for doing it, Art."

[P052] "Did you hit it?" asked Dr. Van Helsing.

[P053] "I am not sure; I doubt it, as it flew off into the woods." Without another word, he sat back down, and the Professor resumed his explanation:

[P054] "We must track down each of these boxes. Once we are prepared, we must either capture or destroy this monster in his lair. Alternatively, we must 'sterilize' the earth, so to speak, preventing him from ever seeking refuge in it again. If we do this, we may eventually corner him in his human form between noon and sunset, engaging him when he is at his weakest.

[P055] "And now, Madam Mina, this night marks the end of your involvement until all is resolved. You are far too precious to us to be exposed to such danger. When we part tonight, you must ask no more questions. We will tell you everything when the time is right. We are men and can bear this burden, but you must remain our guiding star and our hope. We will be much freer to act knowing that you are not in the line of danger with us."

[P056] All the men, even Jonathan, looked visibly relieved. However, it did not sit well with me that they would risk their lives and potentially compromise their safety—for physical strength is the best safety—just to protect me. But their minds were made up. Although it was a bitter pill to swallow, I had no choice but to remain silent and accept their chivalrous protection.

[P057] Mr. Morris steered the conversation back to the task at hand:

[P058] "Since we have no time to lose, I vote we go investigate his house immediately. Time is everything for him, and swift action on our part could save another victim."

[P059] I must admit, my heart sank as the time for action drew near, but I kept my fears to myself. I was terrified that if I appeared to be a burden or an obstacle, they might exclude me from their future planning entirely. They have now left for Carfax, carrying the tools needed to break into the house.

[P060] Typical of men, they told me to go to bed and get some rest. As if any woman could sleep while the people she loves are in mortal danger! I will lie down and pretend to sleep so that Jonathan does not have to worry about me when he returns.

[P061] Dr. Seward's Diary.

[P062] October 1, 4 a.m.—Just as we were preparing to leave the house, an urgent message arrived from Renfield. He wanted to know if I could see him immediately, claiming he had something of the utmost importance to tell me. I told the messenger I was busy and would address his needs in the morning. The orderly added:

[P063] "He is being extremely persistent, sir. I have never seen him so desperate. I honestly believe that if you don't see him soon, he will have one of his violent fits." I knew the orderly wouldn't make such a claim without good reason, so I agreed. "All right, I'll go now," I said. I asked the others to wait a few minutes while I checked on my "patient."

[P064] "Take me with you, friend John," the Professor requested. "The accounts of his case in your diary fascinated me, and they occasionally seemed relevant to our own situation. I would very much like to meet him, especially when his mind is agitated."

[P065] "May I come as well?" Lord Godalming asked.

[P066] "Me too?" Quincey Morris chimed in. "May I come?" Harker asked. I nodded, and the four of us walked down the hall together.

[P067] We found Renfield highly agitated, yet his speech and demeanor were far more rational than I had ever witnessed. He displayed an extraordinary level of self-awareness, something I had never seen in a psychiatric patient. He fully expected his arguments to convince a group of perfectly sane men. All four of us entered the room, but the others remained silent at first. His demand was that I discharge him from the asylum immediately and send him home. He supported this request with arguments claiming a complete recovery and offered his current rational behavior as proof. "I appeal to your friends," he said. "Perhaps they wouldn't mind judging my case. By the way, you haven't introduced us." I was so taken aback that the sheer absurdity of introducing a lunatic in an asylum didn't immediately register. Besides, there was a certain dignity to his manner—an air of equality—that prompted me to make the introductions right away. "Lord Godalming, Professor Van Helsing, Mr. Quincey Morris of Texas; this is Mr. Renfield." He shook hands with each of them, addressing them in turn:

[P068] "Lord Godalming, I had the honor of seconding your father at the Windham Club. Seeing that you now hold the title, I am deeply saddened to learn that he has passed. He was loved and respected by everyone who knew him. I heard that in his youth, he invented a flaming rum punch that was incredibly popular on Derby night. Mr. Morris, you should be immensely proud of your great state. Texas's admission into the Union set a precedent that could have far-reaching consequences in the future, perhaps when the North Pole and the Tropics eventually align with the Stars and Stripes. The power of treaties may become a massive engine for expansion when the Monroe Doctrine finally claims its rightful place as a political fable. And what can one say about the pleasure of meeting Van Helsing? Sir, I will make no apologies for dropping formal titles. When an individual has revolutionized therapeutics by discovering the continuous evolution of brain matter, conventional titles seem inadequate, as they would restrict you to a single category. Gentlemen, you who have secured your respective places in this dynamic world—whether through nationality, heritage, or natural talent—I call upon you to bear witness that I am as sane as the majority of men who freely walk the earth. And I am confident that you, Dr. Seward—a humanitarian, forensic psychiatrist, and scientist—will consider it your moral duty to treat me as someone caught in exceptional circumstances." He delivered this final plea with a dignified, convincing grace that possessed a charm all its own.

[P069] We were all completely staggered. Personally, despite my deep knowledge of this man's character and history, I was convinced that his sanity had been restored. I felt a powerful urge to tell him right then that I believed he was sane and that I would process his release papers in the morning. However, I thought it best to wait before making such a serious commitment, knowing how prone this particular patient was to sudden behavioral shifts. So, I settled for a generic response, telling him that he seemed to be improving rapidly and that we would have a longer chat in the morning to see how I could accommodate his wishes. This did not satisfy him in the least. He replied urgently:

[P070] "But Dr. Seward, I fear you are missing my point entirely. I wish to leave right now—from this place—at this very hour—this exact moment, if you will allow it. Time is running out, and time is the most critical element in our unspoken contract with the grim reaper. I am confident that simply presenting such a straightforward yet momentous request to a practitioner as excellent as yourself will guarantee its fulfillment." He stared at me intently, and reading the refusal on my face, he turned and scrutinized the others. Finding no support, he continued:

[P071] "Is it possible that I have misjudged the situation?"

[P072] "You have," I said frankly, though it felt slightly brutal at the moment. After a long pause, he spoke slowly:

[P073] "Then I suppose I must change the basis of my request. Allow me to ask for this favor—boon, privilege, call it what you will. In this case, I am willing to beg, not for my own sake, but for the sake of others. I cannot reveal my reasons to you in full. However, I assure you, you can trust that my motives are valid, entirely selfless, and stem from the deepest sense of duty. If you could look into my heart, sir, you would wholeheartedly agree with the sentiments driving me. Furthermore, you would consider me one of your truest and best friends." He scanned all our faces again. I grew increasingly convinced that this sudden shift to a highly intellectual approach was simply another manifestation of his madness. Knowing from experience that all lunatics eventually expose their true nature, I decided to let him talk a little longer. Van Helsing was staring at him with ferocious intensity, his bushy eyebrows furrowed in deep concentration. He spoke to Renfield in a tone that surprised me only in hindsight, for he addressed him as an equal:

[P074] "Can you not be completely honest about your real reason for needing freedom tonight? I promise you this: if you can satisfy me—an unbiased stranger with an open mind—Dr. Seward will grant you the privilege you seek, at his own risk and on his own responsibility." Renfield shook his head sadly, a look of profound regret washing over his face. The Professor continued:

[P075] "Come now, think about it. You are claiming the highest privilege of reason by trying to impress us with your utter rationality. Yet you do this while we have good cause to doubt your sanity, as you are still undergoing medical treatment for this exact condition. If you refuse to help us choose the wisest course of action, how can we fulfill the duty you are placing upon us? Be smart and help us; if you do, we will do everything in our power to grant your wish." Renfield continued to shake his head and replied:

[P076] "Dr. Van Helsing, I have nothing more to say. Your logic is flawless, and if I were at liberty to speak, I wouldn't hesitate for a second. But I am not my own master in this matter. I can only beg you to trust me. If my request is denied, the responsibility does not fall on my shoulders." I decided it was time to end the scene, which was becoming absurdly melodramatic. I moved toward the door and said simply:

[P077] "Come along, friends, we have work to do. Good night."

[P078] However, as I neared the door, the patient underwent a sudden transformation. He lunged toward me so quickly that for a split second, I feared another violent attack. My fears were unfounded; he threw his hands up pleadingly and made a moving, impassioned plea. But when he realized that this extreme emotional display was actually working against him—pushing us back into our traditional doctor-patient roles—he became even more frantic. I glanced at Van Helsing and saw my own skepticism mirrored in his eyes. I stiffened my posture, acting sterner, and signaled to Renfield that his efforts were useless. I had witnessed this exact pattern of escalating excitement before, like when he had obsessively requested a cat. I was fully expecting him to collapse back into his usual sullen resentment. But I was wrong. Realizing his appeals were failing, he fell into an absolute frenzy. He dropped to his knees, wrung his hands in desperate supplication, and unleashed a torrent of begging, tears streaming down his face as his entire body radiated profound despair:

[P079] "I am begging you, Dr. Seward! Oh, please, I implore you, let me out of this house immediately! Send me away however you want, wherever you want. Send armed guards with whips and chains; strap me in a straitjacket, lock me in handcuffs and leg irons, throw me in jail—just get me out of here! You have no idea what you are doing by keeping me locked up. I am speaking from the very bottom of my heart, from the depths of my soul! You don't know who you are wronging, or how! And I cannot tell you. Woe is me, I cannot say! By everything you hold sacred—by everything you hold dear—by the love you have lost—by the hope you still cling to—for the love of God, get me out of here and save my soul from this guilt! Are you deaf, man? Do you not understand? Will you never learn? Can't you see that I am sane and deadly serious right now? That I am not a lunatic having a fit, but a sane man fighting for his soul? Oh, listen to me! Please, listen! Let me go! Let me go! Let me go!"

[P080] I figured the longer this went on, the wilder he would become until he triggered a full-blown fit. So, I grabbed his hand and hauled him to his feet.

[P081] "That is enough," I said sternly. "No more of this nonsense; we have had quite enough. Get back into bed and try to behave yourself."

[P082] He stopped instantly and stared at me intensely for a few moments. Then, without a single word, he stood up, walked over, and sat on the edge of his bed. The emotional collapse had hit, just as I had expected from past experiences.

[P083] I was the last of our group to leave the room. As I stepped through the door, he said to me in a quiet, cultured voice:

[P084] "I trust, Dr. Seward, that in the future, you will do me the justice of remembering that I did everything in my power to convince you tonight."
