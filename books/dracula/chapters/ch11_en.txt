[P001] CHAPTER XI

[P002] Lucy Westenra’s Diary.

[P003a] September 12.—Everyone is so good to me. I truly love that dear Dr. Van Helsing. I wonder why he was so worried about these garlic flowers. He honestly frightened me because he was so intense about them. But he must have been right, as I am already feeling comforted by them. Somehow, I am not afraid to be alone tonight, and I can go to sleep without fear. I won't mind any flapping sounds outside my window. Oh, I have struggled so terribly against sleep lately!

[P003b] The pain of sleeplessness, the fear of falling asleep, and the unknown horrors it brings me have been awful. How blessed are those whose lives are free from fear and dread, to whom sleep is a nightly blessing that brings only sweet dreams. Well, here I am tonight, hoping for sleep, lying like Ophelia in the play, adorned with virgin wreaths and scattered flowers. I never liked garlic before, but tonight it smells delightful! There is a sense of peace in its scent, and I can feel sleep approaching already. Good night, everyone.

[P004] Dr. Seward’s Diary.

[P005] September 13.—I stopped by the Berkeley Hotel and found Van Helsing awake and ready on time, as usual. The carriage we had ordered from the hotel was waiting. The Professor brought his bag, which he carries with him everywhere now.

[P006] I must record everything exactly as it happened. Van Helsing and I arrived at Hillingham at eight o’clock. It was a beautiful morning; the bright sunshine and the crisp feel of early autumn felt like nature had completed its yearly work. The leaves were turning into all sorts of lovely colors, though they hadn't started falling from the trees yet. When we walked in, we met Mrs. Westenra coming out of the morning room. She is always an early riser. She welcomed us warmly and said:—

[P007] 'You will be glad to hear that Lucy is doing better. The sweet girl is still asleep. I peeked into her room and saw her, but I didn't go inside because I didn't want to wake her.' The Professor smiled and looked absolutely thrilled. He rubbed his hands together and said:—

[P008] 'Aha! I knew my diagnosis was correct. My treatment is working,' to which she replied:—

[P009] 'You shouldn't take all the credit yourself, Doctor. Lucy’s improvement this morning is partly thanks to me.'

[P010] 'What do you mean, ma’am?' asked the Professor.

[P011a] 'Well, I was worried about the sweet girl during the night, so I went into her room. She was sleeping so deeply that even my arrival didn't wake her. But the room was terribly stuffy. There were so many of those awful, strong-smelling flowers everywhere, and she even had a whole bunch of them wrapped around her neck. I was worried that the overpowering smell would be too much for her in her weakened state, so I took them all away and opened the window a little bit to let in some fresh air.

[P011b] I'm sure you will be pleased with how she looks.'

[P012] She walked off into her sitting room, where she usually had an early breakfast. As she spoke, I watched the Professor’s face turn completely pale. He managed to keep his composure while the poor woman was there, because he knew about her heart condition and how dangerous a shock could be; he even smiled at her as he held the door open for her to go into her room. But the moment she was out of sight, he grabbed me tightly, pulled me into the dining room, and shut the door.

[P013a] Then, for the first time in my life, I saw Van Helsing break down. He threw his hands over his head in silent despair and helplessly clapped his palms together. Finally, he collapsed into a chair, buried his face in his hands, and began to sob loudly and dryly, sounding as if his heart were tearing apart. Then he raised his arms again, as if pleading with the universe. 'God! God! God!' he cried.

[P013b] 'What have we done, what has this poor girl done, that we are being tortured like this? Is there some ancient curse still haunting us, forcing things to happen this way? This poor mother, completely unaware and thinking she was doing the right thing, has just done something that will cost her daughter both body and soul. And we can't tell her, we can't even warn her, because the shock would kill her, and then they would both die. Oh, we are so trapped! All the powers of the devil are working against us!' Suddenly, he jumped to his feet.

[P013c] 'Come,' he said, 'come, we must go see her and take action. Devil or no devil, or even all the devils at once, it doesn't matter; we will fight them anyway.' He marched to the front door to get his bag, and together we rushed up to Lucy’s room.

[P014] Once again, I pulled up the blinds while Van Helsing hurried to the bed. This time he didn't flinch when he saw her poor face, which was covered in the same terrifying, wax-like paleness as before. His expression was one of fierce sadness and boundless pity.

[P015] 'Just as I expected,' he whispered, with that sharp intake of breath that always meant so much. Without saying another word, he locked the door and began arranging the instruments on the small table for yet another blood transfusion. I had already realized what we needed to do and started taking off my coat, but he stopped me with a raised hand. 'No!' he said. 'You have to perform the operation today. I will provide the blood. You are already too weak.' As he spoke, he took off his coat and rolled up his shirt sleeve.

[P016] Once again, we performed the operation. We administered the anesthesia, her pale cheeks regained some color, and her breathing returned to a steady, healthy rhythm. This time I kept watch while Van Helsing rested and regained his strength.

[P017] Later, he found a moment to tell Mrs. Westenra that she must never remove anything from Lucy’s room without asking him first. He explained that the flowers were medicinal and that breathing their scent was part of her treatment. He then took full charge of the case, stating that he would stay awake to watch over her for the next two nights and would send for me when he needed me.

[P018] An hour later, Lucy woke up. She looked refreshed and bright, seeming largely unaffected by her terrifying ordeal.

[P019a] What does all of this mean? I am starting to wonder if my years of living among the insane are finally making me lose my own mind.

[P019b] Lucy Westenra’s Diary.

[P020_021] September 17.—I have had four peaceful days and nights. I am regaining my strength so quickly that I barely recognize myself. It feels like I have just woken up from a long, terrible nightmare to find bright sunshine and fresh morning air all around me. I have a vague, blurry memory of long, stressful periods of waiting and fearing. I remember a darkness where there wasn't even a glimmer of hope to make the suffering easier to bear. Then there were long stretches of complete nothingness, followed by surfacing back to life like a diver swimming up through heavy water.

[P021b] However, since Dr. Van Helsing has been here with me, all those bad dreams seem to have vanished. The noises that used to terrify me—the flapping against the windows, the distant voices that felt so close, the harsh, mysterious sounds commanding me to do things I couldn't understand—have all completely stopped. I go to bed now without any fear of falling asleep. I don't even try to stay awake anymore. I have actually grown to love the garlic, and a fresh box of it arrives for me from Haarlem every day.

[P021c] Tonight, Dr. Van Helsing is leaving to spend a day in Amsterdam. But I don't need anyone to watch over me; I am healthy enough to be left alone. Thank God, for my mother’s sake, for dear Arthur’s, and for all our friends who have been so incredibly kind! I won't even notice he's gone, because last night Dr. Van Helsing slept in his chair most of the time anyway.

[P021d] I caught him sleeping twice when I woke up, but I wasn't afraid to go back to sleep, even though something like branches or bats kept knocking angrily against the windowpanes.

[P022] "The Pall Mall Gazette," September 18.

[P023] THE ESCAPED WOLF.

[P024] OUR INTERVIEWER'S DANGEROUS ADVENTURE.

[P025] An Interview with the Zoo Keeper.

[P026a] After asking around endlessly and facing nearly as many rejections, and constantly using the name "Pall Mall Gazette" like a magic pass, I finally managed to track down the keeper of the zoo section that includes the wolves. Thomas Bilder lives in one of the small cottages in the area behind the elephant house, and he was just sitting down for his tea when I found him. Thomas and his wife are a warm, older couple without children. If the generous hospitality they showed me is normal for them, they must live a very comfortable life.

[P026b] The keeper refused to discuss what he called "business" until supper was completely finished and everyone was full. Once the table was cleared and he had lit his pipe, he said:—

[P027] "Alright, sir, you can go ahead and ask me whatever you want. I hope you'll excuse me for refusing to talk about work before eating. I always make sure the wolves, jackals, and hyenas in my section get their meals before I start asking them questions."

[P028] "What do you mean by asking them questions?" I asked, hoping to get him into a talkative mood.

[P029a] "Hitting them over the head with a pole is one way. Scratching their ears is another, like when rich gentlemen want to show off for their girlfriends. I don't really mind the first way—hitting them with a pole before I toss their dinner in. But I always wait until they've had their dinner—their sherry and coffee, so to speak—before I try scratching their ears. You know," he added philosophically, "we have a lot in common with those animals. Take you, for example.

[P029b] You came here asking questions about my work, and I was so grumpy that if you hadn't given me that half-sovereign gold coin, I would have told you to get lost before answering a single thing. Not even when you sarcastically asked if you needed the Superintendent's permission to ask me questions. No offense intended, but did I tell you to go to hell?"

[P029c] "You did."

[P030_031] "And when you said you'd report me for swearing, that was like hitting me over the head. But the gold coin smoothed everything over. I wasn't going to fight, so I just waited for my food and complained a bit, exactly like the wolves, lions, and tigers do. But goodness me, now that my wife has fed me a chunk of her tea-cake, washed it down with her lovely teapot, and I've lit my pipe, you can ask me whatever you want and I won't even growl. Go ahead with your questions.

[P031b] I know what you're here for: that escaped wolf."

[P032] "Exactly. I want to hear your perspective on it. Just tell me how it happened. Once I know the facts, I'll ask you what you think caused it and how you believe the whole situation will end."

[P033] "Alright, sir. Here is the whole story. The wolf we called Bersicker was one of three gray wolves that came from Norway to Jamrach's exotic animal shop. We bought him from them four years ago. He was a nice, well-behaved wolf who never caused any trouble worth mentioning. I am more surprised that he wanted to escape than I would be for any other animal here. But then again, you can't trust wolves any more than you can trust women."

[P034] "Don't mind him, sir!" interrupted Mrs. Bilder with a cheerful laugh. "He's been looking after animals for so long that he's turned into an old wolf himself! But he's harmless."

[P035a] "Well, sir, it was about two hours after feeding time yesterday when I first heard the commotion. I was preparing a bed in the monkey house for a sick young puma, but when I heard the yelping and howling, I went over immediately. Bersicker was tearing at the bars like a madman, as if he was desperate to get out. There weren't many people around that day, and there was only one man standing nearby. He was a tall, thin fellow with a hooked nose and a pointed beard with a few white hairs in it.

[P035b] He had a hard, cold expression and red eyes. I immediately took a dislike to him because it seemed like he was the one irritating the animals. He was wearing white kid leather gloves, and he pointed at the wolves and said: 'Keeper, these wolves seem upset about something.'"

[P036] "'Maybe it's you,' I said, because I didn't like his arrogant attitude. He didn't get angry like I hoped he would. Instead, he just gave an arrogant smile, showing off a mouth full of sharp, white teeth. 'Oh no, they wouldn't like me,' he said."

[P037] "'Oh yes, they would,' I said, mocking him. 'They always like a bone or two to clean their teeth on around dinnertime, and you're practically a bag of bones.'"

[P038a] "Well, the strangest thing happened. When the animals saw us talking, they all lay down. When I went over to Bersicker, he let me stroke his ears just like he always does. Then that man came over, and I'll be damned if he didn't reach his hand in and stroke the old wolf's ears too!"

[P038b] "'Be careful,' I said. 'Bersicker is fast.'"

[P039_040] "'Never mind,' he said. 'I'm used to them!'"

[P041] "'Are you in the animal business yourself?' I asked, taking off my hat. Anyone who trades in wolves and such is a good friend to us keepers."

[P042a] "'No,' he said, 'not exactly in the business, but I have kept several as pets.' With that, he tipped his hat as politely as a lord and walked away. Old Bersicker kept watching him until he was out of sight, and then he went and lay down in a corner and wouldn't come out for the rest of the evening. Well, last night, as soon as the moon came up, the wolves here all started howling. There was absolutely nothing for them to howl at.

[P042b] There was no one near, except for someone who was evidently calling a dog somewhere out behind the gardens on Park Road. I went out once or twice to check if everything was alright, and it was, and then the howling stopped. Right before twelve o'clock, I took one last look around before going to bed. Goodness me, when I came to old Bersicker's cage, I saw the bars broken and twisted, and the cage was empty. And that's all I know for sure."

[P043] "Did anyone else see anything?"

[P044] "One of our gardeners was coming home around that time from a concert, and he claims he saw a big gray dog coming out through the garden hedges. At least, that's what he says. I don't believe it much myself. If he really saw something, he never mentioned it to his wife when he got home. It was only after the wolf's escape became known and we'd been up all night hunting the park for Bersicker that he suddenly remembered seeing something. My guess is that the music from the concert went to his head."

[P045] "Now, Mr. Bilder, can you explain why the wolf escaped?"

[P046] "Well, sir," he said with a surprisingly modest tone, "I think I can, but I don't know if you'll be satisfied with my theory."

[P047] "I certainly will be. If a man like you, who knows animals from years of experience, can't make a good guess, who can?"

[P048] "Well then, sir, I explain it like this. It seems to me that the wolf escaped—simply because he wanted to get out."

[P049] From the hearty way that both Thomas and his wife laughed at the joke, I could tell it had been used before and that his entire long-winded explanation was just an elaborate setup. I couldn't compete with the good Thomas in bantering, but I thought I knew a better way to win him over, so I said:—

[P050] "Now, Mr. Bilder, let's consider that first half-sovereign fully earned. Once you tell me what you think will happen next, this other coin will be waiting for you."

[P051] "Right you are, sir," he said eagerly. "I know you'll excuse me for joking around, but my wife here winked at me, which was my cue to keep going."

[P052] "Well, I never!" the old lady exclaimed.

[P053a] "My opinion is this: that wolf is hiding out somewhere right now. That forgetful gardener claimed the wolf was galloping north faster than a horse could run, but I don't believe him. You see, sir, wolves don't gallop any more than dogs do; they just aren't built that way. Wolves are impressive in storybooks, and I admit that when they gather in packs and are chasing something that's more scared than they are, they can make a terrifying noise and tear it to pieces.

[P053b] But good Lord, in real life, a wolf is just a lowly animal, not half as smart or brave as a good dog, and with not even a quarter of the fight in him. This one has never had to fight or even find his own food. Most likely, he's hiding somewhere around the park right now, shivering. If he's thinking at all, he's probably wondering where his breakfast is going to come from, or maybe he crawled down into a basement and is hiding in a coal cellar.

[P053c] Goodness me, some cook is going to get a terrible shock when she sees his green eyes shining at her in the dark! If he can't find food, he'll have to go looking for it, and if he's lucky, he might stumble across a butcher shop early on. If he doesn't, and some nanny wanders off with a soldier, leaving a baby alone in a carriage—well, I wouldn't be surprised if the census count goes down by one baby. That's all there is to it."

[P054] I was just handing him the half-sovereign when something suddenly bumped against the window, and Mr. Bilder's face dropped in sheer astonishment.

[P055] "God bless me!" he cried. "If it isn't old Bersicker come back on his own!"

[P056] He went to the door and opened it—a move that seemed incredibly reckless to me. I've always felt that wild animals are best admired when there's a sturdy, reliable barrier between us, and personal experience has only strengthened that belief.

[P057] However, habit is a powerful thing. Neither Bilder nor his wife seemed any more afraid of the wolf than I would be of a dog. The animal itself was as calm and well-behaved as the grandfather of all storybook wolves—Red Riding Hood's old friend, right when he was winning her trust in disguise.

[P058] The whole scene was an indescribable mix of comedy and tragedy. The wicked wolf that had paralyzed London for half a day, terrifying every child in the city, was standing there looking almost apologetic. He was welcomed and petted like a prodigal son returning home. Old Bilder carefully examined his entire body with deep concern, and when he was done checking the remorseful wolf, he said:—

[P059] "There, I knew the poor old guy would get into some kind of trouble. Didn't I say it all along? Look at his head, it's all cut up and full of broken glass. He must have tried jumping over some bloody wall. It's a shame people are allowed to put broken bottles on top of their walls. This is what happens. Come along, Bersicker."

[P060] He led the wolf away, locked him in a cage, and threw him a piece of meat that—at least in size—rivaled the Biblical fatted calf. Then he went off to report the incident.

[P061] I also left to file my report, providing the only exclusive information available today about the strange escape at the Zoo.

[P062] Dr. Seward’s Diary.

[P063a] September 17.—After dinner, I was in my study, updating my records, which had fallen severely behind due to the pressure of other work and my frequent visits to Lucy. Suddenly, the door flew open, and my patient burst in, his face twisted in rage. I was completely shocked, because it is almost unheard of for a patient to voluntarily enter the Superintendent’s study. Without a moment's hesitation, he charged straight at me. He was holding a dinner knife, and seeing how dangerous he was, I tried to keep the desk between us. However, he was too fast and too strong.

[P063b] Before I could regain my balance, he lunged at me and slashed my left wrist quite badly. But before he could strike again, I hit him with my right hand, and he collapsed onto his back on the floor. My wrist was bleeding heavily, and a small pool of blood began to form on the carpet. Seeing that my attacker wasn't trying to get back up, I focused on bandaging my wrist while keeping a close eye on his body. When the guards rushed in and we turned our attention to him, what he was doing made me physically sick.

[P063c] He was lying on his stomach, licking up the blood that had dripped from my wrist like a dog. He was easily restrained, and to my surprise, he went with the guards very peacefully, simply repeating over and over: "The blood is the life! The blood is the life!"

[P064] I really can't afford to lose any blood right now. I've already lost too much lately for my own health, and the prolonged stress of Lucy’s illness and its terrifying phases is taking a toll on me. I am overstimulated and exhausted, and I desperately need rest. Fortunately, Van Helsing hasn't called for me, so I don't have to skip my sleep; I really couldn't do without it tonight.

[P067b] Dr. Seward’s Diary.

[P068_069] September 18.—I am just leaving to catch the train to London. Van Helsing’s telegram filled me with dread. An entire night was lost, and I know from bitter experience exactly what can happen in just one night. Of course, everything might be fine, but what could have happened? It truly feels like some horrible curse is hanging over us, ensuring that every possible accident ruins everything we try to do. I am bringing this phonograph cylinder with me so I can finish recording my entry on Lucy’s machine.

[P070] Memorandum left by Lucy Westenra.

[P071a] September 17. Night.—I am writing this down and leaving it to be found so that no one gets into trouble because of me. This is an exact record of what happened tonight. I feel like I am dying from exhaustion and I barely have the strength to write, but I must finish this even if it kills me.

[P071b] I went to bed as usual, making sure the flowers were placed exactly as Dr. Van Helsing had instructed, and I quickly fell asleep.

[P071c] I was woken up by a flapping sound at the window—the same sound that started after my sleepwalking incident on the cliff in Whitby when Mina saved me, a sound I now know all too well. I wasn't scared, but I wished Dr. Seward was in the next room, like Dr. Van Helsing promised he would be, so I could call him. I tried to go back to sleep, but I couldn't. Then my old fear of falling asleep returned, and I decided to stay awake. Naturally, sleep then tried to overcome me when I didn't want it to.

[P072_073] Feeling frightened to be alone, I opened my door and called out, "Is anybody there?" There was no reply. I was too scared to wake my mother, so I closed the door again. Then, from the bushes outside, I heard a sound like a dog howling, but much fiercer and deeper. I went to the window and looked out, but all I could see was a large bat throwing itself against the glass. I went back to bed, determined to stay awake. Soon after, the door opened, and my mother peeked in.

[P073b] Seeing me moving and realizing I was awake, she came in and sat beside me. She spoke to me even more gently and sweetly than usual:—

[P074] "I was worried about you, darling, so I came in to make sure you were alright."

[P075a] I was worried she might catch a cold sitting there, so I asked her to get into bed and sleep with me. She lay down next to me, still wearing her dressing gown, saying she would only stay for a little while before going back to her own bed. As we lay there holding each other, the flapping and thumping at the window started again. It startled her, and she cried out in fear, "What is that?" I tried to calm her down, and eventually, she lay quietly, but I could hear her poor heart beating frantically.

[P075b] A little while later, the low howl came from the bushes again, followed shortly by a loud crash at the window as shattered glass scattered across the floor. The wind blew the blind back, and through the broken panes, the head of a massive, skinny gray wolf appeared. My mother screamed in terror, struggled to sit up, and desperately grabbed at anything she could reach. During her panic, she grabbed the wreath of garlic flowers that Dr. Van Helsing had insisted I wear and ripped it from my neck.

[P075c] She sat there for a second or two, pointing at the wolf, making a strange, awful gurgling sound in her throat. Then she collapsed—as if she had been struck by lightning—and her head hit my forehead, making me dizzy for a moment. The room and everything in it seemed to spin. I kept my eyes locked on the window; the wolf pulled its head back, and a huge cloud of tiny specks blew in through the broken glass. They swirled and spun around like the massive dust storms travelers describe seeing in the desert.

[P075d] I tried to move, but I felt paralyzed, as if under a spell. My mother’s poor body, which already felt cold as her heart had stopped beating, pinned me down. After that, I blacked out for a while.

[P076a] It didn't feel like a long time, but it was absolutely terrifying when I finally regained consciousness. A funeral bell was tolling somewhere nearby, dogs were howling all over the neighborhood, and a nightingale was singing in our garden, sounding like it was right outside my window. I was confused and numb from the pain, fear, and weakness, but the nightingale's song felt like my dead mother's voice returning to comfort me. The noise must have woken the maids, because I could hear their bare feet running in the hall.

[P076b] I called out to them, and they came in. When they saw the shattered window and what was lying on top of me on the bed, they screamed. The wind howled through the broken window, slamming the door shut. The maids lifted my mother’s body off me, and after I got up, they laid her back down and covered her with a sheet.

[P076c] They were all so terrified and shaking that I told them to go to the dining room and have a glass of wine to calm their nerves. The door blew open for a second and slammed shut again. The maids shrieked and ran to the dining room together. I took the few flowers I had left and placed them on my mother’s chest. As I laid them there, I remembered what Dr. Van Helsing had told me, but I didn't want to move them. Besides, I planned to have some of the servants stay up and keep me company.

[P076d] I was surprised when the maids didn't return. I called for them but got no answer, so I went to the dining room to find them.

[P077a] My heart dropped when I saw what had happened. All four of them were lying unconscious on the floor, breathing heavily. The sherry decanter was on the table, half-empty, but there was a strange, bitter smell in the air. Feeling suspicious, I checked the decanter; it smelled like laudanum. Looking at the cabinet, I saw that the medicine bottle my mother's doctor used for her—oh, used to use!—was completely empty. What am I supposed to do? What can I do? I am back in the room with my mother.

[P077b] I can't leave her, and I am entirely alone, except for the drugged servants. Alone with the dead! I am too terrified to go outside because I can hear the wolf howling low through the broken window.

[P078] The air feels thick with specks, floating and swirling in the draft from the window, and the lights are burning with a dim, blue glow. What should I do? May God protect me tonight! I will hide this paper in my dress, so they will find it when they come to prepare my body. My dear mother is gone! It must be time for me to go too. Goodbye, dear Arthur, if I don't survive this night. May God protect you, my love, and may God help me!
