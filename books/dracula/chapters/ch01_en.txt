Chapter 1

Jonathan Harker's Journal

(Written in shorthand.)

May 3. Bistritz. I left Munich at 8:35 PM on May 1st and arrived in Vienna early the next morning. We should have arrived at 6:46 AM, but the train was delayed by an hour. Budapest seemed like a wonderful place based on the brief glimpse I got from the train and a short walk through the streets. I was afraid to go too far from the station because we arrived late and the train was expected to leave as close to its scheduled time as possible. My impression was that we were leaving the West and entering the East. The stunning, highly western-style bridges over the deep and wide Danube River brought us into a region shaped by the history of Turkish rule.

We departed reasonably on time and arrived in Klausenburg after dark. I stayed the night at the Hotel Royale. For dinner—or rather, supper—I had a chicken dish cooked with red peppers. It was delicious but made me very thirsty. (Note to self: Get the recipe for Mina.) When I asked the waiter, he told me it was called 'paprika hendl.' He mentioned that since it is a traditional national dish, I would be able to find it anywhere along the Carpathian Mountains. I found my basic German skills to be incredibly helpful here; in fact, I don't know how I would manage without them.

When I had some free time in London, I visited the British Museum and searched the library for books and maps about Transylvania. I figured that having some background knowledge about the country would be very useful when dealing with a nobleman from that region. I discovered that the district he mentioned is in the far east of the country, right at the intersection of Transylvania, Moldavia, and Bukovina. It is situated in the middle of the Carpathian Mountains, making it one of the most rugged and least explored parts of Europe. Since there are no detailed maps of this country that compare to our British Ordnance Survey maps, I couldn't find any specific map or document showing the exact location of Castle Dracula. However, I did find out that Bistritz, the post town Count Dracula mentioned, is fairly well known. I will record some of my notes here so they can help jog my memory when I tell Mina about my travels.

Transylvania's population consists of four distinct ethnic groups. The Saxons live in the South, and mixed among them are the Wallachs, who descend from the ancient Dacians. The Magyars (Hungarians) live in the West, while the Szekelys reside in the East and North. I am heading into the territory of the Szekelys, who claim to be the descendants of Attila the Hun and his people. This claim might be true, because when the Magyars conquered the region in the eleventh century, they found the Huns already settled there. I have read that every known superstition in the world is concentrated within the horseshoe-shaped curve of the Carpathian Mountains, as if the area is the center of a massive whirlpool of imagination. If that's the case, my stay here should be very interesting. (Note to self: I must ask the Count all about these superstitions.)

Even though the bed was comfortable, I didn't sleep well because I had a variety of strange dreams. A dog was howling under my window all night, which might have caused them. Or perhaps it was the paprika, as I drank my entire carafe of water and was still thirsty. I finally fell asleep toward morning but was woken up by continuous knocking at my door, which tells me I must have been in a deep sleep by then. For breakfast, I had more paprika, a type of cornmeal porridge they call 'mamaliga,' and a delicious dish of eggplant stuffed with minced meat, known as 'impletata.' (Note to self: Get the recipe for this as well.) I had to eat breakfast in a hurry because the train was scheduled to leave a little before eight. However, after rushing to the station at 7:30, I ended up sitting in the carriage for over an hour before we actually started moving. It seems that the further east you travel, the less punctual the trains become. I can only imagine how delayed they must be in China!

All day long, we seemed to slowly pass through a beautifully scenic countryside. Sometimes we saw small towns or castles sitting on top of steep hills, much like the pictures in ancient religious books. Other times, we traveled alongside rivers and streams, which looked like they frequently experienced major floods due to the wide, rocky banks on either side. It takes a lot of strong, fast-flowing water to sweep the edges of a river completely clear. At every station, there were groups of people, sometimes even crowds, wearing all kinds of clothing. Some looked just like the peasants back home or the ones I saw traveling through France and Germany, wearing short jackets, round hats, and homemade trousers. But others were incredibly picturesque. The women looked pretty from a distance, though up close they seemed rather thick around the waist. They all wore full white sleeves and large belts with strips of material fluttering from them, resembling ballet dresses, though they wore petticoats underneath. The strangest looking people were the Slovaks. They appeared more rugged than the rest, wearing large cowboy hats, very loose dirty-white trousers, white linen shirts, and massive leather belts studded with brass nails that were nearly a foot wide. They wore high boots with their trousers tucked into them, and they had long black hair and heavy black mustaches. They look very striking, but not very approachable. On stage, they would immediately be cast as an ancient gang of Eastern bandits. However, I am told they are actually quite harmless and tend to lack natural assertiveness.

It was late into the twilight when we reached Bistritz, a very interesting and old town. Since it sits practically on the border—the Borgo Pass leads from here directly into Bukovina—it has survived a very turbulent history, and the scars are clearly visible. Fifty years ago, a series of massive fires caused terrible destruction on five separate occasions. In the early 17th century, the town endured a three-week siege and lost 13,000 people, with famine and disease compounding the casualties of war.

Count Dracula had instructed me to stay at the Golden Krone Hotel. To my great delight, I found it to be thoroughly traditional and old-fashioned, which was perfect since I wanted to experience as much of the local culture as possible. They were clearly expecting me. As I approached the door, I was greeted by a cheerful elderly woman dressed in the typical peasant outfit: a white undergarment covered by a long double apron—front and back—made of colorful fabric that fit almost too tightly. When I got close, she bowed and asked, 'The English gentleman?' 'Yes,' I replied, 'Jonathan Harker.' She smiled and gave a message to an older man in white shirtsleeves who had followed her to the door. He left and immediately returned with a letter:

'My Friend. Welcome to the Carpathian Mountains. I have been eagerly awaiting your arrival. Sleep well tonight. Tomorrow at 3:00 PM, the public stagecoach will depart for Bukovina, and a seat has been reserved for you. My personal carriage will be waiting for you at the Borgo Pass to bring you to me. I hope your journey from London was pleasant and that you will enjoy your time in my beautiful country.

'Your friend,

'DRACULA.'

May 4. I found out that the hotel landlord had received a letter from the Count, instructing him to reserve the best seat on the coach for me. However, when I asked him for more details, he became strangely quiet and pretended not to understand my German. This couldn't be true, because he had understood me perfectly up until that point, answering my previous questions normally. He and his wife—the old woman who had greeted me—exchanged frightened looks. He muttered that the money for my ticket had been sent in a letter and claimed that was all he knew. When I asked if he knew Count Dracula and could tell me anything about his castle, both he and his wife crossed themselves. Saying they knew absolutely nothing, they flatly refused to say another word. It was so close to my departure time that I couldn't ask anyone else, but the whole situation felt very mysterious and highly unsettling.

Just before I left, the old woman came up to my room in a state of near hysteria.

'Must you go? Oh, young sir, do you really have to go?' she cried. She was so agitated that she seemed to forget her German, mixing it with another language I didn't recognize at all. I could only understand her by asking many clarifying questions. When I explained that I had to leave immediately for an important business matter, she asked:

'Do you know what day it is?' I told her it was May 4th. She shook her head and repeated:

'Oh, yes! I know that! But do you know what tonight is?' When I said I didn't understand, she continued:

'It is the eve of St. George's Day. Don't you know that tonight, at the stroke of midnight, all the evil forces in the world will be at their strongest? Do you realize where you are going, and what you are heading into?' She was in such obvious distress that I tried to calm her, but nothing worked. Finally, she fell to her knees and begged me not to go, or at least to wait a day or two before leaving. The whole thing seemed ridiculous, but I felt very uneasy. Still, I had a job to do and couldn't let anything stop me. I tried to help her up and told her as seriously as I could that I was grateful, but my duty was urgent and I had to leave. She stood up, dried her tears, took a crucifix from her neck, and offered it to me. I didn't know what to do. As an English Churchman, I was raised to view such things as slightly idol-worshipping, yet it felt too rude to reject the well-meaning gesture of an old woman in such a distressed state. I suppose she saw the hesitation on my face, because she placed the rosary around my neck herself, said 'For your mother's sake,' and left the room. I am writing this part of my diary while waiting for the coach, which is naturally running late, and the crucifix is still around my neck. Whether it's the old woman's fear, the many local ghost stories, or the crucifix itself, I don't know, but I feel much more anxious than usual. If this journal reaches Mina before I do, let it serve as my goodbye. Here comes the coach!

* * * * *

May 5. The Castle. The gray early morning has passed, and the sun is now high above the distant horizon. The horizon looks rough and uneven—whether from trees or hills, I can't tell, because it is so far away that large and small shapes blend together. I am not sleepy, and since I won't be called until I wake up naturally, I plan to write until I feel tired. There are many strange things to record. Just so whoever reads this doesn't think I had too much to drink before leaving Bistritz, let me explain exactly what I had for dinner. I ate a dish they call 'robber steak'—pieces of bacon, onion, and beef, heavily seasoned with red pepper, skewered on sticks and roasted over a fire, much like the cheap meat sold for cats in London! I drank a wine called Golden Mediasch, which leaves a strange tingling sensation on the tongue, though it isn't unpleasant. I only had two glasses of it, and nothing else.

When I boarded the coach, the driver wasn't in his seat yet, and I saw him talking with the landlady. They were clearly talking about me, as they kept glancing in my direction. Some of the people sitting on the bench outside the door—which they call the 'word-bearer' bench—came over to listen and then looked at me with pity. I heard several strange words repeated frequently, as there were many different nationalities in the crowd. I quietly took out my multi-language dictionary from my bag and looked them up. The definitions were not comforting. The words included 'Ordog' (Satan), 'pokol' (hell), 'stregoica' (witch), and 'vrolok' or 'vlkoslak' (words in Slovak and Serbian meaning werewolf or vampire). (Note to self: Ask the Count about these superstitions.)

When we finally departed, the crowd outside the inn had grown quite large. They all made the sign of the cross and pointed two fingers at me. I had to push one of my fellow passengers to tell me what the gesture meant. He hesitated at first, but when he learned I was English, he explained it was a charm to ward off the 'evil eye.' This wasn't very reassuring for someone starting a journey to an unknown place to meet an unknown man. Still, everyone seemed so kind, sorrowful, and sympathetic that I couldn't help but be deeply touched. I will never forget my final view of the inn yard: a crowd of picturesque locals crossing themselves under a wide archway, with beautiful oleander and orange trees planted in green tubs in the center of the yard. Then our driver, wearing wide linen trousers that covered the entire front seat (they call them 'gotza'), cracked his large whip over his four small horses. They ran side by side, and our journey began.

As we drove, the sheer beauty of the scenery quickly made me forget my ghostly fears. However, if I had understood the languages my fellow passengers were speaking, I might not have felt so at ease. Ahead of us lay green, sloping land filled with forests and woods, punctuated by steep hills topped with trees or farmhouses facing away from the road. The landscape was covered in a breathtaking explosion of fruit blossoms—apple, plum, pear, and cherry. As we passed, I could see fallen petals scattered across the green grass beneath the trees. The road wound in and out among these green hills (an area they call the 'Mittel Land'), sometimes disappearing around grassy curves or hiding behind the edges of pine forests that cascaded down the hillsides like tongues of fire. The road was rough, but we seemed to fly over it with intense speed. I didn't understand the rush at the time, but the driver was clearly determined to reach Borgo Prund as fast as possible. I was told this road is excellent in the summer, but it hadn't yet been repaired after the winter snows. This is unusual for roads in the Carpathian Mountains, as there is an old tradition of intentionally keeping them in poor condition. In the past, the local rulers (Hospadars) refused to repair them, fearing that good roads would make the Turks think they were preparing to move foreign troops and thus start a war.

Beyond the green hills of the Mittel Land, massive forested slopes rose up toward the steep peaks of the Carpathians themselves. They towered over us on both sides. The afternoon sun shone directly on them, bringing out the vibrant colors of the mountain range: deep blues and purples in the shadows of the peaks, greens and browns where grass met rock, and an endless view of jagged cliffs fading into the distance where snowy peaks stood majestically. Occasionally, we saw deep cracks in the mountains where, as the sun began to set, the white flash of a waterfall was visible. As we rounded the base of a hill, a towering, snow-covered peak appeared directly in front of us along our winding path. One of my companions touched my arm and pointed:

'Look! Isten szek!'—'God's seat!'—and he crossed himself with deep reverence.

As we continued on our endless winding journey, the sun sank lower behind us, and the evening shadows began to surround us. The snowy mountain peak still caught the last of the sunlight, glowing with a soft, pale pink. We occasionally passed Czechs and Slovaks in their traditional clothing, but I noticed that goiter (a painful swelling of the neck) was very common among them. There were many crosses along the roadside, and my companions crossed themselves every time we passed one. Sometimes we saw a peasant kneeling before a shrine, completely absorbed in prayer, ignoring us entirely as we passed. I saw many things that were new to me, like haystacks built high up in trees and beautiful clusters of weeping birch trees with bright white bark shining through delicate green leaves. We also passed 'leiter-wagons' (traditional long peasant carts) adapted to handle the bumpy roads. These carts were always full of peasants heading home—Czechs in white sheepskin coats and Slovaks in colored ones, carrying long axes like spears. As evening approached, it grew very cold, and the twilight blended the dark trees—oak, beech, and pine—into a single shadowy mist. In the deep valleys between the hills, dark fir trees stood out against patches of lingering snow. Sometimes, the road cut through dark pine forests that felt like they were closing in on us, creating a very spooky and serious atmosphere. This mood deepened the grim thoughts I had earlier when the setting sun illuminated the strange, ghost-like clouds that always seem to drift through the Carpathian valleys. On the steeper hills, our driver's haste didn't matter because the horses could only walk slowly. I wanted to get out and walk up the hills, as we do back home, but the driver refused. 'No, no,' he said. 'You must not walk here; the dogs are too fierce.' Then he added, trying to make a grim joke and looking back for the passengers' approval, 'And you may see more than enough of such things before you go to sleep.' The only time he stopped was briefly to light the carriage lamps.

When it finally got dark, the passengers became very anxious. They kept speaking to the driver, urging him to go faster. He whipped the horses mercilessly and shouted wildly to push them harder. Through the darkness, I saw a patch of gray light ahead, like a gap in the hills. The passengers' excitement peaked as the unstable coach bounced heavily on its leather springs, rocking like a boat on a rough sea. I had to hold on tight. The road leveled out, and we seemed to fly forward. The mountains closed in around us, looming darkly; we were entering the Borgo Pass. One by one, the passengers insisted on giving me gifts. I couldn't refuse their earnestness. The gifts were strange and varied, but each was given with sincere kindness, a blessing, and the same protective gestures I had seen in Bistritz—the sign of the cross and the charm against the evil eye. As we sped along, the driver leaned forward, and the passengers leaned out of the coach, staring eagerly into the dark. It was clear they were expecting something significant to happen, but no one would explain it to me. This intense excitement lasted for a while until we saw the Pass opening up on the eastern side. Dark clouds rolled overhead, and the air felt heavy with an approaching thunderstorm. It felt as if the mountains separated two different weather systems, and we had just entered the stormy one. I started looking for the carriage that was supposed to take me to the Count. I expected to see the glow of its lamps in the dark, but there was nothing. The only light came from our own flickering lamps, illuminating the white steam rising from our exhausted horses. I could see the sandy road clearly, but there was no vehicle waiting. The passengers let out a sigh of relief, which felt like a mockery of my own disappointment. I was just wondering what to do next when the driver checked his watch and muttered to the others, 'An hour earlier than expected.' He then turned to me and said in poor German:

'There is no carriage here. I suppose you aren't expected after all. You should come with us to Bukovina and return tomorrow or the next day—preferably the next day.' While he was speaking, the horses suddenly began to neigh, snort, and rear up wildly, forcing the driver to struggle to control them. Amidst a chorus of screams and the passengers frantically crossing themselves, a four-horse carriage suddenly pulled up behind us and stopped alongside our coach. By the light of our lamps, I could see the horses were magnificent, coal-black animals. The driver was a tall man with a long brown beard and a large black hat that hid his face. All I could see were his eyes, which glowed a bright, terrifying red in the lamplight as he turned toward us. He spoke to our driver:

'You are early tonight, my friend.' The coach driver stammered back:

'The English gentleman was in a hurry.' The stranger replied:

'I suppose that is why you wanted him to go on to Bukovina. You cannot fool me, my friend. I know too much, and my horses are fast.' He smiled, revealing a cruel mouth with bright red lips and sharp, ivory-white teeth. One of my fellow passengers whispered a line from the poem 'Lenore':

'For the dead travel fast.'

The strange driver clearly heard it, because he looked up with a chilling smile. The passenger quickly looked away, making the two-finger gesture and crossing himself again. 'Give me the gentleman's luggage,' the strange driver commanded. My bags were quickly handed over and placed in his carriage. I stepped down from the coach, and the new driver helped me up with a hand that felt like a grip of solid steel—his strength was unbelievable. Without saying a word, he flicked the reins, the horses turned, and we sped off into the dark Pass. Looking back, I saw the steam rising from the coach horses under their lamps and the silhouettes of my former companions crossing themselves. Then their driver cracked his whip, and they sped away toward Bukovina. As they vanished into the darkness, a strange chill and a deep sense of loneliness washed over me. But my new driver threw a cloak over my shoulders and a blanket over my knees, and said in perfect German:

'The night is cold, sir, and my master the Count ordered me to take good care of you. There is a flask of slivovitz (local plum brandy) under the seat if you need it.' I didn't drink any, but it was comforting to know it was there. I felt very strange and quite frightened. If I had had any other option, I would have taken it rather than continue this unknown journey in the dark. The carriage moved at a very fast pace in a straight line, then suddenly made a sharp turn and went down another straight road. It felt like we were just driving in circles over the same ground. I took note of a specific landmark and confirmed this was true. I wanted to ask the driver what was going on, but I was too afraid. Given my situation, I figured any protest would be useless if he was intentionally delaying us. Eventually, curious about the time, I struck a match and checked my watch. It was just a few minutes before midnight. This gave me a shock, as my recent experiences had amplified the usual superstitions about midnight. I waited in sickening suspense.

Then, a dog began to howl at a distant farmhouse—a long, painful wail of pure fear. Another dog joined in, and then another, until the wild howling echoed across the entire countryside, carried by the wind whistling through the Pass. At the first howl, the horses panicked and reared up, but the driver spoke to them calmly, and they settled down, though they were still shivering and sweating with fear. Then, from the distant mountains on either side, a louder, sharper howling began—the howling of wolves. It terrified both me and the horses. I wanted to jump out and run, while the horses reared and plunged so violently that the driver had to use all his massive strength to stop them from running away. After a few minutes, my ears adjusted to the noise, and the horses calmed down enough for the driver to get out and stand in front of them. He stroked and calmed them, whispering into their ears like a professional horse tamer. It worked perfectly; they became manageable again, even though they were still shaking. The driver got back in, shook the reins, and we took off at a high speed. This time, after reaching the far end of the Pass, he took a sharp right turn down a narrow road.

Soon, we were surrounded by trees that formed a dark arch over the road, creating a tunnel effect. Huge, imposing rocks loomed on either side. Despite being sheltered by the trees, we could hear the wind howling through the rocks and the branches crashing together as we drove. It grew colder and colder, and fine, powdery snow began to fall, quickly covering us and the landscape in a white blanket. The wind carried the distant howling of the dogs, which faded as we drove further. But the howling of the wolves sounded closer and closer, as if they were surrounding us. I was terrified, and the horses were too. The driver, however, was completely unbothered. He kept looking left and right, though I couldn't see anything in the dark.

Suddenly, to our left, I saw a faint, flickering blue flame. The driver saw it at the same time. He immediately stopped the horses, jumped down, and vanished into the darkness. I didn't know what to do, especially with the wolves getting closer. But before I could figure it out, the driver reappeared, climbed back in without a word, and we continued. I think I must have fallen asleep and dreamed about this, because it seemed to happen over and over again. Looking back, it feels like an endless nightmare. Once, the flame was close enough to the road that I could watch the driver. He walked quickly to the faint blue flame—which surprisingly didn't light up the ground around it at all—gathered some stones, and arranged them into a specific shape. At one point, I saw a bizarre optical illusion: when the driver stood between me and the flame, his body didn't block the light. I could still see the ghostly flicker right through him. It startled me, but it only lasted a second, so I assumed my eyes were playing tricks on me in the dark. After that, we didn't see any more blue flames, and we sped through the dark while the wolves howled around us, as if moving with us in a large circle.

Eventually, the driver wandered further away from the carriage than before. While he was gone, the horses began to tremble violently, snorting and screaming in terror. I couldn't understand why, because the wolves had stopped howling. But just then, the moon emerged from behind heavy black clouds, lighting up the jagged rocks and pine trees. By the moonlight, I saw that we were completely surrounded by a ring of wolves. They had white teeth, bright red tongues, long powerful limbs, and shaggy fur. Their eerie silence made them a hundred times more terrifying than when they were howling. I was completely paralyzed by fear. A person can only truly understand that kind of terror when they face it themselves.

Suddenly, the wolves all began to howl, as if the moonlight had triggered them. The horses jumped and reared, their eyes rolling in panic. But the ring of terrifying wolves kept them trapped in place. I shouted for the driver to return, thinking our only chance was to try and break through the circle so he could get back in. I yelled and beat the side of the carriage, hoping the noise would scare the wolves away from one side to give him a path. I have no idea how he managed it, but I suddenly heard his voice ringing out with absolute, commanding authority. I looked toward the sound and saw him standing in the middle of the road. He swept his long arms in a wide gesture, as if brushing away an invisible barrier, and the wolves slowly backed away. Just then, a thick cloud covered the moon, plunging us back into total darkness.

When my eyes adjusted, the driver was climbing back into the carriage, and the wolves were entirely gone. The whole experience was so bizarre and supernatural that I was overwhelmed with dread, too afraid to even speak or move. The journey felt endless as we drove through near-total darkness, with heavy clouds blocking the moon. We kept climbing higher and higher, with only a few brief descents. Suddenly, I realized the driver was pulling the horses to a stop inside the courtyard of a massive, ruined castle. There were no lights shining from its tall, dark windows, and its broken, crumbling walls formed a jagged silhouette against the night sky.
