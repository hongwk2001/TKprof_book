[P002] Jonathan Harker's Journal

[P003] (Kept in shorthand.)

[P004a] May 3. Bistritz. - I left Munich at 8:35 PM on May 1 and arrived in Vienna early the next morning. We were supposed to arrive at 6:46 AM, but the train was delayed by an hour. Budapest looked like a beautiful city from what I could see from the train and around the station. Because we arrived late and I didn't know when we would leave, I was afraid to wander too far. It felt as if I was leaving the West and stepping into the East.

[P004b] Crossing the grand, modern bridges over the Danube River, I could really feel that this was a land with traces of old Turkish rule. The Danube seemed unusually wide and deep.

[P005a] We left mostly on time and reached Klausenburg by nightfall. I decided to stay overnight at the Hotel Royale. For dinner, or rather a late supper, I had a chicken dish cooked with plenty of red pepper. It was incredibly tasty, but it made me very thirsty. (Memo: Make sure to get the recipe for Mina.) I asked the waiter, and he said it was called "Paprika Hendl." He told me it was a traditional local dish, so I should be able to find it anywhere along the Carpathian Mountains. My basic German skills turned out to be quite useful.

[P005b] Honestly, if I didn't know German, I can't imagine how I would have managed here.

[P006a] Whenever I had free time in London, I visited the British Museum library to look up books and maps about Transylvania. I figured that learning about the country beforehand would certainly help when dealing with a nobleman from there. The area the Count mentioned was in the extreme east, right where the borders of Transylvania, Moldavia, and Bukovina meet. It is located right in the middle of the Carpathian Mountains, making it one of the wildest and most remote regions in Europe.

[P006b] Unfortunately, I couldn't find any detailed maps comparable to our British maps, so I couldn't find any documents pointing out the exact location of Castle Dracula. However, I did confirm that Bistritz, the town where the Count was supposed to receive his mail, was quite well-known. I am writing down my notes here so I can refresh my memory when I tell Mina about my travels later.

[P007a] Four distinct groups of people live in Transylvania. In the south are the Saxons, mixed with the Wallachians, who are descendants of the ancient Dacians. The Magyars from Hungary live in the west, and the Szekelys live in the east and north. I am heading straight into Szekely territory. They firmly believe they are the descendants of Attila and the Huns. This might actually be true, since the Huns were already there when the Magyars conquered the region in the 11th century.

[P007b] I once read that all the superstitions in the world are gathered in the horseshoe-shaped region of the Carpathian Mountains. It is as if this place is the center of a giant whirlpool of imagination. If that is true, my stay here should be quite interesting. (Memo: I must ask the Count about all these superstitions.)

[P008a] The bed was comfortable, but I couldn't sleep because of strange dreams. It might have been because a dog was barking under my window all night. Or maybe it was that paprika dish. I drank an entire pitcher of water, but my throat still felt like it was burning. I finally fell asleep around dawn, only to be startled awake by someone banging on my door. I must have fallen into a deep sleep. I had paprika again for breakfast.

[P008b] I also had a cornmeal porridge called "Mamaliga" and a dish of stuffed eggplant with minced meat called "Impletata." It was absolutely delicious.

[P008c] (Memo: Don't forget to get this recipe too.) Since the train was scheduled to leave a little before 8 o'clock, I rushed through my breakfast. I ran to the station at 7:30, but to my surprise, I had to sit in the carriage for over an hour before the train finally started moving. It seems that the further east you go, the less reliable the train schedules become. At this rate, I can't even imagine how late the trains must be in China!

[P009a] We traveled slowly all day through picture-perfect countryside. Whenever I saw old castles and small villages perched on steep hills, they reminded me of illustrations from old religious books. Sometimes we ran alongside a wide river, and the large piles of round stones spread out on both sides clearly showed the history of frequent floods. It would take a massive amount of fast-flowing water to sweep the riverbanks so clean. At every station where the train stopped, crowds of people in all sorts of clothing were gathered.

[P009b] Some people wore short jackets, round hats, and clumsily patched pants, much like the peasants I used to see back home in England, France, or Germany. On the other hand, there were quite a few people who looked as exotic as a painting. From a distance, the women looked quite pretty, but up close, their waists looked very thick. They wore wide white sleeves and large belts with fluttering pieces of cloth, which made them look like ballet dancers. Of course, under all that, they were wearing layers of petticoats. But the most bizarre outfits belonged to the Slovaks.

[P009c] They looked much rougher than the others. They wore large cowboy hats, baggy white pants that looked quite dirty, and heavy leather belts that were nearly a foot wide and studded with brass. They tucked their pant legs into heavy boots that reached their knees, showing off long black hair and thick black mustaches. They certainly stood out, but their appearance was too intimidating to approach easily. If they were on stage, they would perfectly fit the role of ancient eastern bandits. However, I was told that despite their looks, they are actually quite gentle and lack assertiveness.

[P010] As darkness fell, we arrived in Bistritz. It was a very old and fascinating town. Because it is near the border leading straight to Bukovina through the Borgo Pass, this town has endured a very stormy history, and the scars are still visible. Fifty years ago, terrible fires swept through the town five times. In the early 17th century, a three-week siege claimed the lives of 13,000 people. Alongside those who died in battle, countless others were struck down by disease and starvation.

[P011a] Count Dracula had directed me to the Golden Krone Hotel. Fortunately, it had preserved its old-fashioned charm, which I liked very much, as I wanted to see as much of the local culture as possible. It was obvious that the people were expecting me. When I walked to the door, a cheerful older woman wearing an apron with bright, colorful patches greeted me. Her clothes were a bit too tight to look modest, but it was a typical peasant outfit.

[P011b] As I approached, she bowed and asked, "Are you the English gentleman?" "Yes, I am Jonathan Harker." She smiled and said something to an older man in his shirtsleeves who had followed her to the door. He went inside and quickly returned with a letter.

[P012a] "My friend. Welcome to the Carpathian Mountains. I have been eagerly waiting for you. Rest well tonight. The stagecoach for Bukovina leaves tomorrow at 3 PM, and I have reserved a seat for you. When you reach the Borgo Pass, my carriage will be waiting to bring you here. I hope your journey from London was safe, and I hope you enjoy your entire stay in my beautiful country.

[P012b] "Your friend,

[P012c] "Dracula."

[P013_015] May 4. I found out that the hotel owner had received a letter from the Count and had pre-booked the best seat on the stagecoach for me. But when I asked him for more details, he kept his mouth shut and pretended not to understand my German. This made no sense at all. Just moments before, he had been answering all my questions easily. He and his wife (the old woman who had greeted me) exchanged frightened looks. He mumbled that the letter only contained the money for the coach fare and that he knew nothing more.

[P015b] When I asked if they knew Count Dracula or had heard anything about his castle, the couple quickly crossed themselves. They insisted they knew nothing and refused to speak to me any further. The coach was about to leave, so I didn't have time to ask anyone else, but the whole situation felt somewhat creepy and ominous.

[P016] Right before I left, the old woman rushed up to my room and pleaded with a very anxious voice.

[P017a] "Must you really go? Oh! Young sir, must you go?" She was so agitated that she seemed to have forgotten the little German she knew. She spoke in a confusing mix of unfamiliar languages that I couldn't understand at all. I had to ask her repeatedly before I could barely guess what she meant. When I explained that I had important business and had to leave right away, she asked again.

[P017b] "Do you know what day it is today?" I answered that it was May 4th. She shook her head and spoke again.

[P017c] "Oh, yes! I know that! Not the date on the calendar. I'm asking if you know what tonight is." When I looked confused, she continued.

[P017d] "Tonight is the eve of St. George's Day. Don't you know that when the clock strikes midnight, all the evil things in this world will roam free? Do you have any idea where you are going right now?" She was in such distress that I tried to comfort her, but it was no use. She finally fell to her knees and tearfully begged me not to leave, or at least to stay a day or two longer. It sounded like nonsense, but honestly, I didn't feel comfortable.

[P017e] However, I had an official duty to fulfill, and nothing could stand in my way.

[P017f] I helped her to her feet. I firmly told her that I appreciated her concern, but my business could not be delayed and I absolutely had to go. Only then did she stand up and wipe her tears. She took the cross from her own neck and offered it to me. I didn't know what to do. As a member of the Church of England, I was raised to view such crosses as somewhat idolatrous. But it felt too cruel to reject the kind gesture of a terrified old woman.

[P018_020] Perhaps she saw the hesitation on my face, for she placed the rosary around my neck herself. "Accept this for the sake of your mother," she said. With those final words, she hurried out of the room.

[P020b] As I write in my journal while waiting for the inevitably delayed coach, the cross is still around my neck. I don't know if it's the old woman's trembling voice, the ghost stories scattered around this town, or the energy coming from the cross itself. But my heart feels unusually heavy and cold. If this journal reaches Mina before I do, this might be my last goodbye. Oh, here comes the coach!

[P020c] *       *       *       *       *

[P020d] May 5. The Castle. The gray dawn has cleared, and the sun has risen high above the distant horizon. I can't even tell if the jagged silhouette of the horizon is made of trees or mountain ridges. The distance is so great that large and small shapes just blur into one solid mass. I don't feel particularly sleepy. Since no one will wake me until I get up naturally, I plan to keep writing until my eyes close. There are mountains of strange events to record.

[P020e] Just in case anyone reading this thinks I was drunk when I left Bistritz, let me write down exactly what I ate last night.

[P021_022] I filled up on a dish called "Robber's Skewers." It consists of pieces of bacon, onion, and beef heavily coated in red pepper, strung on a stick, and roasted over a charcoal fire. It tastes just like the cheap meat given to cats on the streets of London! I paired it with a wine called "Golden Mediasch," which had a strange, slightly stinging taste on the tongue, but it wasn't bad. I had exactly two glasses and didn't touch any other alcohol.

[P022b] As I was about to board the stagecoach, the driver, who hadn't taken his seat yet, was having a serious conversation with the landlady. Seeing them glance at me from time to time, I was sure they were talking about me. People sitting on a bench outside the inn, known as the "gossip seat," also crept closer to eavesdrop. Most of them clicked their tongues and looked at me with pity. Since the crowd was a mix of different nationalities, strange and harsh-sounding words kept catching my ear. I quietly pulled out my multi-language dictionary to look up their meanings.

[P023a] Unfortunately, not a single word made me feel any better. There was "Ordog" (Satan), "Pokol" (hell), "Stregoica" (witch), as well as "Vrolok" and "Vlkoslak." The last two words mean werewolf or vampire in Slovak and Serbian, respectively. (Memo: Don't forget to ask the Count about these superstitions.)

[P023b] By the time we were ready to leave, quite a crowd had gathered in front of the inn. They all crossed themselves at once and pointed two fingers at me. I had to nudge the passenger next to me and ask what the gesture meant. He hesitated, but realizing I was English, he whispered that it was a charm against the "evil eye." For someone heading into an unknown land to meet a stranger, this explanation was incredibly unsettling. However, their faces were so full of genuine pity and sympathy that my heart warmed despite the circumstances.

[P024a] I will never forget the final scene in the inn's courtyard. Beautiful oleanders and orange trees sat in green pots in the middle of the yard, and people in all kinds of clothing stood under the wide archway, crossing themselves at me. Our driver wore baggy linen pants (called "gotza") that covered the entire front seat. As he cracked his long whip over the four small ponies, our bumpy journey finally began.

[P024b] As the beautiful scenery unfolded, my vague fears melted away. Of course, if I had understood the language my fellow passengers were speaking, I probably wouldn't have been able to relax and enjoy the view. Before us rolled sloping green hills covered in forests, and on the rugged mountain peaks, farmhouses were scattered sparsely, facing away from the trees and roads. The whole world was brightly colored with cherry, plum, pear, and apple blossoms. Every time the carriage drove by, white petals fluttered down onto the fresh green grass beneath the branches.

[P025a] The road wound its way through these green hills (called "Mittel-land"). It seemed to disappear over gentle grassy slopes, then hid itself in pine forests that spilled down the mountain ridges like flickering flames. Because it was a dirt road, the wheels rattled terribly, but we flew along at a frantic pace. At the time, I couldn't understand why we were in such a hurry, but the driver seemed determined not to lose a single second before reaching the Borgo Pass.

[P025b] I heard it was a great road for traveling in the summer, but it seemed they hadn't repaired it yet after the winter snows melted.

[P025c] Such rough road conditions are actually rare in the Carpathian Mountains. It is an old custom in this region to purposely leave the roads in bad shape. They say the old lords (called "Hospodars") deliberately avoided building good roads because they constantly worried that foreign enemies, especially the Turkish army, would use them to invade and start wars.

[P026a] As we crossed the gentle hills of the Mittel-land, the true majesty of the Carpathian Mountains revealed itself with massive forests and steep cliffs. The late afternoon sun shone directly on the grand ridges on both sides, bringing out all the brilliant colors of this beautiful mountain range. The deep shadows of the peaks were dyed dark blue and purple, while the areas where grass and rocks mixed were colored green and brown. Jagged rocks and sharp cliffs continued layer after layer, finally disappearing beyond the distant horizon alongside giant snow-capped peaks.

[P026b] Whenever the sun began to set, white waterfalls crashed and gleamed through massive, gaping cracks in the mountains. As the carriage rounded the base of the mountain, a snow-capped peak that seemed to tower right in front of our noses showed off its grandeur. The passenger sitting next to me gently tapped my arm.

[P027a] "Look there! It's Isten Szek!" he said, reverently crossing himself. "It means the Seat of God!"

[P027b] As we endlessly traveled the snake-like winding road, the sun slowly sank behind us, and a thick evening mist wrapped around us. Only the snow-capped mountain peaks held onto the last rays of the sunset, turning a cold, soft pink. From time to time, Czechs and Slovaks in beautiful traditional clothing passed by on the road. Unfortunately, quite a few of them had bulging necks due to swollen thyroids. Whenever we sped past a cross set up by the road, every single passenger crossed themselves.

[P028a] I also frequently saw peasant men and women kneeling and praying earnestly in front of the crosses. They seemed completely oblivious to the world, not even flinching at the loud noise of our carriage.

[P028b] Everything looked fascinating to me, from the haystacks tilted precariously in the trees to the clusters of weeping birches flashing their silver bark through light green leaves. We also passed several traditional wagons called "Leiterwagen." Shaped like the long spine of a snake, these wagons were perfect for traveling on bumpy roads. Inside the wagons, there were usually peasants heading home. There was a mix of Czechs in pure white sheepskin coats and Slovaks in brightly colored sheepskin coats; the latter held long staffs with axes on the end, grasping them like spears.

[P028c] As the day ended, the air turned freezing cold. In the deepening twilight, the oak, beech, and pine forests transformed into one massive, eerie curtain of darkness.

[P028d] In the deep valleys cut between the mountains, the snow hadn't fully melted yet, and the dark fir trees towering over it stood out sharply. Whenever the road cut through a dense pine forest, the darkness on both sides pressed in on us as if it would swallow us at any moment. The gloomy atmosphere created by the gray masses of trees kept pulling me into ominous thoughts. As the setting sun lit up the bizarre clouds that endlessly swirled through the valleys of the Carpathian Mountains, my wild imagination only grew worse.

[P028e] On the steep uphill roads, no matter how much the driver rushed, the horses' steps naturally slowed down. Just like back home in England, I wanted to get out of the carriage and walk up, but the driver jumped at the idea and stopped me.

[P028f] "No, absolutely not," he said flatly. "You shouldn't walk around carelessly here. The dogs are incredibly fierce." He threw out a grim joke and glanced back, as if hoping the passengers would laugh. "You'll see more than enough of those beasts before you go to bed, anyway." Except for a brief pause to light the carriage lamps, the driver whipped the horses relentlessly.

[P029a] As total darkness fell, a tight tension filled the passengers. They took turns shouting at the driver, urging him to drive the carriage even faster. The driver lashed the horses with his long whip and let out wild, savage yells to pick up speed. Far off in the darkness, a faint ray of gray light began to peek through a crack in the mountains. The passengers' excitement reached its peak. The old carriage shook violently on its thick leather springs, tossing around like a tiny boat caught in a fierce storm.

[P029b] I had to hold on for dear life just to keep from falling out. Once the bumpy road smoothed out, the carriage glided forward as if it had wings. The massive mountain ranges pressed in close on both sides, looking down on us threateningly. We had finally entered the Borgo Pass.

[P029c] The passengers began aggressively pressing gifts into my hands. They were so desperate that I couldn't refuse. The types of gifts were incredibly bizarre and varied, but the sincere look in their eyes was all the same. They repeated the strange gesture I had seen outside the inn in Bistritz—crossing themselves and making the two-finger sign to ward off the "evil eye"—while pouring out words of blessing. As the carriage raced on, the driver leaned far forward, and the passengers on either side stretched their necks out of the carriage, staring intently into the pitch-black darkness.

[P029d] It was obvious that something huge was about to happen, but no one would clearly explain it to me.

[P029e] Amidst the bursting tension, the mountain pass finally opened up to the east. Dark storm clouds swirled overhead, and the heavy, murky air pressed down as if lightning would strike at any moment. It felt as if this mountain range divided two separate worlds, and we had been sucked into the heart of a massive storm. I looked out the window to find the place where the Count's carriage was supposed to be waiting. I expected to see the flash of a lamp cutting through the pitch-black darkness, but the world was nothing but black.

[P029f] The only things visible were the white breath steaming from our wildly galloping horses and the flickering light of our carriage lamp illuminating that breath.

[P029g] Now, a long dirt road of white sand stretched out before us, but there were no tracks of any carriage on it. The passengers sighed in relief and leaned back. Their sighs somehow felt like they were mocking my disappointment, which upset me. As I was wondering what to do next, the driver looked at his pocket watch and muttered to himself, "We're an hour earlier than expected." Then he spoke to me in very poor German.

[P030a] "There is no carriage. It seems they didn't know you were coming. You had better come with us to Bukovina and return tomorrow or the day after—yes, the day after tomorrow would be best." Before the driver could even finish his sentence, the horses suddenly snorted, blew air from their noses, and stepped backward. The driver gripped the reins tightly and broke a sweat trying to control them. Then, amidst the screams of the peasants and the chaos of them frantically crossing themselves, a covered carriage pulled by four horses suddenly appeared out of the darkness.

[P030b] It caught up to us in a flash and stopped right beside our stagecoach. In the light of the lamp, the horses were magnificent, pitch-black and shiny like coal. The driver was a tall man with a long brown beard, wearing a wide-brimmed black hat pulled down low so that his face was almost invisible. The only things that could be seen were his two eyes, glowing a terrifying red in the lamp light. He spoke to our driver.

[P031] "You are early tonight, my friend." Our driver stammered in reply.

[P032] "The English gentleman was in such a hurry." Then the stranger shot back.

[P033] "So you planned to take him all the way to Bukovina. Don't try to fool me, friend. I know everything, and my horses are faster than the wind." As he gave a cold smile, his sharp teeth, white as ivory, flashed in the light between his tightly closed lips. One of the passengers whispered a line from Gottfried August Bürger's poem "Lenore" to the person sitting next to him.

[P034] "For the dead travel fast (Denn die Todten reiten schnell)."

[P035a] The strange driver seemed to have heard this perfectly well. With a chilling smile, he raised his head. The passenger who had recited the poem was startled and quickly crossed himself, making the sign to ward off the "evil eye." "Hand over the guest's luggage," the strange driver commanded. The luggage was moved to the covered carriage in an instant. I carefully climbed down the side of the stagecoach, and the new driver grabbed my arm with a grip as strong as steel tongs. He had incredible strength.

[P035b] Without a word, he shook the reins, and the horses turned and darted straight into the deep darkness of the mountain pass.

[P035c] Looking back, I could see the misty breath of the stagecoach horses in the lamp light and the silhouettes of my former companions crossing themselves. Then, as the driver cracked his whip and shouted, the stagecoach sped away toward Bukovina, as if fleeing. When they were completely swallowed by the darkness, a terrible chill and bone-deep loneliness washed over me. However, the new driver wrapped a thick cloak around my shoulders, placed a blanket over my knees, and spoke in fluent German.

[P036a] "The night air is quite harsh, sir. My master, the Count, gave strict orders to take good care of you. I have placed a bottle of slivovitz (a strong plum liquor) under your seat; drink some if you feel cold." I didn't drink any, but just knowing it was there gave me a strange sense of comfort. Fear and bizarre emotions pierced me to the bone. If there had been a mouse hole, I would have crawled into it to escape.

[P036b] Before I could even gather my senses, the carriage sped wildly down a straight road, and then suddenly jerked its direction to race down another straight road. It felt like we were just going around in circles in the exact same spot.

[P036c] I paid close attention to the unique landmarks along the road, and my guess was correct. I wanted to ask the driver what kind of trick he was playing in the middle of the night, but I was so terrified that my lips just trembled. If he was intentionally delaying out of spite, complaining would only put me at a disadvantage. I couldn't even guess how much time had passed, so I struck a match inside my coat to check my watch. It was just a few minutes before midnight. My heart sank.

[P036d] Because of the bizarre things I had experienced all evening, all the dark superstitions about midnight swirled in my head, inflating my fear. I held my breath and waited.

[P037a] Just then, a dog started barking mournfully from a farmhouse far below. It sounded like a terrifying, agonizing scream that made my hair stand on end. As if answering the call, a neighbor's dog barked, and then another. Soon, carried by the eerie wind sweeping through the mountain pass, the horrific howling of wild dogs echoed from every direction. Startled by the first howl, the horses snorted fiercely and reared up on their hind legs, but calmed down once the driver soothed them. However, they still trembled like leaves and sweat poured off them.

[P037b] At that moment, a louder, sharper roar erupted from deep within the mountains on both sides. It was the howling of wolves.

[P037c] The horses and I panicked at the same time. I wanted to jump out of the carriage and run away immediately. The horses reared up again and jumped wildly, forcing the driver to use incredible strength to hold them down. But by the time my ears grew accustomed to the horrific howling, the horses had also gradually calmed down enough for the driver to step down in front of them. He stroked and petted the horses, whispering in their ears in a low voice. He seemed like a master horse trainer. The effect was truly amazing.

[P037d] The horses were still trembling, but they quickly became as obedient as gentle sheep. When the driver got back in his seat and shook the reins, we sped off like an arrow.

[P037e] This time, we drove all the way to the end of the pass before sharply turning onto a narrow mountain road leading to the right.

[P038a] Soon, dense trees surrounded us, their branches forming an arch overhead that made it feel like we were passing through a deep tunnel. Massive, imposing rocks glared down at us from both sides. Even inside the carriage, the eerie howling of the wind squeezing through the rock crevices assaulted my ears. Every time the carriage rushed forward, the branches thrashing about in the wind let out loud screams. The air grew bitingly cold, and soon fine, powdery snow began to fall, burying us and everything around us in white dust.

[P038b] Over the fierce wind, the howling of wild dogs could be heard sparsely, but it seemed to be fading away. In contrast, the ferocious howling of the wolves grew clearer and clearer, as if they were closing in on us from all sides. I wasn't the only one consumed by extreme terror. The horses were also going mad with anxiety. Yet, the driver remained perfectly calm. He merely kept looking left and right, though to my eyes, there was nothing but pitch-black darkness.

[P039a] Suddenly, a faint blue flame flickered in the distance on my side. The driver saw it too. He abruptly stopped the carriage, jumped to the ground, and vanished completely into the darkness. With the wolves' howling practically at our throats, I didn't know what to do. While I sat trembling in pure terror, the driver silently reappeared, took his seat, and the carriage started moving again. I wished it were just a nightmare. No, it had to be a nightmare, because the exact same thing happened over and over again in a maddening loop.

[P039b] Looking back, those moments were a hellish nightmare itself. Once, the flame appeared so close to the road that I could clearly see the driver, even in the thick darkness.

[P039c] He walked over to where the blue flame was flickering. Strangely, the flame did not light up its surroundings at all. The driver gathered some stones to make some sort of marker. When he stood between the flame and me, a bizarre phenomenon occurred that made me doubt my own eyes. Even though his massive body stood in front of the flame, blocking it, I could see right through him and clearly observe the ghostly blue glow flickering behind him.

[P040_041] I was shocked, but since it happened in a split second, I assumed it was just a trick of my eyes from staring too hard into the deep darkness.

[P041b] After that, the blue flame did not appear for a while. We raced through the darkness, surrounded by the howling of a wolf pack that seemed to be moving in a giant circle, tracking our every move.

[P041c] Finally, the time came when the driver had to walk much further away than before. While he was gone, the horses trembled even more violently, snorting and going insane with fear. I couldn't figure out why. The wolves' howling had completely stopped. Right at that moment, the moonlight broke through the dark clouds and peeked out from behind a sheer, pine-covered rocky peak. In the pale moonlight around us, wolves with massive, hairy legs were forming a giant circle, completely surrounding us with their white teeth bared and their bright red tongues hanging out.

[P041d] It was a hundred times more terrifying to see them crouching in dead silence than when they were howling. My entire body froze in fear. It is only when a person faces such overwhelming terror head-on that they truly understand what it looks like.

[P041e] Suddenly, as if on cue, the wolves all pointed their noses to the moonlight and began to howl. The horses rolled their eyes back until only the whites showed and kicked their front legs in a frenzy. But trapped inside a living wall of terror enclosing us from every side, we couldn't take a single step. I screamed for the driver to return at once. My only hope was for him to break through the pack and get back to the carriage.

[P041f] To somehow create a gap, I pounded on the side of the carriage and yelled at the top of my lungs. But out of nowhere, the driver's thunderous roar tore through the air.

[P041g] I looked toward the sound and saw him standing solidly right in the middle of the road. As he swung his long arms as if pulling back an invisible curtain, the wolf pack unbelievably tucked their tails and slunk backward. Just then, a heavy cloud swallowed the moon, and the world was plunged back into perfect darkness.

[P042a] When my vision returned, the driver was climbing onto the covered carriage, and the wolf pack had vanished without a trace. The whole situation was so bizarre and horrifying that I couldn't move an inch or even make a sound. While we raced through the pitch-black darkness hidden from the moonlight by clouds, one minute felt like a year. Sometimes we shot down steep descents, but for the most part, the carriage was climbing a steep uphill path. When I finally came to my senses, the driver was stopping the carriage in the courtyard of an incredibly massive, ruined castle.

[P042b] There wasn't a single light in the tall, pitch-black windows, and only the crumbling battlements showed off their grotesque silhouettes against the moonlight.
