[P001] CHAPTER 5

[P002] Letter from Miss Mina Murray to Miss Lucy Westenra.

[P003] May 9.

[P004] My dearest Lucy,

[P005a] Please forgive me for taking so long to write, but I have simply been overwhelmed with work. Life as an assistant school teacher can be quite challenging at times. I am longing to be with you by the sea, where we can talk freely and daydream about the future. I have been working very hard lately because I want to keep up with Jonathan’s studies, and I have been practicing my shorthand very diligently. When we are married, I want to be useful to Jonathan. If I can take shorthand well enough, I can dictate whatever he wants to say and type it out for him, which is why I am also practicing hard on the typewriter. Jonathan and I sometimes write letters to each other in shorthand, and he is even keeping a shorthand journal of his travels abroad. When I am with you, I plan to keep a diary in the same way. I do not mean one of those restrictive diaries with only two pages per week and Sunday squeezed into a corner, but rather a journal where I can write whenever the mood strikes me. I do not suppose it will be very interesting to other people, but it is not meant for them anyway. I might show it to Jonathan someday if there is anything worth sharing, but it is primarily just a practice book for me.

[P005b] I am going to try doing what female journalists do: conducting interviews, writing detailed descriptions, and trying to memorize conversations. I have heard that with a little practice, a person can remember everything that happens or is said during a day. We will see how it goes. I will tell you all about my little plans when we meet. I just received a quick note from Jonathan in Transylvania. He is doing well and should be returning in about a week. I am dying to hear all his news. It must be so wonderful to see foreign countries. I wonder if we—Jonathan and I—will ever get to travel and see them together. I just heard the ten o'clock bell ringing. Goodbye for now.

[P005c] With love,

[P006_007] MINA.

[P009] Letter from Lucy Westenra to Mina Murray.

[P010a] 17 Chatham Street,

[P010b] Wednesday.

[P011_012] My dearest Mina,

[P013a] I must say it is very unfair of you to accuse me of being a bad correspondent. I have written to you twice since we parted, and your last letter was only your second to me. Besides, I simply do not have anything to tell you. There is truly nothing going on that would interest you. London is quite lively right now, and we spend a lot of time visiting art galleries, going for walks, and riding in the park. As for the tall, curly-haired man, I suppose you are referring to the one who accompanied me to the last popular concert. Someone has clearly been gossiping. That was Mr. Holmwood. He visits us frequently, and he gets along wonderfully with my mother; they have so much in common to talk about. A little while ago, we met a man who would be absolutely perfect for you, if you were not already engaged to Jonathan. He is an excellent match—handsome, wealthy, and from a good family. He is a doctor and incredibly clever. Just imagine! He is only twenty-nine years old, and he manages a massive insane asylum all on his own. Mr. Holmwood introduced him to me; he came to call on us and now drops by often. I think he is one of the most determined men I have ever met, yet also the calmest. He seems completely unflappable. I can only imagine what a powerful influence he must have over his patients. He has a peculiar habit of looking you straight in the face, as if he is trying to read your thoughts. He tries this on me quite a bit, but I flatter myself that I am a tough nut to crack. I know this from looking in my own mirror. Do you ever try to read your own face? I do, and I can assure you it is quite an interesting exercise, requiring far more effort than you might think if you have never tried it. He tells me that I provide a fascinating psychological study for him, and I humbly agree. As you know, I do not take enough interest in clothing to be able to describe the newest fashions. Dresses are a bore. There I go using slang again, but never mind; Arthur uses it every day.

[P013b] Well, there it is, the secret is out. Mina, we have shared all our secrets since we were children. We have slept in the same bed, eaten together, laughed together, and cried together. And now that I have started, I want to say more. Oh, Mina, couldn't you guess? I love him. I am blushing as I write this, because even though I believe he loves me, he has not yet said the words. But oh, Mina, I love him; I love him; I love him! Just admitting it makes me feel better. I wish I were with you right now, dear, sitting by the fire and getting ready for bed like we used to. I would try to explain exactly how I feel. I don't even know how I am managing to write this to you. I am afraid that if I stop, I will tear up the letter, and I do not want to stop because I desperately want to tell you everything. Please write back immediately and tell me what you think about all this. Mina, I have to stop now. Goodnight. Bless me in your prayers, Mina, and please pray for my happiness.

[P014] LUCY.

[P015] P.S.—I do not need to tell you that this is a secret. Goodnight again.

[P016a] L.

[P016b] Letter from Lucy Westenra to Mina Murray.

[P017_021] May 24.

[P021b] My dearest Mina,

[P021c] Thank you over and over again for your sweet letter. It was so wonderful to be able to confide in you and receive your sympathy.

[P021d] My dear, when it rains, it pours. It is amazing how true the old proverbs are. Here I am, turning twenty this September, and I had never received a proposal until today—not a real proposal—and today I received three of them. Just imagine! THREE proposals in a single day! Isn't it overwhelming? I feel genuinely and truly sorry for two of those poor men. Oh, Mina, I am so incredibly happy that I hardly know what to do with myself. And three proposals! But for goodness’ sake, please do not tell any of the other girls. They would get all sorts of ridiculous ideas and feel offended if they didn't receive at least six proposals on their very first day back home. Some girls are so vain! You and I, Mina dear, who are already spoken for and will soon settle down quietly as married women, can afford to despise such vanity. Anyway, I must tell you about the three men, but you must keep it an absolute secret from everyone—except Jonathan, of course. I know you will tell him, just as I would certainly tell Arthur if I were in your place. I believe a woman ought to share everything with her husband, don't you? And I want to be fair. Men expect women—certainly their wives—to be as straightforward as they are, though I am afraid women are not always as fair as they should be.

[P021e] Well, my dear, Suitor Number One arrived just before lunch. I told you about him: Dr. John Seward, the man who runs the insane asylum, the one with the strong jaw and handsome forehead. He appeared very cool on the outside, but he was actually quite nervous. He had clearly rehearsed all sorts of little things to say and remembered them, but he nearly sat right on his own silk top hat, which calm men generally do not do. Then, trying to look at ease, he kept playing with a surgical lancet in a way that made me want to scream. He spoke to me very directly, Mina. He told me how dear I had become to him, even though he had known me for such a short time, and he described what a wonderful life we could have supporting and cheering each other on.

[P021f] He was about to say how miserable he would be if I did not return his affection, but when he saw me start to cry, he called himself a brute and said he did not want to add to my distress. He stopped abruptly and asked if I might be able to love him in time. When I shook my head, his hands trembled. Then, hesitating slightly, he asked if I already cared for someone else. He phrased it very nicely, explaining that he did not want to pry, but he needed to know because if a woman’s heart was free, a man might still have hope. At that moment, Mina, I felt it was my duty to tell him that there was indeed someone else. I only told him that much. He stood up, looking very strong and solemn, took both my hands in his, and said he hoped I would be happy. He told me that if I ever needed a friend, I should count on him as one of my best. Oh, Mina dear, I cannot help but cry; please excuse the teardrops blotting this letter. Being proposed to is all very flattering and lovely, but it is not at all a happy experience when you have to watch a poor man, whom you know loves you honestly, walk away with a broken heart. It is heartbreaking to realize that, no matter what he might say in the moment, you are walking entirely out of his life. My dear, I have to stop writing for now. I feel so miserable, even though I am so happy.

[P021g] Evening.

[P022_023] Arthur just left, and I am in much better spirits than when I stopped writing, so I can continue telling you about my day. Well, my dear, Suitor Number Two arrived after lunch. He is such a delightful man, an American from Texas, and he looks so young and vibrant that it seems almost impossible he has traveled to so many places and experienced such adventures. I can certainly sympathize with poor Desdemona, captivated by the dangerous tales poured into her ears by a Moor. I suppose we women are such cowards that we believe a man will protect us from our fears, and so we marry him. I know exactly what I would do if I were a man trying to make a girl fall in love with me. Actually, no I don't, because while Mr. Morris was thrilling us with his stories, Arthur never told any, and yet— But my dear, I am getting ahead of myself. Mr. Quincey P. Morris found me when I was alone. It seems that a man always manages to find a girl alone. Actually, that is not true either, because Arthur tried twice to create an opportunity, and I helped him as much as I could; I am not ashamed to admit it now.

[P023b] I should mention right away that Mr. Morris does not always use slang. He never speaks that way to strangers or in front of them, as he is thoroughly educated and has impeccable manners. However, he discovered that his American slang amused me, so whenever I was around and there was no one present who might be offended, he would say the funniest things. I suspect, my dear, that he makes it all up on the spot, because his slang fits perfectly into whatever point he is trying to make. But I suppose that is just the nature of slang. I do not know if I will ever use slang myself; I do not even know if Arthur likes it, as I have never heard him use any. Anyway, Mr. Morris sat down beside me, looking as cheerful and jolly as he could manage, but I could still see that he was incredibly nervous. He took my hand in his and said in the sweetest way imaginable:

[P024] "Miss Lucy, I know I am not good enough to even tie your shoes. But I reckon that if you wait around until you find a man who is, you will end up joining those seven foolish virgins with their lamps when your time comes. Why don't you partner up with me, and let us travel down the long road together, pulling as a team?"

[P025] He looked so good-humored and jolly that refusing him did not feel half as difficult as refusing poor Dr. Seward. So I answered, as lightheartedly as I could, that I did not know the first thing about partnering up, and that I was not at all broken in for pulling a carriage. At that, he apologized, saying he had spoken too casually. He hoped I would forgive him if he had made a mistake by being so informal on such a serious and momentous occasion. He looked completely earnest as he said it, and I could not help but feel a little serious myself—I know you will think I am an awful flirt, Mina—even though I could not suppress a thrill of excitement that he was my second proposal in a single day. Then, my dear, before I could utter a word, he unleashed a passionate torrent of romance, laying his heart and soul at my feet. He was so intensely earnest that I will never again assume a man cannot be serious just because he is often cheerful. I suppose he saw something in my expression that stopped him, because he abruptly paused. Then, with a kind of manly fervor that I could have loved him for if my heart were free, he said:

[P026] "Lucy, I know you are an honest-hearted girl. I would not be speaking to you like this if I did not believe you were genuine and true, right down to your very soul. Tell me straight, like one good friend to another: is there someone else you care for? If there is, I promise I will never bother you about this again. But if you will let me, I will remain a very loyal friend."

[P027] My dear Mina, why are men so noble when we women are so unworthy of them? Here I was, practically making a joke out of this generous, true gentleman. I burst into tears—I am afraid you will think this is an overly sentimental, sloppy letter in more ways than one—but I truly felt terrible. Why can't a girl just marry three men, or however many want her, and save everyone all this heartache? But that is heresy, and I shouldn't say such things. I am glad that, even though I was crying, I managed to look into Mr. Morris's brave eyes and tell him straight out:

[P028] "Yes, there is someone I love, even though he has not yet told me that he loves me back." It was the right thing to speak to him so frankly, because his face immediately brightened. He reached out and took both my hands—or perhaps I put them into his—and said with genuine warmth:

[P029] "That is my brave girl. Being too late for a chance to win you is still better than being on time for any other girl in the world. Don't cry, my dear. If you are crying for me, you should know I am tough, and I can take this standing up. If that other fellow does not realize how lucky he is, well, he had better figure it out soon, or he will have to deal with me. Little girl, your honesty and courage have won you a friend, and a friend is rarer than a lover; it is certainly more unselfish. My dear, I am going to have a pretty lonely walk between now and Kingdom Come. Would you give me just one kiss? It would give me something to hold onto to keep the darkness at bay every now and then. You can do it, if you want to, because that other good fellow—and he must be a good, fine man, or you wouldn't love him—hasn't spoken up yet." That completely won me over, Mina. It was so brave, sweet, and noble of him to speak so kindly of a rival, wasn't it? And he looked so sad, so I leaned forward and kissed him. He stood up, still holding my hands, looked down into my face—I am sure I was blushing furiously—and said:

[P030] "Little girl, I am holding your hand, and you have kissed me. If that doesn't make us friends, nothing ever will. Thank you for your sweet honesty. Goodbye." He squeezed my hand, picked up his hat, and walked straight out of the room without a single backward glance, without a tear, a tremor, or a pause. And I am left here crying like a baby. Oh, why must such a wonderful man be made unhappy when there are plenty of girls who would worship the very ground he walks on? I know I would, if I were free—it is just that I do not want to be free. My dear, this whole ordeal has thoroughly upset me. I feel like I cannot write about my own happiness right now, having just told you all of this. And I do not want to talk about Suitor Number Three until the whole story can be a happy one.

[P031] Always your loving,

[P032] LUCY.

[P033a] P.S.—Oh, regarding Number Three—I don't really need to explain who Number Three is, do I? Besides, it was all such a blur. It felt like only a second passed from the moment he walked into the room until he had his arms around me and was kissing me. I am so very, very happy, and I don't know what I have done to deserve this. I must simply spend the future trying to show that I am not ungrateful to God for His immense goodness in sending me such a lover, husband, and friend.

[P033b] Goodbye.

[P034_035] Dr. Seward’s Diary.

[P036] (Recorded on phonograph)

[P037] May 25.—My appetite is at its lowest ebb today. I cannot eat and I cannot rest, so I am turning to my diary instead. Ever since I was rejected yesterday, I have been left with a terrible sense of emptiness; nothing in the world feels important enough to be worth doing.... Knowing that the only cure for this sort of melancholy is work, I went down to the wards among the patients. I singled out one who has provided me with a highly fascinating psychological study. He is so bizarre that I am determined to understand him as thoroughly as possible. Today, I felt that I got closer to the heart of his mystery than ever before.

[P038] I interrogated him more extensively than usual, aiming to fully master the facts of his delusions. Looking back, I can see that there was an element of cruelty in my approach. I seemed intent on holding him at the very peak of his madness—a practice I normally avoid with my patients as strictly as I would avoid the gates of hell.

[P039] (Memo to self: Under what circumstances would I not avoid the pit of hell?) Everything in Rome is for sale. Hell has its price! A word to the wise is sufficient. If there is a deeper instinct driving this behavior, it will be valuable to trace it accurately later on, so I had better start documenting it now. Therefore—

[P040] R. M. Renfield, age 59.—Sanguine temperament; immense physical strength; morbidly excitable; prone to periods of gloom that culminate in some fixed obsession which I cannot yet comprehend. I hypothesize that his sanguine nature, combined with some disturbing external influence, builds up to a mentally constructed conclusion. He is a potentially dangerous man, and ironically, he would likely be even more dangerous if he were unselfish. In selfish men, caution acts as a protective armor for their enemies just as much as for themselves. My theory on this matter is that when a person's core motivation is the self, the inward-pulling and outward-pushing forces remain balanced. However, when the core motivation is duty, a cause, or a higher purpose, the outward-pushing force takes complete control, and only an accident—or a series of accidents—can restore the balance.

[P041] Letter from Quincey P. Morris to the Honorable Arthur Holmwood.

[P042] May 25.

[P043] My dear Art,

[P044] We have swapped tall tales by campfires on the prairies; we have bandaged each other's wounds after attempting to land at the Marquesas Islands; and we have drunk toasts on the shores of Lake Titicaca. There are still more stories to tell, more wounds to heal, and another toast to drink. Won't you let us drink this one at my "campfire" tomorrow night? I do not hesitate to ask you, because I happen to know that a certain young lady is already committed to a dinner party, leaving you completely free. There will only be one other guest: our old friend from Korea, Jack Seward. He is coming over, and we both want to shed a few tears into our wine glasses and raise a heartfelt toast to the happiest man in the entire world—a man who has won the noblest heart God ever created and the one most worth winning. We promise you a warm welcome, a loving greeting, and a toast as true and reliable as your own right hand. And we both swear that if you get too drunk toasting a certain pair of beautiful eyes, we will just leave you at home. Come on over!

[P045] Yours, as ever and always,

[P046] QUINCEY P. MORRIS.
