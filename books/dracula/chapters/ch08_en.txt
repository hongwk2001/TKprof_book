CHAPTER 8

MINA MURRAY'S JOURNAL

Same day, 11 p.m. — Oh, I am so exhausted! If keeping this diary wasn't a strict duty, I wouldn't even open it tonight. We went for a lovely walk. Lucy cheered up quickly, all thanks to some cute cows in a field near the lighthouse that came sniffing up to us and scared us half to death. I think that sudden fright wiped our minds clear of everything else and gave us a fresh start. We enjoyed a fantastic "severe tea"—a hearty late-afternoon meal that serves as dinner—at a charming, old-fashioned inn in Robin Hood's Bay. It had a lovely bow-window jutting out right over the seaweed-covered rocks on the shore. I'm sure our massive appetites would have shocked the modern "New Women." Thank goodness men are much more forgiving about that sort of thing! On our walk back home, we had to stop frequently to rest, and we were constantly terrified that wild bulls might charge at us. Lucy was completely worn out, and our plan was to sneak off to bed the moment we got in. However, the young curate stopped by, and Mrs. Westenra invited him to stay for supper. Lucy and I had to fight desperately to keep our eyes open; it took a heroic effort on my part. I really think bishops should start training a new breed of curates who decline late suppers, no matter how much they are pressured, and who can tell when girls are tired. Lucy is fast asleep now, breathing softly. Her cheeks have more color than usual, and she looks incredibly sweet. If Mr. Holmwood fell in love with her just by seeing her in a formal drawing-room, I can only imagine what he would say if he saw her like this. Maybe someday those "New Woman" writers will argue that men and women should see each other asleep before they propose or accept. Though honestly, I doubt the New Woman of the future will wait around to accept a proposal; she'll probably do the proposing herself. And she'll probably do a great job of it, too! That's somewhat comforting. I am so happy tonight because my dear Lucy seems to be doing much better. I truly believe she has turned a corner and that her sleepwalking troubles are behind us. I would be completely happy if only I had word from Jonathan.... God bless him and keep him safe.

*       *       *       *       *

August 11, 3 a.m. — Back to the diary. I can't sleep now, so I might as well write. I am far too agitated. We have just been through the most agonizing, terrifying ordeal. I fell asleep the moment I closed my diary earlier.... Then, quite suddenly, I jerked awake. I sat up in bed feeling a horrific sense of dread and an eerie emptiness in the room. It was pitch black, so I couldn't see Lucy's bed. I crept over and felt around in the dark. The bed was empty. I struck a match and confirmed she was not in the room. The door was shut, but it wasn't locked the way I had left it. I didn't want to wake her mother, who has been feeling worse than usual lately, so I threw on some clothes to go look for her. As I was leaving the room, it occurred to me that the clothes she chose to wear might indicate where her sleeping mind intended to go. Her dressing-gown would mean she stayed inside; her street dress would mean she went outside. Both were still in the closet. "Thank God," I told myself, "she can't have gone far if she's only in her nightgown." I ran downstairs and checked the sitting room. Empty! I checked all the other open rooms in the house, my panic growing with every second. Finally, I reached the front door and found it unlatched. It wasn't swung wide open, but the lock hadn't caught. The people who own the house are very strict about locking up every night, so I realized with horror that Lucy must have wandered outside exactly as she was. There was no time to consider the consequences; a blind, overwhelming panic took over. I grabbed a large, heavy shawl and ran out into the night. The clock was striking 1:00 a.m. as I reached the Crescent, and the streets were completely deserted. I sprinted down the North Terrace but saw no sign of the white figure I was looking for. Standing on the edge of the West Cliff, above the pier, I looked across the harbor toward the East Cliff. I felt a mix of hope and fear that I might spot Lucy at our favorite bench. The full moon was bright, but heavy, dark clouds were moving fast across the sky, turning the landscape into a flashing, shifting scene of light and shadow. For a moment, a cloud shadowed St. Mary's Church, and I couldn't see anything. But as the cloud shifted, the abbey ruins came into view, and a narrow, sharp beam of moonlight illuminated the church and the graveyard. My instincts were right: there, on our favorite bench, illuminated by the silver moonlight, was a figure in snowy white, half-reclining. The clouds moved too fast for me to get a clear look before the shadow returned, but I thought I saw something dark standing behind the bench, leaning over the white figure. I couldn't tell if it was a man or an animal. I didn't wait for another look; I flew down the steep stairs to the pier, ran past the fish market, and crossed the bridge—the only route to the East Cliff. The town was dead quiet without a single soul in sight, which relieved me, as I didn't want anyone to see poor Lucy in this state. The distance felt infinite. My knees were shaking and I was gasping for air as I struggled up the endless stairs toward the abbey. I must have been moving fast, but it felt like my feet were made of lead and every joint in my body was rusted shut. As I neared the top, I was close enough to see the bench and the white figure clearly, even through the passing shadows. There was, without a doubt, a long, black shape leaning over the reclining white figure. I screamed in terror, "Lucy! Lucy!" The dark shape raised its head, and from where I stood, I saw a pale face and glowing red eyes. Lucy didn't answer, and I kept running toward the graveyard entrance. As I ran through the gates, the church blocked my view of the bench for a minute. When I could see it again, the cloud had passed. In the brilliant moonlight, I saw Lucy leaning back against the bench. She was entirely alone, and there was no sign of any living thing nearby.

When I leaned over her, I realized she was still fast asleep. Her lips were parted, and instead of her usual quiet breathing, she was taking long, heavy, gasping breaths, as if she was struggling to fill her lungs. As I leaned closer, she weakly raised her hand in her sleep and clutched the collar of her nightgown tightly around her throat. A sudden shiver ran through her, as though the cold had just hit her. I immediately wrapped the thick, warm shawl around her and pulled it snug against her neck, terrified she might catch a deadly chill from the night air in only her thin nightgown. I didn't want to startle her awake, so to keep my hands free to support her, I pinned the shawl at her throat with a large safety pin. I must have been shaking from anxiety, though, because I clumsily pinched or pricked her skin with it. A moment later, as her breathing calmed, she reached for her throat again and let out a soft moan. Once she was securely bundled up, I forced my own shoes onto her feet and gently began to wake her. She didn't respond at first, but she grew increasingly restless, moaning and sighing. Knowing we needed to get out of there quickly for a dozen reasons, I finally shook her harder until she opened her eyes. She didn't seem shocked to see me, mostly because she hadn't yet grasped where she was. Lucy always wakes up gracefully, and even in this terrifying situation—freezing cold and waking up half-dressed in a graveyard—she didn't lose her composure. Trembling slightly, she clung to my arm. When I told her we had to go home immediately, she stood up without a word, obedient as a child. As we walked, the gravel path cut into my bare feet, and I winced. Lucy noticed, stopped, and tried to insist I take my shoes back, but I refused. However, when we reached a mud puddle left over from the storm just outside the graveyard, I deliberately smeared mud all over my feet. That way, if we happened to pass anyone on the way home, my bare skin wouldn't be as obvious in the dark.

Luck was on our side, and we made it all the way home without crossing paths with a single person. At one point, we did see a man who looked pretty drunk stumbling down the street ahead of us, but we ducked into a doorway and waited until he disappeared up one of the steep, narrow alleys they call "wynds" in Scotland. My heart was pounding so violently the whole time that I honestly thought I was going to pass out. I was sick with worry for Lucy—both that the freezing exposure might ruin her health, and that her reputation would be destroyed if anyone found out about this. When we finally got inside, washed our feet, and said a quick prayer of thanks, I tucked her safely into bed. Just before she drifted off, she begged—absolutely pleaded with me—not to breathe a word of her sleepwalking adventure to anyone, not even her mother. I was hesitant to promise at first. But when I thought about how fragile Mrs. Westenra's heart is, how this news would distress her, and how viciously the story would inevitably be twisted if it ever leaked to the public, I decided it was best to keep the secret. I pray I made the right choice. I've locked the bedroom door and tied the key to my wrist, so hopefully, there won't be any more disturbances tonight. Lucy is sleeping deeply, and the first light of dawn is just beginning to break over the ocean....

*       *       *       *       *

Same day, noon. — Everything is perfectly fine. Lucy slept deeply until I finally woke her, and she didn't even seem to have rolled over in the night. That terrifying midnight adventure doesn't appear to have harmed her at all; if anything, it actually helped, because she looks healthier this morning than she has in weeks. I felt terrible, though, when I realized that my clumsy fumbling with the safety pin had actually hurt her. In fact, it could have been bad, because the skin on her neck was actually pierced. I must have grabbed a loose fold of skin and run the pin straight through it, because there are two tiny red dots, like pinpricks, and a single drop of blood on the collar of her nightgown. When I apologized profusely and fussed over it, she just laughed, patted my hand, and insisted she hadn't felt a thing. Luckily, the marks are so tiny that they won't leave a scar.

*       *       *       *       *

Same day, night. — We had a lovely day. The air was crisp, the sun was bright, and there was a refreshing breeze. We packed a lunch and went to Mulgrave Woods. Mrs. Westenra took the carriage up the main road, while Lucy and I walked along the cliff path and met her at the gates. I felt a pang of sadness myself, wishing Jonathan could have been there to make the day absolutely perfect. But I just have to be patient! In the evening, we took a stroll along the Casino Terrace, listened to some excellent music by Spohr and Mackenzie, and turned in early. Lucy seems more relaxed than she has been in a long time, and she fell asleep the moment her head hit the pillow. I'm going to lock the door and tie the key to my wrist exactly as I did last night, even though I don't anticipate any problems tonight.

*       *       *       *       *

August 12. — I was completely wrong. Twice during the night, I was woken up by Lucy trying to leave the room. Even in her sleep, she seemed visibly annoyed to find the door locked, and she marched back to bed in a huff. I woke up around dawn and listened to the birds chirping outside the window. When Lucy woke up, I was thrilled to see that she looked even better than the day before. All her usual bubbly energy seemed to have returned. She crawled into my bed, snuggled up next to me, and talked endlessly about Arthur. I confessed how worried I was about Jonathan, and she did her best to comfort me. It actually helped; sympathy doesn't change the facts, but it does make them easier to live with.

*       *       *       *       *

August 13. — Another peaceful day, and then back to bed with the key firmly attached to my wrist. I woke up in the middle of the night again to find Lucy sitting bolt upright in bed, completely asleep, pointing at the window. I slipped out of bed quietly, pulled back the blind, and looked outside. The moonlight was brilliant, and the soft glow washing over the ocean and the sky—blending them into one vast, silent mystery—was breathtakingly beautiful. Right between me and the moonlight, a massive bat fluttered by, flying in large, frantic circles. It swooped close to the glass once or twice, but I suppose it got scared when it saw me, because it fluttered off across the harbor toward the abbey. When I turned back from the window, Lucy had laid down again and was sleeping soundly. She didn't move an inch for the rest of the night.

*       *       *       *       *

August 14. — I spent the entire day reading and writing on the East Cliff. Lucy seems to have fallen in love with the spot just as much as I have, and it's a struggle to drag her away when it's time to head home for meals. This afternoon, she said something incredibly strange. We were walking back for dinner, and as usual, we stopped at the top of the stairs above the West Pier to admire the view. The sun was hanging low in the sky, just about to dip behind Kettleness; the vivid red sunlight hit the East Cliff and the old abbey ruins, bathing the whole scene in a gorgeous rosy glow. We were standing there in silence when Lucy suddenly murmured to herself:

"His red eyes again! They are exactly the same." It was such a bizarre thing to say, coming completely out of nowhere, that it seriously startled me. I turned slightly so I could observe her without staring, and I saw that she was in a kind of daze. She had a strange expression on her face that I couldn't quite decipher, so I didn't say anything and just followed her gaze. She was staring straight across at our favorite bench, and there was a dark figure sitting there all alone. I was a bit rattled myself, because for a split second, it looked as though the stranger had massive eyes like blazing fire. But a second glance broke the illusion. The red sunset was simply glaring off the windows of St. Mary's Church directly behind our bench. As the sun sank lower, the angle of the light shifted just enough to make it look like the glow was moving. I pointed the optical illusion out to Lucy, and she snapped out of her daze with a start, though she still looked deeply sad. It's highly likely she was thinking about that horrific night up there. We never talk about it, so I kept my mouth shut, and we went home to eat. Lucy developed a headache and went to bed early. Once she was asleep, I went out for a quick walk by myself. I strolled westward along the cliffs, feeling a sweet melancholy as I thought of Jonathan. As I was walking back—the moonlight was incredibly bright, so even though the front of our building was in shadow, I could see everything clearly—I glanced up at our window and saw Lucy leaning out. Thinking she might be watching for me, I pulled out my handkerchief and waved. She didn't react or move a muscle. Just then, the angle of the moonlight shifted around the corner of the building and illuminated our window. I could see Lucy clearly, her head resting against the window frame and her eyes firmly shut. She was fast asleep. Right beside her, perched on the window sill, was something that looked like a very large bird. Panicking that she might catch a cold, I sprinted upstairs. But by the time I burst into the room, she was already stumbling back to bed, still fast asleep and breathing heavily. She was clutching her throat as if trying to keep it warm.

I didn't wake her, but I tucked her in snugly under the blankets. I double-checked that the door is locked and the window is firmly latched.

She looks so precious when she sleeps, but she is far paler than usual, and there are dark, exhausted circles under her eyes that really worry me. I'm afraid she is stressing over something. I desperately wish I could figure out what it is.

*       *       *       *       *

August 15. — We woke up later than usual. Lucy was sluggish and exhausted, and she fell right back asleep after the maid knocked. We received a wonderful surprise at breakfast. Arthur's father is doing much better, and he wants the wedding to happen soon. Lucy is quietly overjoyed, and her mother is feeling very bittersweet about it. Later in the day, Mrs. Westenra explained why. She is heartbroken to lose Lucy to another man, but thrilled that her daughter will soon have a husband to protect her. The poor, sweet woman! She confided in me that she is practically living under a death sentence. She hasn't told Lucy, and she made me swear not to tell; her doctor warned her that she only has a few months left to live, at most, because her heart is failing. Any sudden shock, even right now, would almost certainly kill her instantly. Thank goodness we decided to keep Lucy's terrifying sleepwalking adventure a secret from her.

*       *       *       *       *

August 17. — I haven't written in my diary for two whole days. I simply haven't had the heart for it. A dark cloud seems to be settling over our happiness. There is still no news from Jonathan, Lucy seems to be growing weaker by the day, and her mother's time on earth is rapidly running out. I cannot understand why Lucy is fading away like this. She eats perfectly well, sleeps deeply, and spends plenty of time in the fresh air, yet the rosy color is draining from her cheeks, and she becomes more exhausted and lethargic with every passing day. At night, I hear her gasping heavily, as if she is fighting for oxygen. I keep the key to our bedroom tied securely to my wrist every night, but she still gets out of bed, wanders around the room, and sits by the open window. Last night, I woke up to find her leaning entirely out the window. When I tried to rouse her, I couldn't; she had completely fainted. When I finally managed to bring her round, she was weak as water and wept silently between painful, agonizing gasps for breath. When I asked her how she ended up at the window, she just shook her head and looked away. I pray to God that her illness isn't an infection from that stupid safety pin prick. I checked her throat just a moment ago while she was sleeping, and the tiny puncture wounds don't seem to have healed at all. They are still open, and if anything, they look larger than before. The edges around them are a pale white. They look like two little white dots with red centers. If they don't heal in the next day or two, I'm going to insist that a doctor look at them.

Mina Murray's Journal.

August 18. — I am so happy today! I am writing this while sitting on our favorite bench in the graveyard. Lucy is doing so much better. She slept soundly through the night and didn't wake me once. Her cheeks are already getting their rosy color back, even though she still looks a little pale and tired. If she were anemic, it would make sense, but she isn't. She is in high spirits, full of life and energy. All of her strange, gloomy silence seems to have vanished. In fact, she just brought up that terrifying night completely on her own, reminding me that this very bench is where I found her asleep. As she was telling the story, she playfully tapped the heel of her boot against the stone tomb slab and said:

"My poor little bare feet didn't make much noise that night! I bet poor old Mr. Swales would have joked that I was walking on tiptoe because I didn't want to wake up Geordie inside the tomb." Because she was in such a talkative, lighthearted mood, I asked her if she had dreamed at all that night. Before answering, she got that sweet, thoughtful little wrinkle on her forehead—the one Arthur says he adores, and frankly, I don't blame him. Then she started speaking in a distant, dreamy voice, as if she were piecing the memories together for herself:

"It wasn't exactly a dream; it all felt entirely real. I just had this overwhelming urge to be right here, on this spot. I don't know why, because I was terrified of something, though I couldn't say what. I actually remember—even though I must have been asleep—walking through the streets and crossing the bridge. A fish jumped in the water as I passed, and I leaned over the rail to look at it. As I was climbing the stairs to the abbey, I heard dogs howling; it sounded like the entire town was filled with dogs, all howling at the exact same time. Then I have a blurry memory of something tall and dark with glowing red eyes, exactly like the optical illusion we saw at sunset. Suddenly, there was a smell all around me, something incredibly sweet but also terribly bitter. After that, I felt like I was sinking deep under green water, and I had a loud ringing in my ears—the kind people say you hear when you're drowning. Then everything just started to fade away. It felt like my soul was leaving my body and floating up into the air. I think I even remember looking down and seeing the West Lighthouse directly beneath me. Finally, there was this agonizing, violent sensation, like being caught in an earthquake, and I snapped back into my body and realized you were shaking me. Oddly enough, I saw you shaking me before I actually felt it."

Then she just started laughing. The whole story unsettled me deeply, and I held my breath while listening to her. I didn't like it one bit, and I decided it was best not to let her dwell on the subject, so I shifted the conversation to other things, and Lucy went right back to her normal, cheerful self. The brisk wind on the walk home really energized her, and her pale cheeks genuinely looked rosier. Her mother was thrilled to see her looking so well, and we all had a wonderful, happy evening together.

*       *       *       *       *

August 19. — Joy, joy, joy! Well, mostly joy. I finally got news of Jonathan! My poor darling has been terribly ill; that's why he hasn't written. Now that I know the truth, I'm not afraid to think about it or say it out loud. Mr. Hawkins forwarded a letter to me and included a very kind note of his own. I am leaving first thing in the morning to go to Jonathan, help nurse him back to health if needed, and bring him safely home. Mr. Hawkins even suggested it might be a good idea for us to just get married while I'm out there. I've been crying over the kind nun's letter so much that it's wet where I have it tucked inside my dress against my chest. It's about Jonathan, so it belongs next to my heart, because that's where he is. I have my entire journey planned out and my bags are packed. I'm only taking one change of clothes; Lucy is going to bring my main trunk to London and hold onto it until I send for it, just in case... No, I shouldn't write anymore; I need to save all my words for Jonathan, my husband. The letter that he has actually seen and touched will have to comfort me until I see him in person.

Letter from Sister Agatha, Hospital of St. Joseph and Ste. Mary, Budapest, to Miss Wilhelmina Murray.

"12 August.

"Dear Madam,—

"I am writing this on behalf of Mr. Jonathan Harker. He is still too weak to write himself, though he is recovering well, thanks be to God, St. Joseph, and Ste. Mary. He has been in our care for nearly six weeks, suffering from a severe brain fever. He asked me to send you his love. He also asked me to let you know that I am mailing another letter today to Mr. Peter Hawkins in Exeter, passing along his deepest respects, apologizing for the delay, and confirming that all his assigned work has been completed. He will need a few more weeks of rest at our mountain sanatorium, but he will return home after that. He wanted me to mention that he doesn't have enough money with him right now, but he fully intends to pay for his medical care so that our charity funds aren't drained and we can continue helping others in need.

"Believe me,

"Yours, with sympathy and all blessings,

"SISTER AGATHA.

"P.S. — My patient has fallen asleep, so I am reopening this envelope to share a few extra details with you. He has told me all about you, and that you two are soon to be married. May God bless you both! According to our doctor, he has suffered some kind of horrific trauma. When he was delirious with fever, his ravings were absolutely terrifying. He screamed about wolves, poison, and blood; about ghosts and demons; and things I am too frightened to even write down. Please be very careful to keep him away from anything that might trigger these memories for a long time. The psychological scars from an illness like this do not fade easily. We would have written to you much sooner, but we had no idea who his friends or family were, and there was nothing in his belongings that gave us any clues. He arrived here on a train from Klausenburg. The train conductor told us that the local stationmaster said he ran into the station screaming and demanding a ticket home. Seeing that he was English and acting completely frantic, the station staff just sold him a ticket for the furthest stop on the line heading in that general direction.

"Please rest assured that he is receiving excellent care. He has won over everyone here with his sweet and gentle nature. He is making genuine progress, and I am confident that in a few weeks, he will be completely back to his old self. But please, for his own safety, treat him gently. I pray to God, St. Joseph, and Ste. Mary that you both have many, many happy years ahead of you."

Dr. Seward's Diary.

August 19. — There was a strange and sudden change in Renfield last night. Around eight o'clock, he became highly agitated, sniffing the air like a hunting dog catching a scent. The ward attendant was so surprised by his behavior—and knowing how interested I am in this case—that he encouraged Renfield to talk. He is usually very polite, sometimes even subservient, to the attendant, but last night, the man told me, Renfield was incredibly arrogant. He completely refused to speak to him. The only thing he would say was:

"I don't want to talk to you. You don't matter anymore; the Master is here."

The attendant believes Renfield has been gripped by a sudden onset of religious mania. If that's the case, we need to brace for a storm; a powerful man suffering from both homicidal and religious mania could be incredibly dangerous. It's a terrifying combination. I went to visit him myself at nine o'clock. He treated me with the exact same arrogance he showed the attendant; trapped in his delusions of grandeur, he saw absolutely no difference between a doctor and a guard. It certainly looks like religious mania, and it won't be long before he decides he is God. To an omnipotent being, the minor differences in human social status are meaningless. It's amazing how these lunatics reveal their true nature! The real God notices even a sparrow falling to the ground, but a god created from human arrogance sees no difference between an eagle and a sparrow. Oh, if only people understood!

For over half an hour, Renfield grew more and more excited. I pretended not to pay attention, but I kept a very close eye on him. Suddenly, that shifty, cunning look flashed in his eyes—the one we always see when a madman locks onto a fixed idea. Along with it came the jerky, restless movements of the head and shoulders that asylum workers know so well. Then he fell completely silent, walked over to his bed, and sat on the edge in a state of resignation, staring blankly into space. I wanted to test whether this sudden apathy was genuine or an act, so I tried to bring up his pets, a topic that usually never fails to get his attention. At first, he ignored me, but finally, he snapped:

"To hell with them all! I don't care about them in the slightest."

"What?" I said. "Are you honestly telling me you don't care about your spiders anymore?" (Spiders are his current obsession, and he has been filling his notebook with columns of tiny calculations about them.) To this, he gave a cryptic reply:

"The bridesmaids are delighted to wait for the bride to arrive; but when the bride actually approaches, the bridesmaids no longer shine to eyes that are already filled with her glory."

He refused to explain what he meant, and he just sat stubbornly on his bed for the rest of my visit.

I am exhausted and depressed tonight. I can't stop thinking about Lucy, and how completely different my life could have been. If I can't fall asleep right away, I'll have to turn to chloral hydrate, our modern god of sleep—C{2}HCl{3}O·H{2}O! I have to be careful not to make a habit of it. Actually, no, I won't take any tonight! I've been thinking of Lucy, and I won't disrespect her memory by mixing it with drugs. If I have to, I will just stay awake all night....

*       *       *       *       *

Later. — I'm glad I made that decision, and even gladder that I stuck to it. I had been tossing and turning, and the clock had only chimed twice, when the night watchman came up from the ward to tell me that Renfield had escaped. I threw my clothes on and sprinted downstairs immediately; he is far too dangerous to be wandering around loose. His delusions could turn violent if he runs into strangers. The attendant was waiting for me. He said he had checked on Renfield through the observation window in the door less than ten minutes earlier, and the man appeared to be fast asleep. Then he heard the sound of the metal window frame being wrenched out of the wall. He ran back and saw Renfield's feet disappearing out the window, so he sent for me right away. Renfield is only wearing his nightclothes, so he can't have gotten far. The attendant decided it was smarter to watch which way he ran rather than chasing him directly; if he had run down the hall to go out the main doors, he might have lost sight of him in the dark. The attendant is a heavily built man and couldn't fit through the broken window. I'm thin, so with his help, I squeezed through feet-first. Since we are only on the ground floor, I dropped down safely. The attendant shouted that Renfield had sprinted to the left in a straight line, so I took off running as fast as I could. As I broke through the line of trees, I saw a pale figure scrambling over the high stone wall that separates our asylum grounds from the abandoned Carfax estate.

I immediately ran back, ordered the watchman to grab three or four orderlies, and told them to follow me into the Carfax grounds in case our friend turned violent. I grabbed a ladder, climbed over the wall, and dropped into the neighboring yard. I caught a glimpse of Renfield disappearing around the corner of the massive old house, so I chased after him. On the far side of the building, I found him pressed flat against the heavy, iron-bound oak doors of the old chapel. He was talking—apparently speaking to someone—but I was too scared to get close enough to hear what he was saying. I didn't want to startle him into running off again. Trying to catch a frantic swarm of bees is nothing compared to trying to corner a naked lunatic who is determined to escape! After a few minutes, though, I realized he was completely oblivious to his surroundings, so I crept closer. I felt a bit braver because my men had made it over the wall and were slowly closing in on him. I heard him pleading:

"I am here to do Your bidding, Master. I am Your slave, and You will reward me, because I will be faithful. I have worshipped You for a long time from afar. Now that You are near, I await Your commands. You won't pass me over, will You, dear Master, when You hand out Your rewards?"

He's a selfish old beggar, really. He's still begging for handouts even when he fully believes he's standing in the presence of a god. His multiple obsessions create a truly bizarre mindset. When we finally rushed him, he fought like a tiger. His strength is unbelievable; he fought more like a wild beast than a human being. I have never seen a psychiatric patient fly into such a violent, blinding rage, and I hope I never have to again. Thank God we discovered how strong and dangerous he truly is before it was too late. With that level of brute strength and single-minded determination, he could have slaughtered someone if he hadn't been locked up. He's secure now, though. Even the great escape artist Jack Sheppard couldn't break out of the straitjacket we have him strapped into, and he is chained to the wall in a padded isolation cell. His screams are sometimes horrifying, but the dead silences between them are even worse, because you can tell he is plotting murder with every twitch of his muscles.

He just spoke his first coherent sentence since the fight:

"I will be patient, Master. It is coming—coming—coming!"

I decided to take the hint, and I came back to my room. I was too pumped full of adrenaline to sleep, but writing this diary entry has calmed my nerves, and I think I'll be able to get some rest tonight.
