[P001] CHAPTER VIII

[P002] MINA MURRAY’S JOURNAL

[P003] Same day, 11 p.m. — Oh, I am so exhausted! If keeping this diary wasn't a strict duty, I wouldn't even open it tonight. We went for a lovely walk. Lucy cheered up quickly, all thanks to some cute cows in a field near the lighthouse that came sniffing up to us and scared us half to death. I think that sudden fright wiped our minds clear of everything else and gave us a fresh start. We enjoyed a fantastic "severe tea"—a hearty late-afternoon meal that serves as dinner—at a charming, old-fashioned inn in Robin Hood's Bay.

[P004] It had a lovely bow-window jutting out right over the seaweed-covered rocks on the shore. I'm sure our massive appetites would have shocked the modern "New Women. " Thank goodness men are much more forgiving about that sort of thing! On our walk back home, we had to stop frequently to rest, and we were constantly terrified that wild bulls might charge at us. Lucy was completely worn out, and our plan was to sneak off to bed the moment we got in. However, the young curate stopped by, and Mrs. Westenra invited him to stay for supper.

[P005] Lucy and I had to fight desperately to keep our eyes open; it took a heroic effort on my part. I really think bishops should start training a new breed of curates who decline late suppers, no matter how much they are pressured, and who can tell when girls are tired. Lucy is fast asleep now, breathing softly. Her cheeks have more color than usual, and she looks incredibly sweet. If Mr. Holmwood fell in love with her just by seeing her in a formal drawing-room, I can only imagine what he would say if he saw her like this.

[P006] Maybe someday those "New Woman" writers will argue that men and women should see each other asleep before they propose or accept. Though honestly, I doubt the New Woman of the future will wait around to accept a proposal; she'll probably do the proposing herself. And she'll probably do a great job of it, too! That's somewhat comforting. I am so happy tonight because my dear Lucy seems to be doing much better. I truly believe she has turned a corner and that her sleepwalking troubles are behind us. I would be completely happy if only I had word from Jonathan.... God bless him and keep him safe.

[P007] *       *       *       *       *

[P008] August 11, 3 a.m. — Back to the diary. I can't sleep now, so I might as well write. I am far too agitated. We have just been through the most agonizing, terrifying ordeal. I fell asleep the moment I closed my diary earlier.... Then, quite suddenly, I jerked awake. I sat up in bed feeling a horrific sense of dread and an eerie emptiness in the room. It was pitch black, so I couldn't see Lucy's bed. I crept over and felt around in the dark. The bed was empty. I struck a match and confirmed she was not in the room. The door was shut, but it wasn't locked the way I had left it.

[P009] I didn't want to wake her mother, who has been feeling worse than usual lately, so I threw on some clothes to go look for her. As I was leaving the room, it occurred to me that the clothes she chose to wear might indicate where her sleeping mind intended to go. Her dressing-gown would mean she stayed inside; her street dress would mean she went outside. Both were still in the closet. "Thank God," I told myself, "she can't have gone far if she's only in her nightgown. " I ran downstairs and checked the sitting room. Empty!

[P010] I checked all the other open rooms in the house, my panic growing with every second. Finally, I reached the front door and found it unlatched. It wasn't swung wide open, but the lock hadn't caught. The people who own the house are very strict about locking up every night, so I realized with horror that Lucy must have wandered outside exactly as she was. There was no time to consider the consequences; a blind, overwhelming panic took over. I grabbed a large, heavy shawl and ran out into the night. The clock was striking 1:00 a.m. as I reached the Crescent, and the streets were completely deserted.

[P011] I sprinted down the North Terrace but saw no sign of the white figure I was looking for. Standing on the edge of the West Cliff, above the pier, I looked across the harbor toward the East Cliff. I felt a mix of hope and fear that I might spot Lucy at our favorite bench. The full moon was bright, but heavy, dark clouds were moving fast across the sky, turning the landscape into a flashing, shifting scene of light and shadow. For a moment, a cloud shadowed St. Mary's Church, and I couldn't see anything.

[P012] But as the cloud shifted, the abbey ruins came into view, and a narrow, sharp beam of moonlight illuminated the church and the graveyard. My instincts were right: there, on our favorite bench, illuminated by the silver moonlight, was a figure in snowy white, half-reclining. The clouds moved too fast for me to get a clear look before the shadow returned, but I thought I saw something dark standing behind the bench, leaning over the white figure. I couldn't tell if it was a man or an animal.

[P013] I didn't wait for another look; I flew down the steep stairs to the pier, ran past the fish market, and crossed the bridge—the only route to the East Cliff. The town was dead quiet without a single soul in sight, which relieved me, as I didn't want anyone to see poor Lucy in this state. The distance felt infinite. My knees were shaking and I was gasping for air as I struggled up the endless stairs toward the abbey. I must have been moving fast, but it felt like my feet were made of lead and every joint in my body was rusted shut.

[P014] As I neared the top, I was close enough to see the bench and the white figure clearly, even through the passing shadows. There was, without a doubt, a long, black shape leaning over the reclining white figure. I screamed in terror, "Lucy! Lucy! " The dark shape raised its head, and from where I stood, I saw a pale face and glowing red eyes. Lucy didn't answer, and I kept running toward the graveyard entrance. As I ran through the gates, the church blocked my view of the bench for a minute. When I could see it again, the cloud had passed.

[P015] In the brilliant moonlight, I saw Lucy leaning back against the bench. She was entirely alone, and there was no sign of any living thing nearby.

[P016] When I leaned over her, I realized she was still fast asleep. Her lips were parted, and instead of her usual quiet breathing, she was taking long, heavy, gasping breaths, as if she was struggling to fill her lungs. As I leaned closer, she weakly raised her hand in her sleep and clutched the collar of her nightgown tightly around her throat. A sudden shiver ran through her, as though the cold had just hit her. I immediately wrapped the thick, warm shawl around her and pulled it snug against her neck, terrified she might catch a deadly chill from the night air in only her thin nightgown.

[P017] I didn't want to startle her awake, so to keep my hands free to support her, I pinned the shawl at her throat with a large safety pin. I must have been shaking from anxiety, though, because I clumsily pinched or pricked her skin with it. A moment later, as her breathing calmed, she reached for her throat again and let out a soft moan. Once she was securely bundled up, I forced my own shoes onto her feet and gently began to wake her. She didn't respond at first, but she grew increasingly restless, moaning and sighing.

[P018] Knowing we needed to get out of there quickly for a dozen reasons, I finally shook her harder until she opened her eyes. She didn't seem shocked to see me, mostly because she hadn't yet grasped where she was. Lucy always wakes up gracefully, and even in this terrifying situation—freezing cold and waking up half-dressed in a graveyard—she didn't lose her composure. Trembling slightly, she clung to my arm. When I told her we had to go home immediately, she stood up without a word, obedient as a child. As we walked, the gravel path cut into my bare feet, and I winced.

[P019] Lucy noticed, stopped, and tried to insist I take my shoes back, but I refused. However, when we reached a mud puddle left over from the storm just outside the graveyard, I deliberately smeared mud all over my feet. That way, if we happened to pass anyone on the way home, my bare skin wouldn't be as obvious in the dark.

[P020] Luck was on our side, and we made it all the way home without crossing paths with a single person. At one point, we did see a man who looked pretty drunk stumbling down the street ahead of us, but we ducked into a doorway and waited until he disappeared up one of the steep, narrow alleys they call "wynds" in Scotland. My heart was pounding so violently the whole time that I honestly thought I was going to pass out. I was sick with worry for Lucy—both that the freezing exposure might ruin her health, and that her reputation would be destroyed if anyone found out about this.

[P021] When we finally got inside, washed our feet, and said a quick prayer of thanks, I tucked her safely into bed. Just before she drifted off, she begged—absolutely pleaded with me—not to breathe a word of her sleepwalking adventure to anyone, not even her mother. I was hesitant to promise at first. But when I thought about how fragile Mrs. Westenra's heart is, how this news would distress her, and how viciously the story would inevitably be twisted if it ever leaked to the public, I decided it was best to keep the secret. I pray I made the right choice.

[P022] I've locked the bedroom door and tied the key to my wrist, so hopefully, there won't be any more disturbances tonight. Lucy is sleeping deeply, and the first light of dawn is just beginning to break over the ocean....

[P023] *       *       *       *       *

[P024] Same day, noon. — Everything is perfectly fine. Lucy slept deeply until I finally woke her, and she didn't even seem to have rolled over in the night. That terrifying midnight adventure doesn't appear to have harmed her at all; if anything, it actually helped, because she looks healthier this morning than she has in weeks. I felt terrible, though, when I realized that my clumsy fumbling with the safety pin had actually hurt her. In fact, it could have been bad, because the skin on her neck was actually pierced.

[P025] I must have grabbed a loose fold of skin and run the pin straight through it, because there are two tiny red dots, like pinpricks, and a single drop of blood on the collar of her nightgown. When I apologized profusely and fussed over it, she just laughed, patted my hand, and insisted she hadn't felt a thing. Luckily, the marks are so tiny that they won't leave a scar.

[P026] *       *       *       *       *

[P027] Same day, night. — We had a lovely day. The air was crisp, the sun was bright, and there was a refreshing breeze. We packed a lunch and went to Mulgrave Woods. Mrs. Westenra took the carriage up the main road, while Lucy and I walked along the cliff path and met her at the gates. I felt a pang of sadness myself, wishing Jonathan could have been there to make the day absolutely perfect. But I just have to be patient! In the evening, we took a stroll along the Casino Terrace, listened to some excellent music by Spohr and Mackenzie, and turned in early.

[P028] Lucy seems more relaxed than she has been in a long time, and she fell asleep the moment her head hit the pillow. I'm going to lock the door and tie the key to my wrist exactly as I did last night, even though I don't anticipate any problems tonight.

[P029] *       *       *       *       *

[P030] _12 August._--My expectations were wrong, for twice during the night I
was wakened by Lucy trying to get out. She seemed, even in her sleep, to
be a little impatient at finding the door shut, and went back to bed
under a sort of protest. I woke with the dawn, and heard the birds
chirping outside of the window. Lucy woke, too, and, I was glad to see,
was even better than on the previous morning. All her old gaiety of
manner seemed to have come back, and she came and snuggled in beside me
and told me all about Arthur. I told her how anxious I was about
Jonathan, and then she tried to comfort me. Well, she succeeded
somewhat, for, though sympathy can’t alter facts, it can help to make
them more bearable.

[P031] *       *       *       *       *

[P032] August 13. — Another peaceful day, and then back to bed with the key firmly attached to my wrist. I woke up in the middle of the night again to find Lucy sitting bolt upright in bed, completely asleep, pointing at the window. I slipped out of bed quietly, pulled back the blind, and looked outside. The moonlight was brilliant, and the soft glow washing over the ocean and the sky—blending them into one vast, silent mystery—was breathtakingly beautiful. Right between me and the moonlight, a massive bat fluttered by, flying in large, frantic circles.

[P033] It swooped close to the glass once or twice, but I suppose it got scared when it saw me, because it fluttered off across the harbor toward the abbey. When I turned back from the window, Lucy had laid down again and was sleeping soundly. She didn't move an inch for the rest of the night.

[P034] *       *       *       *       *

[P035] _14 August._--On the East Cliff, reading and writing all day. Lucy seems
to have become as much in love with the spot as I am, and it is hard to
get her away from it when it is time to come home for lunch or tea or
dinner. This afternoon she made a funny remark. We were coming home for
dinner, and had come to the top of the steps up from the West Pier and
stopped to look at the view, as we generally do. The setting sun, low
down in the sky, was just dropping behind Kettleness; the red light was
thrown over on the East Cliff and the old abbey, and seemed to bathe
everything in a beautiful rosy glow. We were silent for a while, and
suddenly Lucy murmured as if to herself:--

[P036] "His red eyes again! They are exactly the same. " It was such a bizarre thing to say, coming completely out of nowhere, that it seriously startled me. I turned slightly so I could observe her without staring, and I saw that she was in a kind of daze. She had a strange expression on her face that I couldn't quite decipher, so I didn't say anything and just followed her gaze. She was staring straight across at our favorite bench, and there was a dark figure sitting there all alone.

[P037] I was a bit rattled myself, because for a split second, it looked as though the stranger had massive eyes like blazing fire. But a second glance broke the illusion. The red sunset was simply glaring off the windows of St. Mary's Church directly behind our bench. As the sun sank lower, the angle of the light shifted just enough to make it look like the glow was moving. I pointed the optical illusion out to Lucy, and she snapped out of her daze with a start, though she still looked deeply sad. It's highly likely she was thinking about that horrific night up there.

[P038] We never talk about it, so I kept my mouth shut, and we went home to eat. Lucy developed a headache and went to bed early. Once she was asleep, I went out for a quick walk by myself. I strolled westward along the cliffs, feeling a sweet melancholy as I thought of Jonathan. As I was walking back—the moonlight was incredibly bright, so even though the front of our building was in shadow, I could see everything clearly—I glanced up at our window and saw Lucy leaning out. Thinking she might be watching for me, I pulled out my handkerchief and waved. She didn't react or move a muscle.

[P039] Just then, the angle of the moonlight shifted around the corner of the building and illuminated our window. I could see Lucy clearly, her head resting against the window frame and her eyes firmly shut. She was fast asleep. Right beside her, perched on the window sill, was something that looked like a very large bird. Panicking that she might catch a cold, I sprinted upstairs. But by the time I burst into the room, she was already stumbling back to bed, still fast asleep and breathing heavily. She was clutching her throat as if trying to keep it warm.

[P040] I did not wake her, but tucked her up warmly; I have taken care that the
door is locked and the window securely fastened.

[P041] She looks so sweet as she sleeps; but she is paler than is her wont, and
there is a drawn, haggard look under her eyes which I do not like. I
fear she is fretting about something. I wish I could find out what it
is.

[P042] *       *       *       *       *

[P043] August 15. — We woke up later than usual. Lucy was sluggish and exhausted, and she fell right back asleep after the maid knocked. We received a wonderful surprise at breakfast. Arthur's father is doing much better, and he wants the wedding to happen soon. Lucy is quietly overjoyed, and her mother is feeling very bittersweet about it. Later in the day, Mrs. Westenra explained why. She is heartbroken to lose Lucy to another man, but thrilled that her daughter will soon have a husband to protect her. The poor, sweet woman! She confided in me that she is practically living under a death sentence.

[P044] She hasn't told Lucy, and she made me swear not to tell; her doctor warned her that she only has a few months left to live, at most, because her heart is failing. Any sudden shock, even right now, would almost certainly kill her instantly. Thank goodness we decided to keep Lucy's terrifying sleepwalking adventure a secret from her.

[P045] *       *       *       *       *

[P046] August 17. — I haven't written in my diary for two whole days. I simply haven't had the heart for it. A dark cloud seems to be settling over our happiness. There is still no news from Jonathan, Lucy seems to be growing weaker by the day, and her mother's time on earth is rapidly running out. I cannot understand why Lucy is fading away like this. She eats perfectly well, sleeps deeply, and spends plenty of time in the fresh air, yet the rosy color is draining from her cheeks, and she becomes more exhausted and lethargic with every passing day.

[P047] At night, I hear her gasping heavily, as if she is fighting for oxygen. I keep the key to our bedroom tied securely to my wrist every night, but she still gets out of bed, wanders around the room, and sits by the open window. Last night, I woke up to find her leaning entirely out the window. When I tried to rouse her, I couldn't; she had completely fainted. When I finally managed to bring her round, she was weak as water and wept silently between painful, agonizing gasps for breath. When I asked her how she ended up at the window, she just shook her head and looked away.

[P048] I pray to God that her illness isn't an infection from that stupid safety pin prick. I checked her throat just a moment ago while she was sleeping, and the tiny puncture wounds don't seem to have healed at all. They are still open, and if anything, they look larger than before. The edges around them are a pale white. They look like two little white dots with red centers. If they don't heal in the next day or two, I'm going to insist that a doctor look at them.

[P066] _Mina Murray’s Journal._

[P067] _18 August._--I am happy to-day, and write sitting on the seat in the
churchyard. Lucy is ever so much better. Last night she slept well all
night, and did not disturb me once. The roses seem coming back already
to her cheeks, though she is still sadly pale and wan-looking. If she
were in any way anæmic I could understand it, but she is not. She is in
gay spirits and full of life and cheerfulness. All the morbid reticence
seems to have passed from her, and she has just reminded me, as if I
needed any reminding, of _that_ night, and that it was here, on this
very seat, I found her asleep. As she told me she tapped playfully with
the heel of her boot on the stone slab and said:--

[P068] “My poor little feet didn’t make much noise then! I daresay poor old Mr.
Swales would have told me that it was because I didn’t want to wake up
Geordie.” As she was in such a communicative humour, I asked her if she
had dreamed at all that night. Before she answered, that sweet, puckered
look came into her forehead, which Arthur--I call him Arthur from her
habit--says he loves; and, indeed, I don’t wonder that he does. Then she
went on in a half-dreaming kind of way, as if trying to recall it to
herself:--

[P069] “I didn’t quite dream; but it all seemed to be real. I only wanted to be
here in this spot--I don’t know why, for I was afraid of something--I
don’t know what. I remember, though I suppose I was asleep, passing
through the streets and over the bridge. A fish leaped as I went by, and
I leaned over to look at it, and I heard a lot of dogs howling--the
whole town seemed as if it must be full of dogs all howling at once--as
I went up the steps. Then I had a vague memory of something long and
dark with red eyes, just as we saw in the sunset, and something very
sweet and very bitter all around me at once; and then I seemed sinking
into deep green water, and there was a singing in my ears, as I have
heard there is to drowning men; and then everything seemed passing away
from me; my soul seemed to go out from my body and float about the air.
I seem to remember that once the West Lighthouse was right under me,
and then there was a sort of agonising feeling, as if I were in an
earthquake, and I came back and found you shaking my body. I saw you do
it before I felt you.”

[P070] Then she began to laugh. It seemed a little uncanny to me, and I
listened to her breathlessly. I did not quite like it, and thought it
better not to keep her mind on the subject, so we drifted on to other
subjects, and Lucy was like her old self again. When we got home the
fresh breeze had braced her up, and her pale cheeks were really more
rosy. Her mother rejoiced when she saw her, and we all spent a very
happy evening together.

[P071] *       *       *       *       *

[P072] _19 August._--Joy, joy, joy! although not all joy. At last, news of
Jonathan. The dear fellow has been ill; that is why he did not write. I
am not afraid to think it or say it, now that I know. Mr. Hawkins sent
me on the letter, and wrote himself, oh, so kindly. I am to leave in the
morning and go over to Jonathan, and to help to nurse him if necessary,
and to bring him home. Mr. Hawkins says it would not be a bad thing if
we were to be married out there. I have cried over the good Sister’s
letter till I can feel it wet against my bosom, where it lies. It is of
Jonathan, and must be next my heart, for he is _in_ my heart. My journey
is all mapped out, and my luggage ready. I am only taking one change of
dress; Lucy will bring my trunk to London and keep it till I send for
it, for it may be that ... I must write no more; I must keep it to say
to Jonathan, my husband. The letter that he has seen and touched must
comfort me till we meet.

[P073] _Letter, Sister Agatha, Hospital of St. Joseph and Ste. Mary,
Buda-Pesth, to Miss Wilhelmina Murray._

[P074] “_12 August._

[P075] “Dear Madam,--

[P076] “I write by desire of Mr. Jonathan Harker, who is himself not strong
enough to write, though progressing well, thanks to God and St. Joseph
and Ste. Mary. He has been under our care for nearly six weeks,
suffering from a violent brain fever. He wishes me to convey his love,
and to say that by this post I write for him to Mr. Peter Hawkins,
Exeter, to say, with his dutiful respects, that he is sorry for his
delay, and that all of his work is completed. He will require some few
weeks’ rest in our sanatorium in the hills, but will then return. He
wishes me to say that he has not sufficient money with him, and that he
would like to pay for his staying here, so that others who need shall
not be wanting for help.

[P077] “Believe me,

[P078] “Yours, with sympathy and all blessings,

[P079] “SISTER AGATHA.

[P080] “P. S.--My patient being asleep, I open this to let you know something
more. He has told me all about you, and that you are shortly to be his
wife. All blessings to you both! He has had some fearful shock--so says
our doctor--and in his delirium his ravings have been dreadful; of
wolves and poison and blood; of ghosts and demons; and I fear to say of
what. Be careful with him always that there may be nothing to excite him
of this kind for a long time to come; the traces of such an illness as
his do not lightly die away. We should have written long ago, but we
knew nothing of his friends, and there was on him nothing that any one
could understand. He came in the train from Klausenburg, and the guard
was told by the station-master there that he rushed into the station
shouting for a ticket for home. Seeing from his violent demeanour that
he was English, they gave him a ticket for the furthest station on the
way thither that the train reached.

[P081] “Be assured that he is well cared for. He has won all hearts by his
sweetness and gentleness. He is truly getting on well, and I have no
doubt will in a few weeks be all himself. But be careful of him for
safety’s sake. There are, I pray God and St. Joseph and Ste. Mary, many,
many, happy years for you both.”

[P082] _Dr. Seward’s Diary._

[P083] _19 August._--Strange and sudden change in Renfield last night. About
eight o’clock he began to get excited and sniff about as a dog does when
setting. The attendant was struck by his manner, and knowing my interest
in him, encouraged him to talk. He is usually respectful to the
attendant and at times servile; but to-night, the man tells me, he was
quite haughty. Would not condescend to talk with him at all. All he
would say was:--

[P084] “I don’t want to talk to you: you don’t count now; the Master is at
     hand.”

[P085] The attendant thinks it is some sudden form of religious mania which has
seized him. If so, we must look out for squalls, for a strong man with
homicidal and religious mania at once might be dangerous. The
combination is a dreadful one. At nine o’clock I visited him myself. His
attitude to me was the same as that to the attendant; in his sublime
self-feeling the difference between myself and attendant seemed to him
as nothing. It looks like religious mania, and he will soon think that
he himself is God. These infinitesimal distinctions between man and man
are too paltry for an Omnipotent Being. How these madmen give themselves
away! The real God taketh heed lest a sparrow fall; but the God created
from human vanity sees no difference between an eagle and a sparrow. Oh,
if men only knew!

[P086] For half an hour or more Renfield kept getting excited in greater and
greater degree. I did not pretend to be watching him, but I kept strict
observation all the same. All at once that shifty look came into his
eyes which we always see when a madman has seized an idea, and with it
the shifty movement of the head and back which asylum attendants come to
know so well. He became quite quiet, and went and sat on the edge of his
bed resignedly, and looked into space with lack-lustre eyes. I thought I
would find out if his apathy were real or only assumed, and tried to
lead him to talk of his pets, a theme which had never failed to excite
his attention. At first he made no reply, but at length said testily:--

[P087] “Bother them all! I don’t care a pin about them.”

[P088] “What?” I said. “You don’t mean to tell me you don’t care about
spiders?” (Spiders at present are his hobby and the note-book is filling
up with columns of small figures.) To this he answered enigmatically:--

[P089] “The bride-maidens rejoice the eyes that wait the coming of the bride;
but when the bride draweth nigh, then the maidens shine not to the eyes
that are filled.”

[P090] He would not explain himself, but remained obstinately seated on his bed
all the time I remained with him.

[P091] I am weary to-night and low in spirits. I cannot but think of Lucy, and
how different things might have been. If I don’t sleep at once, chloral,
the modern Morpheus--C_{2}HCl_{3}O. H_{2}O! I must be careful not to let
it grow into a habit. No, I shall take none to-night! I have thought of
Lucy, and I shall not dishonour her by mixing the two. If need be,
to-night shall be sleepless....

[P092] *       *       *       *       *

[P093] _Later._--Glad I made the resolution; gladder that I kept to it. I had
lain tossing about, and had heard the clock strike only twice, when the
night-watchman came to me, sent up from the ward, to say that Renfield
had escaped. I threw on my clothes and ran down at once; my patient is
too dangerous a person to be roaming about. Those ideas of his might
work out dangerously with strangers. The attendant was waiting for me.
He said he had seen him not ten minutes before, seemingly asleep in his
bed, when he had looked through the observation-trap in the door. His
attention was called by the sound of the window being wrenched out. He
ran back and saw his feet disappear through the window, and had at once
sent up for me. He was only in his night-gear, and cannot be far off.
The attendant thought it would be more useful to watch where he should
go than to follow him, as he might lose sight of him whilst getting out
of the building by the door. He is a bulky man, and couldn’t get through
the window. I am thin, so, with his aid, I got out, but feet foremost,
and, as we were only a few feet above ground, landed unhurt. The
attendant told me the patient had gone to the left, and had taken a
straight line, so I ran as quickly as I could. As I got through the belt
of trees I saw a white figure scale the high wall which separates our
grounds from those of the deserted house.

[P094] I ran back at once, told the watchman to get three or four men
immediately and follow me into the grounds of Carfax, in case our friend
might be dangerous. I got a ladder myself, and crossing the wall,
dropped down on the other side. I could see Renfield’s figure just
disappearing behind the angle of the house, so I ran after him. On the
far side of the house I found him pressed close against the old
ironbound oak door of the chapel. He was talking, apparently to some
one, but I was afraid to go near enough to hear what he was saying, lest
I might frighten him, and he should run off. Chasing an errant swarm of
bees is nothing to following a naked lunatic, when the fit of escaping
is upon him! After a few minutes, however, I could see that he did not
take note of anything around him, and so ventured to draw nearer to
him--the more so as my men had now crossed the wall and were closing him
in. I heard him say:--

[P095] “I am here to do Your bidding, Master. I am Your slave, and You will
reward me, for I shall be faithful. I have worshipped You long and afar
off. Now that You are near, I await Your commands, and You will not pass
me by, will You, dear Master, in Your distribution of good things?”

[P096] He _is_ a selfish old beggar anyhow. He thinks of the loaves and fishes
even when he believes he is in a Real Presence. His manias make a
startling combination. When we closed in on him he fought like a tiger.
He is immensely strong, for he was more like a wild beast than a man. I
never saw a lunatic in such a paroxysm of rage before; and I hope I
shall not again. It is a mercy that we have found out his strength and
his danger in good time. With strength and determination like his, he
might have done wild work before he was caged. He is safe now at any
rate. Jack Sheppard himself couldn’t get free from the strait-waistcoat
that keeps him restrained, and he’s chained to the wall in the padded
room. His cries are at times awful, but the silences that follow are
more deadly still, for he means murder in every turn and movement.

[P097] Just now he spoke coherent words for the first time:--

[P098] “I shall be patient, Master. It is coming--coming--coming!”

[P099] So I took the hint, and came too. I was too excited to sleep, but this
diary has quieted me, and I feel I shall get some sleep to-night.
