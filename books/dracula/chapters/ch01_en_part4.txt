"No, absolutely not," he said flatly. "You shouldn't walk around carelessly here. The dogs are incredibly fierce." He threw out a grim joke and glanced back, as if hoping the passengers would laugh. "You'll see more than enough of those beasts before you go to bed, anyway." Except for a brief pause to light the carriage lamps, the driver whipped the horses relentlessly.

As total darkness fell, a tight tension filled the passengers. They took turns shouting at the driver, urging him to drive the carriage even faster. The driver lashed the horses with his long whip and let out wild, savage yells to pick up speed. Far off in the darkness, a faint ray of gray light began to peek through a crack in the mountains. The passengers' excitement reached its peak. The old carriage shook violently on its thick leather springs, tossing around like a tiny boat caught in a fierce storm. I had to hold on for dear life just to keep from falling out. Once the bumpy road smoothed out, the carriage glided forward as if it had wings. The massive mountain ranges pressed in close on both sides, looking down on us threateningly. We had finally entered the Borgo Pass.

The passengers began aggressively pressing gifts into my hands. They were so desperate that I couldn't refuse. The types of gifts were incredibly bizarre and varied, but the sincere look in their eyes was all the same. They repeated the strange gesture I had seen outside the inn in Bistritz—crossing themselves and making the two-finger sign to ward off the "evil eye"—while pouring out words of blessing. As the carriage raced on, the driver leaned far forward, and the passengers on either side stretched their necks out of the carriage, staring intently into the pitch-black darkness. It was obvious that something huge was about to happen, but no one would clearly explain it to me.

Amidst the bursting tension, the mountain pass finally opened up to the east. Dark storm clouds swirled overhead, and the heavy, murky air pressed down as if lightning would strike at any moment. It felt as if this mountain range divided two separate worlds, and we had been sucked into the heart of a massive storm. I looked out the window to find the place where the Count's carriage was supposed to be waiting. I expected to see the flash of a lamp cutting through the pitch-black darkness, but the world was nothing but black. The only things visible were the white breath steaming from our wildly galloping horses and the flickering light of our carriage lamp illuminating that breath.

Now, a long dirt road of white sand stretched out before us, but there were no tracks of any carriage on it. The passengers sighed in relief and leaned back. Their sighs somehow felt like they were mocking my disappointment, which upset me. As I was wondering what to do next, the driver looked at his pocket watch and muttered to himself, "We're an hour earlier than expected." Then he spoke to me in very poor German.

"There is no carriage. It seems they didn't know you were coming. You had better come with us to Bukovina and return tomorrow or the day after—yes, the day after tomorrow would be best." Before the driver could even finish his sentence, the horses suddenly snorted, blew air from their noses, and stepped backward. The driver gripped the reins tightly and broke a sweat trying to control them. Then, amidst the screams of the peasants and the chaos of them frantically crossing themselves, a covered carriage pulled by four horses suddenly appeared out of the darkness. It caught up to us in a flash and stopped right beside our stagecoach. In the light of the lamp, the horses were magnificent, pitch-black and shiny like coal. The driver was a tall man with a long brown beard, wearing a wide-brimmed black hat pulled down low so that his face was almost invisible. The only things that could be seen were his two eyes, glowing a terrifying red in the lamp light. He spoke to our driver.

"You are early tonight, my friend." Our driver stammered in reply.

"The English gentleman was in such a hurry." Then the stranger shot back.

"So you planned to take him all the way to Bukovina. Don't try to fool me, friend. I know everything, and my horses are faster than the wind." As he gave a cold smile, his sharp teeth, white as ivory, flashed in the light between his tightly closed lips. One of the passengers whispered a line from Gottfried August Bürger's poem "Lenore" to the person sitting next to him.

"For the dead travel fast (Denn die Todten reiten schnell)."

The strange driver seemed to have heard this perfectly well. With a chilling smile, he raised his head. The passenger who had recited the poem was startled and quickly crossed himself, making the sign to ward off the "evil eye." "Hand over the guest's luggage," the strange driver commanded. The luggage was moved to the covered carriage in an instant. I carefully climbed down the side of the stagecoach, and the new driver grabbed my arm with a grip as strong as steel tongs. He had incredible strength. Without a word, he shook the reins, and the horses turned and darted straight into the deep darkness of the mountain pass.

Looking back, I could see the misty breath of the stagecoach horses in the lamp light and the silhouettes of my former companions crossing themselves. Then, as the driver cracked his whip and shouted, the stagecoach sped away toward Bukovina, as if fleeing. When they were completely swallowed by the darkness, a terrible chill and bone-deep loneliness washed over me. However, the new driver wrapped a thick cloak around my shoulders, placed a blanket over my knees, and spoke in fluent German.

"The night air is quite harsh, sir. My master, the Count, gave strict orders to take good care of you. I have placed a bottle of slivovitz (a strong plum liquor) under your seat; drink some if you feel cold." I didn't drink any, but just knowing it was there gave me a strange sense of comfort. Fear and bizarre emotions pierced me to the bone. If there had been a mouse hole, I would have crawled into it to escape. Before I could even gather my senses, the carriage sped wildly down a straight road, and then suddenly jerked its direction to race down another straight road. It felt like we were just going around in circles in the exact same spot.

I paid close attention to the unique landmarks along the road, and my guess was correct. I wanted to ask the driver what kind of trick he was playing in the middle of the night, but I was so terrified that my lips just trembled. If he was intentionally delaying out of spite, complaining would only put me at a disadvantage. I couldn't even guess how much time had passed, so I struck a match inside my coat to check my watch. It was just a few minutes before midnight. My heart sank. Because of the bizarre things I had experienced all evening, all the dark superstitions about midnight swirled in my head, inflating my fear. I held my breath and waited.
