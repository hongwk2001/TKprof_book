CHAPTER 23

DR. SEWARD’S DIARY

October 3.—The wait for Godalming and Quincey Morris felt agonizingly long. Professor Van Helsing tried to keep us distracted by talking continuously. I could see his good intentions in the frequent sideways glances he cast toward Harker. The poor man was consumed by a despair that was heartbreaking to witness. Just the night before, he had been a healthy, energetic young man with a cheerful face and dark brown hair. Today, he looked like a withered old man; his hair had turned completely white, matching his hollow, feverish eyes and the deep lines of sorrow etched into his face. Yet, his fiery energy remained unbroken. This inner fire might be his saving grace; if things go well, it could carry him through this dark time and eventually pull him back into the real world. I used to think my own suffering was immense, but it was nothing compared to his. The Professor knew this too, which was why he was doing his best to keep Harker's mind engaged. Given our situation, what he shared was deeply compelling. I recall his words went something like this:

"Ever since I obtained the documents regarding this monster, I have studied them thoroughly. The more I learn, the more I realize we must destroy him completely. There is evidence everywhere that he is evolving—not just in raw power, but in understanding how to use it. According to the research of my friend Arminius in Budapest, Dracula was an extraordinary man during his mortal life. He was a soldier, a statesman, and an alchemist—alchemy being the pinnacle of scientific knowledge at the time. He possessed a brilliant mind, unmatched learning, and a heart completely devoid of fear or remorse. Legend says he even attended the Scholomance, a mythical school of dark magic, and mastered every field of knowledge available to him. After his physical death, his incredible intellect survived, though his memory seems fragmented. In some ways, his mind operates like a child's, but it is constantly growing, maturing from childish instincts into sophisticated thought. He is experimenting, and doing it well. If we had not interfered—and if we fail now—he could become the founder of a new race of beings, born not from life, but from death."

Harker groaned. "And all of this is aimed at my wife! But what exactly is he experimenting with? Knowing that might help us defeat him!"

"From the moment he arrived, he has been slowly and carefully testing his limits," Van Helsing explained. "That developing mind of his is hard at work. We are lucky that he is still learning; if he had been bolder from the start, he would already be unstoppable. However, he is determined to succeed, and since he has centuries ahead of him, he can afford to be patient. His motto is likely the Latin phrase 'Festina lente'—make haste slowly."

"I don't understand," Harker said wearily. "Please, explain it simply. Perhaps all this grief has clouded my thinking."

The Professor gently placed a hand on Harker's shoulder.

"I will be plain, my boy. Haven't you noticed how this monster has been gaining knowledge through trial and error? Think of how he manipulated Renfield, the patient who eats animals, to gain access to John's asylum. A vampire can come and go as he pleases later, but his very first entry into a home requires an invitation from someone who lives there. Yet, that is minor compared to his larger experiments. At first, he had human workers move all his massive earth boxes. He didn't realize he could do it himself. But as his mind developed, he started wondering if he could manage them on his own. He began by helping, and once he saw it worked, he moved them alone. In doing so, he scattered his resting places where only he knows their locations. He probably intended to bury them deep underground, keeping them safe and secret, ready for him to use at night or whenever he changed form. But do not despair—he learned this a little too late! We have already purified all but one of his hiding places using the sacred Host, rendering them useless to him. By sunset, the final box will be destroyed too. Then, he will have nowhere to hide. I waited until this morning to ensure our plan was foolproof. We have much more to lose than he does, so we must be far more cautious. My watch shows exactly one o'clock; Arthur and Quincey should be arriving any moment. Today is our day. We must proceed slowly but with absolute certainty, leaving nothing to chance. Once they return, there will be five of us against him."

Just then, we were startled by a sharp, double knock at the front door—a telegraph boy. We all rushed to the hallway. Van Helsing motioned for us to stay quiet, opened the door, and took the telegram. He closed the door, checked the address, and read the message aloud.

"Watch out for Dracula. At 12:45, he hastily left Carfax and headed south. He seems to be on the move and might be coming to find you. —Mina."

Jonathan Harker broke the silence:

"Thank God, we will face him soon!" Van Helsing turned to him sharply.

"God will act in His own time and in His own way. Do not be afraid, but do not rejoice just yet. Sometimes getting exactly what we want can be our downfall."

"I care about nothing else now," Harker replied hotly, "except wiping this beast off the face of the earth. I would sell my soul to do it!"

"Hush, my boy, hush!" Van Helsing urged. "God does not deal in souls that way, and the Devil, though he may take your soul, never keeps his promises. But God is merciful and just; He sees your pain and your devotion to dear Mina. Think of how it would multiply her suffering to hear you speak so recklessly. Have faith in us—we are fully committed to this cause, and today we will end it. The time for action is near. Until sunset, this vampire is bound to a mortal form and cannot shape-shift. It will take him time to travel here. Look, it is twenty minutes past one. No matter how fast he moves, he won't arrive for a little while. We must hope that Lord Godalming and Quincey arrive before he does."

About half an hour after we received Mina’s telegram, there was a steady, resolute knock at the front door. It was an ordinary sound, the kind you might hear a hundred times a day, but it made both the Professor's heart and mine race. We exchanged a glance and moved into the hall together. We held our weapons ready—spiritual items like crucifixes and communion wafers in our left hands, and traditional weapons in our right. Van Helsing unlatched the door, pulled it half open, and stepped back, freeing his hands for a fight. Our immense relief must have been obvious when we saw Lord Godalming and Quincey Morris standing on the steps. They hurried inside and shut the door behind them. As they walked down the hall, Godalming announced:

"It's done. We found both locations. There were six boxes in each, and we destroyed them all."

"Destroyed them?" the Professor asked.

"Made them useless to him, at least!" For a minute, no one spoke. Then Quincey added:

"There’s nothing left to do but wait here. If he doesn’t show up by five o'clock, though, we need to head out. We can't leave Mrs. Harker alone after sunset."

"He will be here shortly," Van Helsing said, checking his notebook. "Note this well: the telegram said he went south from Carfax. That means he crossed the river, which he can only do during slack tide—shortly before one o'clock. Heading south is significant. He is suspicious, but not certain. He likely went straight from Carfax to the hiding place he thought was least likely to be compromised. You boys must have left Bermondsey just before he got there. The fact that he isn't here yet means he went to Mile End next. Getting there required crossing the river again, taking up more time. Trust me, my friends, we won't be waiting much longer. We need an attack plan right now so we don't miss our chance. Hush! No more talking. Ready your weapons! Prepare yourselves!" He raised a hand in warning as we all heard a key sliding quietly into the lock of the front door.

Even in that tense moment, I admired how a natural leader takes charge. In all our hunting trips around the world, Quincey Morris had always planned our strategies, and Arthur and I instinctively obeyed him. Now, that old habit kicked in. With a quick look around the room, Quincey silently laid out an ambush and used hand gestures to position us. Van Helsing, Harker, and I stood behind the door, ready to block the intruder's escape once the Professor guarded the exit. Godalming and Quincey hid out of sight near the front, ready to cut off the window. The seconds crawled by with agonizing slowness. We heard deliberate, cautious footsteps coming down the hall; the Count was clearly expecting a trap, or at least wary of one.

Suddenly, he burst into the room in a single leap, tearing past us before anyone could react. His movement was so fast and inhuman, like a striking panther, that the shock actually broke our paralysis. Harker moved first, throwing himself in front of the door that led to the front parlor. When the Count saw us, his face twisted into a monstrous snarl, exposing his long, sharp fangs. But the wicked sneer quickly faded into a cold, arrogant glare. His expression shifted again as all five of us charged at him simultaneously. In hindsight, we should have planned the attack better, because I honestly wasn't sure what to do or if our mundane weapons would even work on him. Harker, however, intended to find out. Gripping his large Kukri knife, he slashed violently at Dracula. It was a brutal strike, and only the Count's unnatural speed saved his life. If he had been a fraction of a second slower, the heavy blade would have cleaved his heart. Instead, the tip of the knife merely slashed the Count's coat, ripping a large hole that spilled a bundle of banknotes and a cascade of gold coins onto the floor. The fury on Dracula's face was so demonic that I feared for Harker's life, even as I saw my friend raise the bloody knife for another swing. Acting on protective instinct, I stepped forward, holding out my crucifix and the sacred wafer. I felt an incredible surge of holy power rush down my arm, and I watched the monster recoil as the rest of my friends spontaneously made the same gesture. Words cannot describe the sheer hatred, fury, and frustrated malice that warped the Count's features. His pale skin turned a sickly greenish-yellow in stark contrast to his blazing eyes, and the red scar on his forehead throbbed like an open wound. But in a flash, he ducked smoothly under Harker's arm before the next blow could land. Scooping up a handful of dropped money, he bolted across the room and launched himself at the window. Amid the deafening shatter of glass, he plummeted into the stone courtyard below. Over the noise of the breaking pane, I heard the ringing of gold coins bouncing on the flagstones.

We rushed to the window and saw him stand up, completely unharmed. He sprinted up the steps, crossed the courtyard, and shoved open the heavy stable door. There, he stopped, turned back, and shouted at us:

"You think you can defeat me? You, with your pale, frightened faces lined up like sheep in a slaughterhouse? You will regret this, every single one of you! You think you've destroyed all my resting places, but I have more. My revenge is only just beginning! I will stretch it over centuries; time is on my side. The women you love already belong to me. And through them, you—and everyone else—will eventually serve me. You will be my creatures, my scavengers, doing my bidding and feeding me when I demand it! Bah!" With a mocking sneer, he vanished through the doorway, and we heard the rusty bolt grind shut. Another door opened and closed in the distance. The Professor was the first to speak as we moved toward the hallway, realizing it would be too difficult to chase him through the stable.

"We have learned a great deal! Despite his arrogant threats, he is afraid. He fears time, and he fears running out of resources! If he isn't scared, why is he in such a hurry? His tone gave him away, unless my ears deceive me. And why did he scramble for the money? Go after him quickly—you are big game hunters; use those skills. Meanwhile, I will destroy everything here so it cannot aid him if he returns." As he spoke, Van Helsing scooped the remaining money into his pocket. He gathered the property deeds Harker had left on the floor and tossed the rest of the documents into the fireplace, striking a match to burn them.

Godalming and Morris sprinted out to the courtyard, while Harker dropped down from the window to give chase. But the Count had bolted the stable door from the inside, and by the time they broke it open, Dracula was gone. Van Helsing and I checked the back alley, but the mews were completely empty; no one had seen him leave.

It was late afternoon, and sunset was rapidly approaching. We had to admit that we had lost him for now. With heavy hearts, we agreed with the Professor when he said:

"Let us return to dear, sweet Mina. We have done all we can here, and at the very least, we can protect her. But we must not give up hope. There is still one box of earth left, and we must find it. Once we do, we can still win this." I could tell he was trying to sound confident for Harker's sake. The poor man was completely devastated, occasionally letting out quiet groans of despair. His thoughts were entirely focused on his wife.

We returned to my house feeling utterly defeated, only to find Mrs. Harker waiting for us. She forced a cheerful expression, a testament to her incredible bravery and selflessness. When she saw our grim faces, however, she turned pale as death. She closed her eyes for a moment, silently praying, and then forced a bright smile as she spoke:

"I cannot thank you all enough. Oh, my poor darling!" She cradled her husband's graying head in her hands and kissed him tenderly. "Rest your head here, my love. Everything will be all right. God will protect us if it is His will." Harker could only groan. His profound misery left no room for words.

We ate a quick, somber dinner, which managed to lift our spirits slightly. Perhaps it was just the warmth of a meal—none of us had eaten since breakfast—or perhaps it was simply the comfort of being together. Regardless, we felt a bit less wretched and could face tomorrow with a sliver of hope. Keeping our promise, we recounted the entire day's events to Mrs. Harker. She turned pale when we described the dangers her husband faced, and flushed red when we told her of his devotion, but she listened bravely and calmly. When we described how recklessly Harker had charged at the Count, she gripped his arm tightly, as if holding him could shield him from harm. She remained silent until the story was finished, bringing us up to the present moment. Then, still holding her husband's hand, she stood up to address us. I wish I could properly describe that scene. She stood there, a truly good and sweet woman, radiant in her youth and vitality, bearing that terrible red scar on her forehead—a scar we all noted with clenched jaws, knowing exactly how it got there. Her loving kindness stood in stark contrast to our grim hatred, and her gentle faith contrasted with our doubts and fears. It broke our hearts to know that, despite all her purity and goodness, that scar symbolized her current alienation from God.

"Jonathan," she said, speaking his name so tenderly it sounded like music. "Jonathan, my dear, and all of you, my true friends—I need you to remember something as we navigate this nightmare. I know you must fight. I know you must destroy this creature, just as you destroyed the corrupted Lucy so her true soul could find peace. But you must not do this out of hatred. The poor soul who caused all this misery is the most tragic victim of all. Think of the joy he will feel when his evil half is finally destroyed, allowing his better self to achieve spiritual peace. You must feel pity for him, even as you strike him down."

As she spoke, I watched her husband's face darken, his internal rage seemingly burning him alive from the inside out. He instinctively squeezed her hand so tightly his knuckles turned white. She didn't flinch from the pain, but looked at him with pleading eyes. The moment she finished speaking, he sprang to his feet, practically tearing his hand from hers.

"May God give me the chance to destroy his cursed earthly body! If I had the power to damn his soul to a fiery Hell for eternity, I would do that too!"

"Oh, hush! Please, hush, in the name of a merciful God!" she cried. "Don't say such things, Jonathan, my husband. You are terrifying me. Please think, my love—I have been thinking about it all day... perhaps... someday... I might need that same pity. And someone like you, with every right to be furious, might deny it to me! Oh, Jonathan, I would have spared you this awful thought if there was any other way. I pray that God ignores your angry words and sees them only as the heartbroken cries of a deeply loving and suffering man. Oh, God, let his prematurely white hair be the proof of all the pain he has endured—a man who has never done any wrong, yet has suffered so much."

By this point, all of us men were crying. We couldn't hold back our tears and wept openly. She cried, too, relieved that her gentle wisdom had gotten through to us. Her husband dropped to his knees, wrapped his arms around her waist, and buried his weeping face in her dress. Van Helsing silently signaled to the rest of us, and we quietly slipped out of the room, leaving the devoted couple alone with God.

Before they went to bed, the Professor warded their room to prevent the Vampire from entering, promising Mrs. Harker she could sleep safely. She tried to convince herself it was true, putting on a brave face mostly for her husband's sake. It was a courageous effort, and I believe it brought her some small comfort. Van Helsing left a bell nearby, instructing them to ring it immediately if there was an emergency. Once they retired, Quincey, Godalming, and I agreed to stand guard, taking shifts throughout the night to protect the poor woman. Quincey took the first watch, meaning the rest of us needed to get some sleep while we could. Godalming is already in bed, as he takes the second shift. Now that my journal entry is finished, I will go to sleep as well.

Jonathan Harker’s Journal.

October 3–4, almost midnight.—I felt like yesterday would drag on forever. I desperately wanted to sleep, clinging to a naive hope that when I woke up, things would somehow be different, and better. Before we separated, we debated our next move but couldn't agree on a plan. All we know for certain is that one box of earth is left, and only the Count knows its location. If he decides to lay low, he could evade us for years, and in the meantime... the thought is too horrifying to dwell on. But I do know this: if any woman embodies perfection, it is my poor, suffering wife. I love her a thousand times more for the incredible compassion she showed tonight—a pity so pure it made my own burning hatred feel petty and small. Surely, God won't let the world lose such a beautiful soul. That thought gives me hope. We are sailing dangerously close to the rocks, and faith is our only anchor. Thank God, Mina is sleeping soundly, without any nightmares. Given the horrors she's experienced, I dread to think what her dreams might look like. I haven't seen her this calm since sunset. For a brief moment, her face relaxed into an expression of deep peace, like the arrival of spring after a harsh winter storm. At first, I thought it was just the soft glow of the sunset, but now I suspect it meant something more profound. I am exhausted to my very bones, yet I don't feel sleepy. Still, I have to try to get some rest. We have tomorrow to worry about, and there will be no real peace for me until...

*       *       *       *       *

Later.—I must have dozed off because I was woken by Mina sitting bolt upright in bed, looking panicked. I could see her clearly because we left a light on. She clamped a hand firmly over my mouth to keep me quiet and whispered urgently:

"Hush! There's someone out in the hallway!" I slid out of bed quietly, crept across the room, and slowly cracked the door open.

Lying on a mattress right outside our door was Mr. Morris, wide awake. He immediately raised a hand, gesturing for silence, and whispered:

"Shh! Go back to bed; everything is fine. One of us is going to be out here all night. We aren't taking any risks!"

His firm expression made it clear there was no room for argument, so I quietly closed the door and relayed the message to Mina. She let out a long sigh, and a faint, genuine smile appeared on her pale face as she hugged me tightly and whispered:

"Thank God for good, brave men!" With another sigh, she lay back down and drifted off to sleep. I am writing this down now because my sleepiness has vanished, though I need to try and rest again.

*       *       *       *       *

October 4, morning.—Mina woke me up once more during the night. We had all managed to get a decent amount of sleep; the early morning light was beginning to outline the windows in sharp gray rectangles, and the gas lamp now looked like a dim speck instead of a bright glow. She spoke to me with urgent haste:

"Go wake the Professor. I need to speak with him right now."

"Why?" I asked, confused.

"I had a realization. It must have come to me while I was sleeping, forming in my mind without me knowing it. He needs to hypnotize me before the sun comes up; then I think I'll be able to speak freely. Hurry, darling, we don't have much time!" I went to the door. Dr. Seward was on guard duty, resting on the mattress outside. When he saw me, he jumped up.

"Is something wrong?" he asked, alarmed.

"No," I answered, "but Mina needs to see Dr. Van Helsing immediately."

"I'll get him," he said, and hurried toward the Professor's room.

A few minutes later, Van Helsing arrived in his dressing gown. Mr. Morris and Lord Godalming lingered near the doorway with Dr. Seward, asking what was happening. When the Professor saw Mina smiling, a look of immense relief replaced his anxious expression. He rubbed his hands together eagerly and said:

"Ah, dear Madam Mina, this is a wonderful change! Look, friend Jonathan, we have our old Mina back with us today!" Turning his attention back to her, he asked warmly, "Now, what can I do for you? I know you wouldn't call for me at this hour for no reason."

"I need you to hypnotize me!" she replied. "Do it right now, before dawn breaks. I feel like I'll be able to speak freely then. Please hurry, time is running out!" Without another word, he gestured for her to sit up.

Staring intently into her eyes, he began moving his hands methodically from the top of her head down past her face. Mina stared back at him without blinking for a few minutes. My heart pounded so hard it felt like a hammer against my ribs; I knew we were approaching a critical moment. Slowly, her eyes fluttered shut, and she sat completely frozen. Only the faint rise and fall of her chest showed she was still breathing. The Professor continued the hand motions a few more times before stopping. I noticed sweat beading heavily on his forehead. Mina opened her eyes, but she seemed completely different. Her gaze was unfocused, looking right through us, and when she spoke, her voice had an eerie, dreamlike quality I had never heard before. Raising a hand to keep us quiet, the Professor signaled for me to bring the other men inside. They crept in on tiptoe, shut the door, and stood silently at the foot of the bed, watching closely. Mina didn't seem to notice them. Van Helsing broke the heavy silence, keeping his voice low and steady so as not to break her trance:

"Where are you?" Her answer came in a flat, emotionless tone:

"I don't know. Sleep has no specific place to call its own." The room was completely silent for a few minutes. Mina remained frozen, while the Professor stared at her intently; the rest of us barely dared to take a breath. It was getting brighter outside. Without taking his eyes off Mina, Dr. Van Helsing motioned for me to open the blinds. I did so, revealing that dawn had arrived. A bright red streak of light hit the horizon, filling the room with a rosy glow. Instantly, the Professor asked:

"Where are you right now?" Her answer was still dreamy, but more deliberate, as if she were translating a feeling. It sounded like the tone she used when reading back her shorthand notes.

"I don't know. Everything feels unfamiliar."

"What do you see?"

"Nothing. It's completely dark."

"What do you hear?" I could hear the tense patience in the Professor's voice.

"Water splashing. I hear it gurgling past, and the sound of small waves breaking against something outside."

"Are you on a ship, then?" We all exchanged terrified looks, trying to read each other's thoughts. We were scared to acknowledge what this meant. Her answer came quickly:

"Oh, yes!"

"What else can you hear?"

"Men stomping heavily on the deck above me. I hear a chain squeaking, and the loud, rhythmic clanking of an anchor being raised."

"And what are you doing?"

"I am completely still... so very still. It feels like death!" Her voice trailed off into a deep, heavy sigh, like someone falling into a deep sleep, and her eyes closed once more.

By now, the sun was fully up, and the room was bright with daylight. Dr. Van Helsing gently placed his hands on Mina's shoulders and laid her down on the pillows. She rested peacefully, looking like a sleeping child, before taking a deep breath and waking up. She looked around at us in surprise. "Was I talking in my sleep?" she asked simply. She seemed to instantly grasp the situation, even though she couldn't remember it, and was desperate to know what she had revealed. The Professor recounted everything she had said, and she immediately replied:

"We don't have a second to lose! We might still be able to catch him!" Mr. Morris and Lord Godalming bolted for the door, but the Professor calmly called them back:

"Wait, my friends. Wherever that ship is, it was raising its anchor while she was speaking. Dozens of ships are leaving the great Port of London right now. Which one are you going to look for? Thank God we finally have a lead, even if we don't know exactly where it points. We have been blind—blind in the way all mortals are. Looking back, it's obvious, and we should have anticipated it! Forgive me, I'm rambling. Now we understand why the Count grabbed that money, even when Jonathan's fierce attack nearly killed him. He was planning an escape. Listen to me: an escape! He realized he only had one earth-box left, and with a pack of men hunting him down like hounds after a fox, London was no longer safe. He brought his final box aboard a ship and is fleeing the country. He thinks he can get away, but he is wrong! We will hunt him down. 'Tally-ho!' as our friend Arthur says when he's hunting foxes! The old fox is clever, yes, incredibly clever, so we must be just as cunning. I, too, am clever, and I think I can predict his next move. For now, we can finally relax. There is a vast ocean between us, water he has no desire to cross. In fact, he cannot leave the ship unless it docks, and even then, only at high or low tide. Look, the sun is up, and we have the whole day until sunset. Let's bathe, dress, and eat a hearty breakfast. We all desperately need it, and we can finally eat in peace knowing he is no longer in this country." Mina looked at him with pleading eyes and asked:

"But why do we need to keep chasing him if he's already run away?" He gently patted her hand and replied:

"Don't ask me that just yet. After we've had breakfast, I will answer all your questions." He refused to elaborate, so we all split up to get dressed.

After we finished breakfast, Mina asked her question again. He stared at her seriously for a long moment before answering with deep sadness:

"Because, my dearest Madam Mina, we must find him now more than ever, even if we have to follow him to the very gates of Hell!" Her face turned incredibly pale as she whispered:

"Why?"

"Because," he said grimly, "he is immortal and can live for centuries, while you are only a mortal woman. And time is now our greatest enemy... ever since he forced you to drink his blood."

I just barely caught her before she collapsed in a dead faint.
