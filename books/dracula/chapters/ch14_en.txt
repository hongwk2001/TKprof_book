[P001] CHAPTER XIV

[P002] MINA HARKER’S JOURNAL

[P003] _23 September_.--Jonathan is better after a bad night. I am so glad that
he has plenty of work to do, for that keeps his mind off the terrible
things; and oh, I am rejoiced that he is not now weighed down with the
responsibility of his new position. I knew he would be true to himself,
and now how proud I am to see my Jonathan rising to the height of his
advancement and keeping pace in all ways with the duties that come upon
him. He will be away all day till late, for he said he could not lunch
at home. My household work is done, so I shall take his foreign journal,
and lock myself up in my room and read it....

[P004] September 24.?I could not bring myself to write last night; Jonathan's terrifying account shook me too deeply. My poor, dear husband! How much he must have suffered, whether those events were real or just his imagination. I wonder if there is any truth to it at all. Did he catch a severe stress-induced fever and write all those awful things, or did something actually cause it? I suppose I will never know, for I do not dare bring it up to him.... But what about the man we saw yesterday! Jonathan seemed so sure it was him.... Poor man! I guess Mr.

[P005] Hawkins's funeral upset him and triggered some past trauma.... He genuinely believes it all. I remember what he said on our wedding day: "Unless some solemn duty comes upon me to go back to those bitter hours, asleep or awake, mad or sane. " There seems to be a consistent thread running through it all.... That terrifying Count was coming to London.... If it is true, and he has arrived in London with his masses.... There might be a solemn duty awaiting us, and if it comes, we must not run from it.... I will be ready. I will get my typewriter right now and begin transcribing his journal.

[P006] Then we will be prepared for others to read it if necessary. And if it is needed, my preparation might spare poor Jonathan from getting upset, because I can speak on his behalf and ensure he is never troubled or worried by it again. If Jonathan ever fully recovers from his nervous condition, he might want to tell me everything. Then I can ask him questions, understand what happened, and figure out how to comfort him.

[P007] _Letter, Van Helsing to Mrs. Harker._

[P008] “_24 September._

[P009] (_Confidence_)

[P010] “Dear Madam,--

[P011] "Please forgive me for writing to you, taking advantage of the fact that we are acquainted through my sending you the sad news of Miss Lucy Westenra's death. Thanks to the kindness of Lord Godalming, I have been given permission to read her letters and papers, because I am deeply concerned about certain highly important matters. Among them, I found some letters from you that show what great friends you were and how much you loved her. Oh, Madam Mina, I beg you, in the name of that love, please help me.

[P012] I ask this for the good of others?to correct a grave wrong and to relieve many terrible troubles that may be far worse than you can imagine. Would it be possible for me to see you? You can trust me. I am a friend of Dr. John Seward and of Lord Godalming (who was Arthur to Miss Lucy). For now, I must keep this matter a secret from everyone. I would come to Exeter to see you immediately if you grant me permission and tell me when and where. I beg your pardon, madam. Having read your letters to poor Lucy, I know how kind you are and how much your husband is suffering.

[P013] Therefore, I pray you, if possible, do not tell him about this, lest it cause him harm. Again, I ask your pardon and forgiveness.

[P014] “VAN HELSING.”

[P015] _Telegram, Mrs. Harker to Van Helsing._

[P016] “_25 September._--Come to-day by quarter-past ten train if you can catch
it. Can see you any time you call.

[P017] “WILHELMINA HARKER.”

[P018] MINA HARKER’S JOURNAL.

[P019] September 25.?I cannot help feeling terribly excited as the time draws near for Dr. Van Helsing's visit. Somehow I expect he will shed some light on Jonathan's sad experience. Also, since he attended to poor, dear Lucy during her final illness, he can tell me all about her. That must be why he is coming; it is about Lucy and her sleepwalking, not Jonathan. Then I guess I will never know the real truth now! How silly I am. That awful journal has taken hold of my imagination and is coloring everything with its own dark hue. Of course he is coming about Lucy.

[P020] Her sleepwalking habit returned, and that awful night on the cliff must have made her sick. I had been so wrapped up in my own affairs that I almost forgot how ill she was afterward. She must have told him about her sleepwalking adventure on the cliff and that I knew all about it. Now he wants me to tell him what I know so that he can understand it better. I hope I did the right thing by not mentioning it to Mrs. Westenra. I could never forgive myself if any action of mine?even keeping silent?brought harm to poor, dear Lucy. I also hope Dr.

[P021] Van Helsing will not blame me; I have had so much trouble and anxiety lately that I feel I cannot handle any more right now.

[P022] I suppose a cry does us all good at times--clears the air as other rain
does. Perhaps it was reading the journal yesterday that upset me, and
then Jonathan went away this morning to stay away from me a whole day
and night, the first time we have been parted since our marriage. I do
hope the dear fellow will take care of himself, and that nothing will
occur to upset him. It is two o’clock, and the doctor will be here soon
now. I shall say nothing of Jonathan’s journal unless he asks me. I am
so glad I have type-written out my own journal, so that, in case he asks
about Lucy, I can hand it to him; it will save much questioning.

[P023] *       *       *       *       *

[P024] Later.?He came and went. Oh, what a strange meeting, and how it has made my head spin! I feel like I am in a dream. Could all of this, or even a part of it, possibly be real? If I had not read Jonathan's journal first, I never would have accepted even the possibility. Poor, poor, dear Jonathan! How much he must have suffered. Please, God, let this not upset him again. I will try to shield him from it, but perhaps it might actually comfort and help him?as terrible and awful as the consequences may be?to know for certain that his eyes, ears, and mind did not deceive him, and that it is all true.

[P025] Maybe the doubt is what haunts him the most. If that doubt is removed, whether it proves he was awake or dreaming, he might feel more satisfied and better equipped to handle the shock. Dr. Van Helsing must be both a good and clever man if Arthur and Dr. Seward sent for him all the way from Holland to look after Lucy. Just from seeing him, I feel he is kind, good, and of a noble nature. When he returns tomorrow, I will ask him about Jonathan. Then, God willing, all this sorrow and anxiety might lead to a good resolution.

[P026] I used to think I wanted to practice interviewing; Jonathan's friend from "The Exeter News" told him that memory is everything in such work. He said you must be able to record almost exactly every word spoken, even if you have to polish it later. This was a rare interview opportunity, and I will try to record it word for word.

[P027] It was half-past two o’clock when the knock came. I took my courage _à
deux mains_ and waited. In a few minutes Mary opened the door, and
announced “Dr. Van Helsing.”

[P028] I stood up and bowed, and he walked toward me. He is a man of medium build but very sturdy, with his shoulders squared over a broad, deep chest. His neck is thick and perfectly balances his well-proportioned head. The way he holds his head immediately strikes you as a sign of thought and power. His head is noble and well-sized, broad and large behind the ears.

[P029] His clean-shaven face features a strong, square chin, a large, determined, and expressive mouth, and a good-sized, fairly straight nose with quick, sensitive nostrils that seem to widen when his thick, bushy eyebrows furrow and his mouth tightens. His forehead is broad and fine; it rises almost straight up at first before sloping back over two prominent ridges that are set wide apart. It is the kind of forehead that forces his reddish hair to fall naturally back and to the sides, never tumbling over it.

[P030] He has large, dark blue eyes that are set far apart; they can quickly shift from tender to stern depending on his mood. He said to me:?

[P031] “Mrs. Harker, is it not?” I bowed assent.

[P032] “That was Miss Mina Murray?” Again I assented.

[P033] “It is Mina Murray that I came to see that was friend of that poor dear
child Lucy Westenra. Madam Mina, it is on account of the dead I come.”

[P034] “Sir,” I said, “you could have no better claim on me than that you were
a friend and helper of Lucy Westenra.” And I held out my hand. He took
it and said tenderly:--

[P035] “Oh, Madam Mina, I knew that the friend of that poor lily girl must be
good, but I had yet to learn----” He finished his speech with a courtly
bow. I asked him what it was that he wanted to see me about, so he at
once began:--

[P036] “I have read your letters to Miss Lucy. Forgive me, but I had to begin
to inquire somewhere, and there was none to ask. I know that you were
with her at Whitby. She sometimes kept a diary--you need not look
surprised, Madam Mina; it was begun after you had left, and was in
imitation of you--and in that diary she traces by inference certain
things to a sleep-walking in which she puts down that you saved her. In
great perplexity then I come to you, and ask you out of your so much
kindness to tell me all of it that you can remember.”

[P037] “I can tell you, I think, Dr. Van Helsing, all about it.”

[P038] “Ah, then you have good memory for facts, for details? It is not always
so with young ladies.”

[P039] “No, doctor, but I wrote it all down at the time. I can show it to you
if you like.”

[P040] “Oh, Madam Mina, I will be grateful; you will do me much favour.” I
could not resist the temptation of mystifying him a bit--I suppose it is
some of the taste of the original apple that remains still in our
mouths--so I handed him the shorthand diary. He took it with a grateful
bow, and said:--

[P041] “May I read it?”

[P042] “If you wish,” I answered as demurely as I could. He opened it, and for
an instant his face fell. Then he stood up and bowed.

[P043] “Oh, you so clever woman!” he said. “I knew long that Mr. Jonathan was a
man of much thankfulness; but see, his wife have all the good things.
And will you not so much honour me and so help me as to read it for me?
Alas! I know not the shorthand.” By this time my little joke was over,
and I was almost ashamed; so I took the typewritten copy from my
workbasket and handed it to him.

[P044] “Forgive me,” I said: “I could not help it; but I had been thinking that
it was of dear Lucy that you wished to ask, and so that you might not
have time to wait--not on my account, but because I know your time must
be precious--I have written it out on the typewriter for you.”

[P045] He took it and his eyes glistened. “You are so good,” he said. “And may
I read it now? I may want to ask you some things when I have read.”

[P046] “By all means,” I said, “read it over whilst I order lunch; and then you
can ask me questions whilst we eat.” He bowed and settled himself in a
chair with his back to the light, and became absorbed in the papers,
whilst I went to see after lunch chiefly in order that he might not be
disturbed. When I came back, I found him walking hurriedly up and down
the room, his face all ablaze with excitement. He rushed up to me and
took me by both hands.

[P047] "Oh, Madam Mina," he said, "how can I ever tell you how much I owe you? This paper is like a ray of sunshine. It opens a door for me. I am dazed?I am dazzled by so much light, even though dark clouds still roll in behind that light every time. But that is something you do not, and cannot, understand. Oh, but I am so grateful to you, you clever woman. Madam"?he said this very seriously?"if ever Abraham Van Helsing can do anything for you or your family, I hope you will let me know. It would be my joy and pleasure to serve you as a friend.

[P048] Not just as a friend, but I pledge that all I have ever learned and everything I can do will be at the service of you and those you love. There is darkness in life, and there is light; you are one of the lights. You will have a happy, good life, and your husband will be blessed because of you."

[P049] “But, doctor, you praise me too much, and--and you do not know me.”

[P050] "I do not know you? I, an old man who has studied men and women his entire life? I, who have made the brain and everything that comes from it my specialty! I have read the diary you so kindly prepared for me, which breathes truth in every single line. I, who read your sweet letter to poor Lucy about your marriage and your deep trust?you say I do not know you! Oh, Madam Mina, good women reveal the truth of their lives every day, every hour, and every minute, so clearly that angels could read it. And we men who seek to understand have a bit of that angelic vision in us.

[P051] Your husband has a noble nature, and so do you. You have trust, and trust cannot exist where there is a cruel or mean spirit. Now, tell me about your husband. Is he completely well? Is his fever completely gone, and is he strong and healthy again? " I saw this as the perfect opening to ask him about Jonathan, so I said:?

[P052] “He was almost recovered, but he has been greatly upset by Mr. Hawkins’s
death.” He interrupted:--

[P053] “Oh, yes, I know, I know. I have read your last two letters.” I went
on:--

[P054] “I suppose this upset him, for when we were in town on Thursday last he
had a sort of shock.”

[P055] “A shock, and after brain fever so soon! That was not good. What kind of
a shock was it?”

[P056] “He thought he saw some one who recalled something terrible, something
which led to his brain fever.” And here the whole thing seemed to
overwhelm me in a rush. The pity for Jonathan, the horror which he
experienced, the whole fearful mystery of his diary, and the fear that
has been brooding over me ever since, all came in a tumult. I suppose I
was hysterical, for I threw myself on my knees and held up my hands to
him, and implored him to make my husband well again. He took my hands
and raised me up, and made me sit on the sofa, and sat by me; he held my
hand in his, and said to me with, oh, such infinite sweetness:--

[P057] "My life is barren and lonely, so consumed by work that I have not had much time for friendships. But since my friend John Seward called me here, I have met so many good people and witnessed such nobility that I feel the loneliness of my life more than ever?a feeling that has only grown as I get older. Believe me, then, when I say that I come to you with the utmost respect. You have given me hope?not necessarily hope for what I am investigating, but hope that there are still good women left to make life happy. Women whose lives and truths can serve as a great lesson for future children.

[P058] I am so very glad that I might be of some use to you here. If your husband is suffering, his suffering falls within my field of study and experience. I promise you that I will gladly do everything in my power to help him?to make his life strong and manly again, and to make your life happy. Now, you must eat. You are exhausted and perhaps overly anxious. Your husband Jonathan would not want to see you looking so pale. Whatever displeases the one he loves is not good for him. So, for his sake, you must eat and smile.

[P059] You have told me everything about Lucy, so we will not speak of her anymore right now, lest it upset you. I will stay in Exeter tonight because I need to think deeply about what you have told me. Once I have thought it over, I will ask you questions, if I may. And then, you will tell me as much as you can about husband Jonathan's troubles?but not yet. You must eat now; you can tell me everything afterward."

[P060] After lunch, when we went back to the drawing-room, he said to me:--

[P061] “And now tell me all about him.” When it came to speaking to this great
learned man, I began to fear that he would think me a weak fool, and
Jonathan a madman--that journal is all so strange--and I hesitated to go
on. But he was so sweet and kind, and he had promised to help, and I
trusted him, so I said:--

[P062] “Dr. Van Helsing, what I have to tell you is so queer that you must not
laugh at me or at my husband. I have been since yesterday in a sort of
fever of doubt; you must be kind to me, and not think me foolish that I
have even half believed some very strange things.” He reassured me by
his manner as well as his words when he said:--

[P063] “Oh, my dear, if you only know how strange is the matter regarding which
I am here, it is you who would laugh. I have learned not to think little
of any one’s belief, no matter how strange it be. I have tried to keep
an open mind; and it is not the ordinary things of life that could close
it, but the strange things, the extraordinary things, the things that
make one doubt if they be mad or sane.”

[P064] “Thank you, thank you, a thousand times! You have taken a weight off my
mind. If you will let me, I shall give you a paper to read. It is long,
but I have typewritten it out. It will tell you my trouble and
Jonathan’s. It is the copy of his journal when abroad, and all that
happened. I dare not say anything of it; you will read for yourself and
judge. And then when I see you, perhaps, you will be very kind and tell
me what you think.”

[P065] “I promise,” he said as I gave him the papers; “I shall in the morning,
so soon as I can, come to see you and your husband, if I may.”

[P066] “Jonathan will be here at half-past eleven, and you must come to lunch
with us and see him then; you could catch the quick 3:34 train, which
will leave you at Paddington before eight.” He was surprised at my
knowledge of the trains off-hand, but he does not know that I have made
up all the trains to and from Exeter, so that I may help Jonathan in
case he is in a hurry.

[P067] So he took the papers with him and went away, and I sit here
thinking--thinking I don’t know what.

[P069] _Letter (by hand), Van Helsing to Mrs. Harker._

[P070] “_25 September, 6 o’clock._

[P071] “Dear Madam Mina,--

[P072] "I have read your husband's truly wonderful diary. You can sleep peacefully tonight without a single doubt. As strange and terrifying as it is, it is all true! I would stake my life on it. It may spell disaster for others, but for you and your husband, there is nothing to fear. He is a brave and noble man. Let me tell you, from my vast experience with men, that anyone who could climb down that castle wall and enter that room?and then do it a second time?is not the kind of man to be permanently broken by a shock.

[P073] His brain and his heart are completely sound; I swear this to you, even before I have met him. So, please rest easy. I will have many things to ask him about later. I am so blessed that I came to see you today, because I have learned so much all at once that my head is spinning?spinning more than ever, and I need time to think.

[P074] “Yours the most faithful,

[P075] “ABRAHAM VAN HELSING.”

[P076] _Letter, Mrs. Harker to Van Helsing._

[P077] “_25 September, 6:30 p. m._

[P078] “My dear Dr. Van Helsing,--

[P079] "A thousand thanks for your incredibly kind letter. It has lifted a massive weight from my mind. And yet, if his journal is true, what terrifying things exist in this world! How awful it would be if that man?that monster?is actually here in London! I am terrified even to think of it. Just this moment, as I was writing, I received a telegram from Jonathan saying he is leaving Launceston on the 6:25 train tonight and will be here by 10:18. Knowing he is coming home means I won't have to be afraid tonight.

[P080] Therefore, instead of joining us for lunch tomorrow, would you please come over for breakfast at eight o'clock, if that isn't too early for you? If you are in a rush, you can easily catch the 10:30 train, which will get you to Paddington around 2:35. You don't need to reply to this; if I don't hear from you, I will assume you are coming for breakfast.

[P081] “Believe me,

[P082] “Your faithful and grateful friend,

[P083] “MINA HARKER.”

[P084] _Jonathan Harker’s Journal._

[P085] September 26.?I never thought I would write in this diary again, but the time has come. When I got home last night, Mina had supper ready. After we ate, she told me about Van Helsing's visit, how she had given him copies of our diaries, and how terribly worried she had been about me. She showed me the doctor's letter confirming that everything I wrote down was true. The news seems to have made me a completely new man. It was the crushing doubt about what was real and what wasn't that had broken me down. I felt powerless, lost in the dark, and paranoid.

[P086] But now that I know the truth, I am no longer afraid?not even of the Count. So, he finally succeeded in his plan to get to London, and the man I saw really was him. He has grown younger, but how? If Van Helsing is the man Mina claims he is, he is exactly the person we need to unmask the Count and hunt him down. Mina and I stayed up late discussing everything. She is getting dressed now, and I will head over to the hotel in a few minutes to fetch the doctor....

[P087] He was, I think, surprised to see me. When I came into the room where he
was, and introduced myself, he took me by the shoulder, and turned my
face round to the light, and said, after a sharp scrutiny:--

[P088] “But Madam Mina told me you were ill, that you had had a shock.” It was
so funny to hear my wife called “Madam Mina” by this kindly,
strong-faced old man. I smiled, and said:--

[P089] “I _was_ ill, I _have_ had a shock; but you have cured me already.”

[P090] “And how?”

[P091] “By your letter to Mina last night. I was in doubt, and then everything
took a hue of unreality, and I did not know what to trust, even the
evidence of my own senses. Not knowing what to trust, I did not know
what to do; and so had only to keep on working in what had hitherto been
the groove of my life. The groove ceased to avail me, and I mistrusted
myself. Doctor, you don’t know what it is to doubt everything, even
yourself. No, you don’t; you couldn’t with eyebrows like yours.” He
seemed pleased, and laughed as he said:--

[P092] “So! You are physiognomist. I learn more here with each hour. I am with
so much pleasure coming to you to breakfast; and, oh, sir, you will
pardon praise from an old man, but you are blessed in your wife.” I
would listen to him go on praising Mina for a day, so I simply nodded
and stood silent.

[P093] “She is one of God’s women, fashioned by His own hand to show us men and
other women that there is a heaven where we can enter, and that its
light can be here on earth. So true, so sweet, so noble, so little an
egoist--and that, let me tell you, is much in this age, so sceptical and
selfish. And you, sir--I have read all the letters to poor Miss Lucy,
and some of them speak of you, so I know you since some days from the
knowing of others; but I have seen your true self since last night. You
will give me your hand, will you not? And let us be friends for all our
lives.”

[P094] We shook hands, and he was so earnest and so kind that it made me quite
choky.

[P095] “And now,” he said, “may I ask you for some more help? I have a great
task to do, and at the beginning it is to know. You can help me here.
Can you tell me what went before your going to Transylvania? Later on I
may ask more help, and of a different kind; but at first this will do.”

[P096] “Look here, sir,” I said, “does what you have to do concern the Count?”

[P097] “It does,” he said solemnly.

[P098] “Then I am with you heart and soul. As you go by the 10:30 train, you
will not have time to read them; but I shall get the bundle of papers.
You can take them with you and read them in the train.”

[P099] After breakfast I saw him to the station. When we were parting he
said:--

[P100] “Perhaps you will come to town if I send to you, and take Madam Mina
too.”

[P101] “We shall both come when you will,” I said.

[P102] I had got him the morning papers and the London papers of the previous
night, and while we were talking at the carriage window, waiting for the
train to start, he was turning them over. His eyes suddenly seemed to
catch something in one of them, “The Westminster Gazette”--I knew it by
the colour--and he grew quite white. He read something intently,
groaning to himself: “Mein Gott! Mein Gott! So soon! so soon!” I do not
think he remembered me at the moment. Just then the whistle blew, and
the train moved off. This recalled him to himself, and he leaned out of
the window and waved his hand, calling out: “Love to Madam Mina; I shall
write so soon as ever I can.”

[P103] _Dr. Seward’s Diary._

[P104] _26 September._--Truly there is no such thing as finality. Not a week
since I said “Finis,” and yet here I am starting fresh again, or rather
going on with the same record. Until this afternoon I had no cause to
think of what is done. Renfield had become, to all intents, as sane as
he ever was. He was already well ahead with his fly business; and he had
just started in the spider line also; so he had not been of any trouble
to me. I had a letter from Arthur, written on Sunday, and from it I
gather that he is bearing up wonderfully well. Quincey Morris is with
him, and that is much of a help, for he himself is a bubbling well of
good spirits. Quincey wrote me a line too, and from him I hear that
Arthur is beginning to recover something of his old buoyancy; so as to
them all my mind is at rest. As for myself, I was settling down to my
work with the enthusiasm which I used to have for it, so that I might
fairly have said that the wound which poor Lucy left on me was becoming
cicatrised. Everything is, however, now reopened; and what is to be the
end God only knows. I have an idea that Van Helsing thinks he knows,
too, but he will only let out enough at a time to whet curiosity. He
went to Exeter yesterday, and stayed there all night. To-day he came
back, and almost bounded into the room at about half-past five o’clock,
and thrust last night’s “Westminster Gazette” into my hand.

[P105] “What do you think of that?” he asked as he stood back and folded his
arms.

[P106] I looked over the paper, for I really did not know what he meant; but he
took it from me and pointed out a paragraph about children being decoyed
away at Hampstead. It did not convey much to me, until I reached a
passage where it described small punctured wounds on their throats. An
idea struck me, and I looked up. “Well?” he said.

[P107] “It is like poor Lucy’s.”

[P108] “And what do you make of it?”

[P109] “Simply that there is some cause in common. Whatever it was that injured
her has injured them.” I did not quite understand his answer:--

[P110] “That is true indirectly, but not directly.”

[P111] “How do you mean, Professor?” I asked. I was a little inclined to take
his seriousness lightly--for, after all, four days of rest and freedom
from burning, harrowing anxiety does help to restore one’s spirits--but
when I saw his face, it sobered me. Never, even in the midst of our
despair about poor Lucy, had he looked more stern.

[P112] “Tell me!” I said. “I can hazard no opinion. I do not know what to
think, and I have no data on which to found a conjecture.”

[P113] “Do you mean to tell me, friend John, that you have no suspicion as to
what poor Lucy died of; not after all the hints given, not only by
events, but by me?”

[P114] “Of nervous prostration following on great loss or waste of blood.”

[P115] “And how the blood lost or waste?” I shook my head. He stepped over and
sat down beside me, and went on:--

[P116] “You are clever man, friend John; you reason well, and your wit is bold;
but you are too prejudiced. You do not let your eyes see nor your ears
hear, and that which is outside your daily life is not of account to
you. Do you not think that there are things which you cannot understand,
and yet which are; that some people see things that others cannot? But
there are things old and new which must not be contemplate by men’s
eyes, because they know--or think they know--some things which other men
have told them. Ah, it is the fault of our science that it wants to
explain all; and if it explain not, then it says there is nothing to
explain. But yet we see around us every day the growth of new beliefs,
which think themselves new; and which are yet but the old, which pretend
to be young--like the fine ladies at the opera. I suppose now you do not
believe in corporeal transference. No? Nor in materialisation. No? Nor
in astral bodies. No? Nor in the reading of thought. No? Nor in
hypnotism----”

[P117] “Yes,” I said. “Charcot has proved that pretty well.” He smiled as he
went on: “Then you are satisfied as to it. Yes? And of course then you
understand how it act, and can follow the mind of the great
Charcot--alas that he is no more!--into the very soul of the patient
that he influence. No? Then, friend John, am I to take it that you
simply accept fact, and are satisfied to let from premise to conclusion
be a blank? No? Then tell me--for I am student of the brain--how you

Methuselah lived nine hundred years, and ‘Old Parr’ one hundred and
sixty-nine, and yet that poor Lucy, with four men’s blood in her poor
veins, could not live even one day? For, had she live one more day, we
could have save her. Do you know all the mystery of life and death? Do
you know the altogether of comparative anatomy and can say wherefore the
qualities of brutes are in some men, and not in others? Can you tell me
why, when other spiders die small and soon, that one great spider lived
for centuries in the tower of the old Spanish church and grew and grew,
till, on descending, he could drink the oil of all the church lamps? Can
you tell me why in the Pampas, ay and elsewhere, there are bats that
come at night and open the veins of cattle and horses and suck dry their
veins; how in some islands of the Western seas there are bats which hang
on the trees all day, and those who have seen describe as like giant
nuts or pods, and that when the sailors sleep on the deck, because that
it is hot, flit down on them, and then--and then in the morning are
found dead men, white as even Miss Lucy was?”

[P118] “Good God, Professor!” I said, starting up. “Do you mean to tell me that
Lucy was bitten by such a bat; and that such a thing is here in London
in the nineteenth century?” He waved his hand for silence, and went
on:--

[P119] “Can you tell me why the tortoise lives more long than generations of
men; why the elephant goes on and on till he have seen dynasties; and
why the parrot never die only of bite of cat or dog or other complaint?
Can you tell me why men believe in all ages and places that there are
some few who live on always if they be permit; that there are men and
women who cannot die? We all know--because science has vouched for the
fact--that there have been toads shut up in rocks for thousands of
years, shut in one so small hole that only hold him since the youth of
the world. Can you tell me how the Indian fakir can make himself to die
and have been buried, and his grave sealed and corn sowed on it, and the
corn reaped and be cut and sown and reaped and cut again, and then men
come and take away the unbroken seal and that there lie the Indian
fakir, not dead, but that rise up and walk amongst them as before?” Here
I interrupted him. I was getting bewildered; he so crowded on my mind
his list of nature’s eccentricities and possible impossibilities that my
imagination was getting fired. I had a dim idea that he was teaching me
some lesson, as long ago he used to do in his study at Amsterdam; but
he used then to tell me the thing, so that I could have the object of
thought in mind all the time. But now I was without this help, yet I
wanted to follow him, so I said:--

[P120] “Professor, let me be your pet student again. Tell me the thesis, so
that I may apply your knowledge as you go on. At present I am going in
my mind from point to point as a mad man, and not a sane one, follows an
idea. I feel like a novice lumbering through a bog in a mist, jumping
from one tussock to another in the mere blind effort to move on without
knowing where I am going.”

[P121] “That is good image,” he said. “Well, I shall tell you. My thesis is
this: I want you to believe.”

[P122] “To believe what?”

[P123] “To believe in things that you cannot. Let me illustrate. I heard once
of an American who so defined faith: ‘that faculty which enables us to
believe things which we know to be untrue.’ For one, I follow that man.
He meant that we shall have an open mind, and not let a little bit of
truth check the rush of a big truth, like a small rock does a railway
truck. We get the small truth first. Good! We keep him, and we value
him; but all the same we must not let him think himself all the truth in
the universe.”

[P124] “Then you want me not to let some previous conviction injure the
receptivity of my mind with regard to some strange matter. Do I read
your lesson aright?”

[P125] “Ah, you are my favourite pupil still. It is worth to teach you. Now
that you are willing to understand, you have taken the first step to
understand. You think then that those so small holes in the children’s
throats were made by the same that made the hole in Miss Lucy?”

[P126] “I suppose so.” He stood up and said solemnly:--

[P127] “Then you are wrong. Oh, would it were so! but alas! no. It is worse,
far, far worse.”

[P128] “In God’s name, Professor Van Helsing, what do you mean?” I cried.

[P129] He threw himself with a despairing gesture into a chair, and placed his
elbows on the table, covering his face with his hands as he spoke:--

[P130] “They were made by Miss Lucy!”
