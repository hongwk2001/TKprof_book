[P001] CHAPTER XVII

[P002] Dr. Seward's Diary—continued

[P003] When we got to the Berkeley Hotel, Van Helsing found a telegram waiting for him:

[P004] "I am coming up by train. Jonathan is in Whitby. I have important news.—Mina Harker."

[P005] The Professor was thrilled. "Ah, that wonderful Madam Mina," he said, "a true pearl among women! She will arrive soon, but I cannot stay. She must go to your house, my friend John. You must meet her at the station. Send her a telegram while she is on her way, so she will know what to expect."

[P006a] After sending the telegram, he had a cup of tea. While drinking it, he told me about a diary Jonathan Harker kept during his time abroad in Transylvania. He gave me a typed copy of it, along with a copy of Mina Harker's diary from her time in Whitby. "Take these," he told me, "and study them carefully. By the time I come back, you will know all the facts, and we will be ready to begin our investigation. Keep them safe, because they contain very valuable information. You will need all your faith, even after everything you saw today.

[P006b] The things written here," he added, placing his hand heavily and seriously on the papers, "could be the beginning of the end for us and many others. Or, they might signal the end for the vampires that walk the earth. Please, read everything with an open mind. If you have anything to add to this story, do so, because it is extremely important. You have also kept a diary of all these strange events, haven't you? Yes!

[P006c] Then we will review everything together when we meet again." He then got ready to leave, and soon took a carriage to Liverpool Street Station. I went to Paddington Station, arriving about fifteen minutes before her train was scheduled to pull in.

[P007] The crowd cleared out in the usual rushed way you see on arrival platforms. I was starting to feel anxious that I might have missed her when a sweet-faced, delicate-looking woman walked up to me. After a quick look, she asked, "Are you Dr. Seward?"

[P008] "And you must be Mrs. Harker!" I replied instantly. At that, she offered me her hand.

[P009] "I recognized you from dear Lucy's descriptions. But..." She stopped talking suddenly, and a quick blush spread across her face.

[P010] I felt a blush rise to my own cheeks as well. Somehow, this unspoken response to her own embarrassment put both of us at ease. I gathered her luggage, which included a typewriter, and we took the Underground train to Fenchurch Street. Before we left, I had sent a telegram to my housekeeper to prepare a sitting room and bedroom for Mrs. Harker right away.

[P011] We arrived shortly after. She knew, of course, that my home was a psychiatric hospital, but I could tell she couldn't stop herself from shuddering as we walked inside.

[P012a] She asked if it would be alright to come to my study soon, as she had a lot to tell me. So, here I am, finishing my phonograph diary entry while I wait for her. I haven't had a chance to read the papers Van Helsing left with me, even though they are sitting open on my desk. I need to find a way to keep her occupied so I can have time to read them. She doesn't realize how little time we have or how massive our task is. I must be careful not to scare her.

[P012b] Oh, here she comes!

[P013] Mina Harker's Journal.

[P014] September 29.—After freshening up, I went down to Dr. Seward's study. I paused at the door because I thought I heard him talking to someone. Since he had asked me to hurry, though, I knocked. When he called out, "Come in," I opened the door.

[P015] To my great surprise, there was no one in the room with him. He was completely alone, and on the table across from him sat a device I instantly recognized from descriptions as a phonograph. I had never actually seen one before, so I was very curious.

[P016] "I hope I didn't keep you waiting," I said. "I stood at the door because I heard you talking, and I assumed someone was in here with you."

[P017] "Oh," he replied with a smile, "I was just recording my diary."

[P018] "Your diary?" I asked, surprised.

[P019] "Yes," he answered. "I keep my journal on this machine." As he spoke, he rested his hand on the phonograph. I was thrilled by the idea and blurted out:

[P020] "Wow, this is even better than shorthand! Could I hear it play something?"

[P021] "Of course," he replied cheerfully, standing up to prepare it to speak. But then he paused, and a worried look crossed his face.

[P022] "The truth is," he began awkwardly, "I only record my diary on it. And since it is almost entirely about my patients, it might be a bit awkward... I mean..." He trailed off, and I tried to help him out of his discomfort:

[P023] "You helped care for dear Lucy at the very end. Please let me hear how she died. I will be incredibly grateful for any information about her. She meant so very much to me."

[P024] To my shock, he replied with a look of absolute horror:

[P025] "Tell you about her death? Not for anything in the world!"

[P026] "Why not?" I asked, as a heavy, dreadful feeling washed over me. He paused again, and I could tell he was scrambling for an excuse. Finally, he stammered:

[P027a] "You see, I simply don't know how to find a specific part of the diary." Even as he said it, a realization hit him. He spoke in a different, almost childlike voice, with complete innocence: "That is actually true, I swear on my honor. Honest truth!" I couldn't help but smile, which made him wince. "I gave myself away that time!" he said.

[P027b] "But did you know, even though I've kept this diary for months, it never occurred to me how I would actually find a specific entry if I needed to look it up?" By this point, I had made up my mind. I knew the diary of the doctor who treated Lucy might give us more information about that terrible creature. I said boldly:

[P028] "Then, Dr. Seward, you should let me type it all out for you on my typewriter." He turned deadly pale as he replied:

[P029] "No! No! No! I would never let you know that terrible story, for anything in the world!"

[P030] So, it was terrible; my gut feeling was right! I paused for a moment to think. As I scanned the room, unconsciously looking for something to help my case, my eyes landed on a large stack of typed pages on his table. He noticed where I was looking, and without thinking, his eyes followed mine. As soon as he saw the stack of papers, he understood what I meant.

[P031] "You do not know me yet," I told him. "Once you have read those papers—my own diary and my husband's, which I typed out—you will understand me better. I have not held back a single thought or feeling for this cause. But, of course, you don't know me yet, and I shouldn't expect you to trust me this much right away."

[P032] He is truly a noble man; poor dear Lucy was right about him. He stood up, opened a large drawer filled with rows of dark wax-covered hollow metal cylinders, and said:

[P033a] "You are absolutely right. I didn't trust you because I didn't know you. But I know you now, and I should have recognized who you were long ago. I know Lucy told you about me, just as she told me about you. Will you let me make the only apology I can? Take these cylinders and listen to them. The first half-dozen are just personal entries about me, so they won't frighten you. Once you listen, you will know me better. Dinner will be ready by then.

[P033b] In the meantime, I will read some of your documents so I can better understand everything that is happening." He carried the phonograph up to my sitting room himself and set it up for me. Now I am sure I will hear something pleasant, because it will give me the other perspective of a true love story that I only know half of...

[P034] Dr. Seward's Diary.

[P035a] September 29.—I was so completely engrossed in Jonathan Harker's incredible diary and the one written by his wife that I completely lost track of time. Mrs. Harker had not come downstairs when the maid announced dinner, so I simply said, "She is probably tired; let dinner wait an hour," and went back to my work. I had just finished reading Mrs. Harker's diary when she walked into the room. She looked absolutely lovely, but terribly sad, and her eyes were red and swollen from crying. This stirred a deep emotion in me.

[P035b] God knows I have had plenty of reasons to cry lately, but I have been denied the comfort of shedding tears. Seeing her sweet eyes, wet with recent tears, went straight to my heart. So I spoke as gently as I could:

[P035c] "I am very afraid that I have upset you."

[P037] "Oh, no, you haven't upset me," she replied. "But I have been more deeply moved by your grief than words can express. That machine is wonderful, but it is also brutally honest. It revealed the sheer agony in your heart through the very tone of your voice. It sounded like a soul crying out to Almighty God. No one should ever have to hear those words spoken aloud again! Look, I have tried to make myself useful.

[P037b] I typed everything out on my typewriter, so now no one else will ever have to listen to your heart breaking, as I did."

[P038a] "No one else needs to know, and no one ever will," I said softly. She placed her hand on mine and looked at me very seriously:

[P038b] "Ah, but they have to know!"

[P040] "Have to! But why?" I asked.

[P041a] "Because it is a crucial part of this terrible story. It's part of the truth about poor dear Lucy's death and everything that caused it. In the fight ahead of us to rid the world of this horrific monster, we need all the information and help we can possibly get. I believe those cylinders contained more personal feelings than you meant for me to hear, but I can also see that your records shed a lot of light on this dark mystery. You will let me help, won't you? I know the story up to a certain point.

[P041b] Even though your diary only goes up to September 7th, I can already see how poor Lucy was stalked and how her tragic fate unfolded. Jonathan and I have been working day and night ever since Professor Van Helsing visited us. The Professor has gone to Whitby to gather more information, and he will be here tomorrow to help. We cannot have any secrets among us.

[P041c] If we work together with complete trust, we will certainly be stronger than if some of us are kept in the dark." She looked at me so pleadingly, while showing such incredible courage and determination, that I immediately gave in to her request. "You may do exactly as you wish in this matter," I told her. "God forgive me if I am making a mistake! There are still terrible things you have yet to learn. But since you have followed the path to poor Lucy's death this far, I know you won't be satisfied staying in the dark.

[P041d] In fact, the end—the very end of it all—might actually give you a small sense of peace. Come, dinner is ready. We have to keep each other strong for what lies ahead; we face a cruel and terrifying task. After you have eaten, you will learn the rest of the story. I will answer any questions you have, in case there is anything you don't understand, even if it seemed obvious to those of us who were there."

[P042] Mina Harker's Journal.

[P043] September 29.—After dinner, I went with Dr. Seward to his study. He brought the phonograph down from my room, and I brought my typewriter. He seated me in a comfortable chair, arranged the phonograph so I could reach the controls without standing up, and showed me how to pause it. Then, very considerately, he pulled up a chair and sat with his back to me so I wouldn't feel watched, and began to read his own papers. I put the metal earpieces on and listened.

[P044a] When the horrific account of Lucy's death—and everything that happened afterward—was finished, I sank back into my chair, completely drained. Thankfully, I am not the type of woman who faints easily. When Dr. Seward saw me, he jumped up with a cry of alarm, rushed to a cabinet to grab a bottle, and gave me some brandy. Within a few minutes, I felt somewhat better.

[P044b] My mind was spinning with the unbelievable horrors I had just heard. The only thing that kept me from breaking down completely was the holy, comforting thought that my dear, sweet Lucy was finally at peace. The whole story is so wild, mysterious, and bizarre that if I hadn't already known about Jonathan's terrifying experience in Transylvania, I never would have believed it. As it was, I didn't know what to think, so I coped with my overwhelming emotions by focusing on work. I took the cover off my typewriter and said to Dr. Seward:

[P045a] "Let me type all of this out right now. We need to have everything ready for Dr. Van Helsing when he arrives. I sent a telegram to Jonathan, telling him to come straight here when his train from Whitby gets to London. In this situation, the exact dates and timelines are everything. If we get all our materials prepared and organize every detail in chronological order, we will have accomplished a great deal. You mentioned that Lord Godalming and Mr. Morris are coming as well.

[P045b] We need to be ready to brief them when they get here." Agreeing with my plan, he set the phonograph to play at a slower speed, and I began typing from the start of the seventh cylinder. I used carbon paper to make three copies of the diary, just as I had done with all our other documents.

[P045c] It was quite late by the time I finished, but Dr. Seward had left to make his rounds checking on his patients. When he was done, he came back and sat near me, reading, so I wouldn't feel alone while I worked. He is such a good and thoughtful man; the world seems to be full of good men, even if monsters exist in it too. Before I left his study, I remembered Jonathan writing in his diary about how Professor Van Helsing had been deeply disturbed by something he read in an evening newspaper at the Exeter train station.

[P045d] Noticing that Dr. Seward kept copies of his newspapers, I borrowed his files of The Westminster Gazette and The Pall Mall Gazette and took them to my room. I remember how much the newspaper clippings I had saved from The Dailygraph and The Whitby Gazette helped us piece together the terrifying events that happened in Whitby when Count Dracula first arrived. So, I plan to look through the evening papers from that time; maybe I will find some new clues. I am not feeling sleepy at all, and the work will help calm my nerves.

[P046] Dr. Seward's Diary.

[P047] September 30.—Mr. Harker arrived at nine o'clock this morning. He received his wife's telegram just before he left. Judging from his appearance, he seems incredibly intelligent and full of energy. If his journal is true—and considering our own bizarre experiences, it almost certainly is—he is also a man of immense courage. Going down into that vampire's vault for a second time was a breathtaking act of bravery. After reading his account, I expected to meet a rugged, imposing man, but the quiet, professional gentleman who showed up today was quite a surprise.

[P048] *       *       *       *       *

[P049a] Later.—After lunch, Harker and his wife went back to their room. When I walked past a little while ago, I heard the rapid clicking of the typewriter. They are working intensely. Mrs. Harker told me they are piecing together every scrap of evidence they have in exact chronological order. Harker managed to get copies of the letters exchanged between the person who received the boxes in Whitby and the delivery men who transported them in London. He is currently reading the typed copy of my diary that his wife made. I wonder what they will conclude from it.

[P049b] Ah, here it is...

[P050a] It's unbelievable that it never occurred to me that the house right next door might be the Count's hideout! Heaven knows we had more than enough clues from the behavior of my patient, Renfield! The letters documenting the purchase of the house were included with the typed pages. Oh, if only we had seen them sooner, we might have been able to save poor Lucy! But I have to stop thinking like that; going down that path will only drive me crazy! Harker has gone back to their room and is organizing his materials again.

[P050b] He says that by dinner, they should have a complete, cohesive timeline to show us. In the meantime, he suggested I check on Renfield, since the man's behavior has acted as a sort of gauge for the Count's movements in the past. I don't entirely see the connection yet, but once I review the timeline, I suppose it will make sense. Thank goodness Mrs. Harker typed out my phonograph recordings! We never would have been able to track the dates accurately otherwise...

[P051a] I found Renfield sitting peacefully in his room, hands folded, smiling calmly. At that moment, he seemed as perfectly sane as anyone I have ever met. I sat down and we chatted about various topics, and he discussed all of them rationally. Then, completely on his own, he brought up the idea of going home—something he has never once mentioned during his entire stay here. In fact, he spoke very confidently about being discharged immediately.

[P051b] I truly believe that if I hadn't spoken with Harker and matched the dates of Renfield's violent outbursts with the letters, I would have been ready to sign his release papers after just a little more observation. However, as things stand, I am deeply suspicious. All of his previous manic episodes were somehow connected to the Count being nearby. So, what does this current, perfect contentment mean? Could it be that his twisted instincts are satisfied because he believes the vampire has ultimately won?

[P051c] Wait a minute; Renfield is obsessed with consuming life, and during his wild fits outside the abandoned chapel next door, he constantly shouted about his "master." This all seems to confirm our theory. I left shortly after; my patient is acting a little too sane right now, making it dangerous to interrogate him too heavily. If I push him, he might start analyzing my questions, and then—who knows what he might do! So, I left him alone. I don't trust these calm periods of his.

[P051d] I gave the orderly a quiet warning to keep a very close eye on him and to have a straitjacket ready just in case.

[P052] Jonathan Harker's Journal.

[P053a] September 29, on the train to London.—When I received Mr. Billington's polite message offering to help me in any way he could, I decided the best course of action was to go down to Whitby and investigate in person. My main goal was to track the Count's horrific cargo of earth boxes to its final destination in London. If we find them, we might be able to deal with them later. Billington Jr., a polite young man, met me at the station and took me to his father's house, where they insisted I stay the night.

[P053b] They are incredibly welcoming, showing true Yorkshire hospitality: they offer a guest everything while leaving him completely free to do as he pleases. They understood that I was busy and that my visit would be short, so Mr. Billington had all the shipping documents for the boxes waiting in his office. It made my stomach drop to recognize one of the letters; I had seen it on the Count's table before I ever realized his evil plans. Everything had been meticulously planned out and executed with systematic precision.

[P053c] He seemed to have anticipated every possible obstacle that might accidentally disrupt his plans. To use an American phrase, he had "taken no chances," and the flawless execution of his instructions was simply the logical result of his careful planning.

[P053d] I reviewed the invoice and took notes: "Fifty cases of common earth, to be used for experimental purposes." I also obtained copies of a letter sent to the London delivery company, Carter Paterson, and their reply. Since this was all the information Mr. Billington had, I went down to the port and spoke with the coastguards, the customs officers, and the harbormaster.

[P053e] They all had something to say about the bizarre arrival of the ship, which is already becoming a local legend, but no one could provide any more details beyond the simple description: "Fifty cases of common earth." I then spoke with the stationmaster, who kindly introduced me to the men who had physically unloaded the boxes. Their records matched the invoice exactly. They had nothing to add, except that the boxes were "deadly heavy" and that moving them was incredibly thirsty work.

[P053f] One of them complained that it was unfair that a gentleman "like yourself, sir" hadn't been around at the time to show some appreciation for their hard work by buying them a drink. Another man chimed in, claiming that the thirst they worked up that day was so severe that even now, they hadn't fully quenched it. Naturally, I made sure to permanently and generously solve this "problem" by giving them some money for drinks before I left.

[P054] *       *       *       *       *

[P055] September 30.—The Whitby stationmaster kindly gave me a note to pass on to his old friend, the stationmaster at King's Cross in London. When I arrived there in the morning, I was able to ask him about the boxes' arrival. He immediately introduced me to the right officials, and I confirmed that their records also perfectly matched the original invoice. The workers here didn't have as many opportunities to work up a massive thirst, but they had clearly made the most of what chances they had. Once again, I felt obligated to reward their past efforts with some cash.

[P056a] From there, I went to Carter Paterson's main office, where I was treated with the utmost courtesy. They looked up the transaction in their daily logs and correspondence books, and immediately called their King's Cross branch for more details. Luckily, the men who drove the delivery carts were waiting for assignments, so the manager sent them over right away. He also had one of them bring the waybill and all the paperwork related to delivering the boxes to the Carfax estate.

[P056b] The records matched perfectly once again. The delivery men were able to add a few colorful details to the dry paperwork. As I quickly learned, their stories mostly revolved around how incredibly dusty the job was, and how this dust, naturally, gave them a terrible thirst. After I offered a few coins to help them quench this "terrible thirst" later, one of the men remarked:

[P057] "That house, governor, is the weirdest place I've ever been in. Blimey! It hasn't been touched in a hundred years. The dust was so thick you could've slept on it without hurting your bones. And the place was so neglected it smelled ancient. But the old chapel—that took the cake, it did! My mate and I couldn't get out of there fast enough. Lord, I wouldn't stay in there after dark for a million pounds."

[P058] Having actually been inside the house myself, I completely believed him. But if he knew the truth about what was really in there, I think he would have asked for a lot more money.

[P059] I am now completely certain of one thing: all fifty boxes that arrived in Whitby from Varna on the Demeter were successfully delivered to the old chapel at Carfax. There should still be fifty of them there, unless some have been moved since—which, based on Dr. Seward's diary, I am afraid might be the case.

[P060] I will try to track down the driver who moved some of the boxes away from Carfax when Renfield attacked the cart. If we follow that lead, we might learn a lot more.

[P061] *       *       *       *       *

[P062] Later.—Mina and I have been working all day, and we finally have all the papers organized.

[P063] Mina Harker's Journal

[P064a] September 30.—I am so happy I can barely contain myself. I think it's the relief washing over me after the terrifying fear I've been carrying: that this horrific situation and reopening his old psychological wounds might break Jonathan. I put on the bravest face I could when he left for Whitby, but inside, I was sick with worry. However, taking action has actually done him good. I have never seen him so determined, so strong, or so full of explosive energy.

[P064b] It is exactly like dear Professor Van Helsing said: Jonathan has true grit, and he actually grows stronger under pressure that would crush a weaker man. He came back full of life, hope, and resolve. We have everything prepared for tonight. I am wild with excitement. I suppose a person should normally feel pity for anyone being hunted down like the Count. But that's the thing: this creature is not human—it isn't even an animal. Reading Dr. Seward's account of poor Lucy's death and the nightmare that followed is enough to kill any drop of pity in a person's heart.

[P065] *       *       *       *       *

[P066a] Later.—Lord Godalming and Mr. Morris arrived earlier than we expected. Dr. Seward was out handling some business and had taken Jonathan with him, so I had to greet them. It was a painful meeting for me, as it brought back all the memories of poor dear Lucy's bright hopes from just a few months ago. Naturally, they had heard Lucy talk about me, and it seems Dr. Van Helsing had also been singing my praises—or, as Mr.

[P066b] Morris put it, "blowing my trumpet." The poor men had no idea that I knew all about their marriage proposals to Lucy. Unsure of how much I actually knew, they didn't know quite what to say or do, so they kept the conversation to safe, neutral topics. However, after thinking it over, I decided the best thing to do was to bring them completely up to speed. Thanks to Dr.

[P066c] Seward's diary, I knew they had both been present for Lucy's true, final death, so I didn't need to worry about accidentally revealing any secrets too soon. I explained as best I could that I had read all the relevant diaries and papers, and that my husband and I had just finished typing them out and organizing them chronologically. I gave each of them a copy to read in the library. When Lord Godalming received his stack—which makes for a rather thick pile of papers—he asked:

[P067] "Did you type all of this yourself, Mrs. Harker?"

[P068] I nodded, and he continued:

[P069a] "I don't completely understand what all this means yet. But you people are so good and kind, and you have been working so incredibly hard, that all I can do is trust your judgment completely and try to help. I've recently learned a harsh lesson about accepting unbelievable facts—a lesson that will keep me humble for the rest of my life. Besides, I know you loved my poor Lucy—" He stopped mid-sentence, turned away, and buried his face in his hands. I could hear the tears choking his voice. Showing an instinctive, quiet politeness, Mr.

[P069b] Morris simply rested a hand on his friend's shoulder for a second before silently leaving the room. I think there is something in a woman's nature that makes a man feel safe enough to break down and show his tender, emotional side without feeling like he is losing his masculinity. Because as soon as Lord Godalming and I were alone, he collapsed onto the sofa and wept openly, holding nothing back. I sat down beside him and took his hand. I hope he didn't think I was being too forward, and I hope he never feels embarrassed about it later.

[P069c] But I shouldn't misjudge him; I know he won't—he is a true gentleman. Seeing that his heart was utterly broken, I said to him:

[P070] "I loved dear Lucy, and I know exactly what you meant to each other. She and I were like sisters. Now that she is gone, will you let me be like a sister to you through this terrible time? I know you have suffered incredible heartbreak, even if I can't fully comprehend the depth of it. If sympathy and compassion can offer you any comfort at all, please let me help you—for Lucy's sake."

[P071] In a flash, the poor man was completely overwhelmed by his grief. It was as if all the silent suffering he had endured recently suddenly found a way out. He became almost hysterical, raising his hands and clapping his palms together in sheer agony. He stood up, then immediately sat back down, tears pouring down his face. I felt an overwhelming wave of pity for him and instinctively opened my arms. With a heavy sob, he rested his head on my shoulder and cried like an exhausted child, his whole body shaking with emotion.

[P072] When a situation calls for it, women possess a maternal instinct that allows us to look past social awkwardness. I felt this large, grieving man resting his head on my shoulder—much like the baby I hope to hold one day—and I stroked his hair as if he were my own child. At the time, I didn't even realize how unusual the situation was.

[P073a] After a few minutes, his sobbing slowed, and he sat up. He apologized politely, though he didn't try to hide his emotions. He told me that for days and nights—endless, sleepless nights—he hadn't been able to talk to anyone, even though a man desperately needs to talk during a time of such deep grief. Because of the horrific circumstances surrounding Lucy's death, there was no woman he could speak to freely or receive comfort from. "I realize now just how much I was suffering," he said, wiping his eyes.

[P073b] "But even I don't fully understand yet—and no one else ever will—how much your sweet kindness has meant to me today. I will understand it better as time passes. Please believe me when I say that, while I am incredibly grateful right now, my gratitude will only grow as I fully grasp what you've done. You will let me be like a brother to you, won't you, for the rest of our lives—for dear Lucy's sake?"

[P074a] "For dear Lucy's sake," I agreed, as we held hands. "Yes, and for your own sake as well," he added. "Because if a man's respect and gratitude are worth anything at all, you have certainly earned mine today. If there ever comes a day when you need a man's help, believe me, you will not ask in vain. I pray to God that no dark days ever ruin the sunshine of your life.

[P074b] But if they ever do, promise me you will let me know." He was so sincere, and his grief was so raw, that I knew agreeing would comfort him, so I said:

[P075] "I promise."

[P076] As I walked down the hallway, I saw Mr. Morris looking out a window. He turned when he heard my footsteps. "How is Art doing?" he asked. Then, noticing my red, teary eyes, he continued: "Ah, I see you have been comforting him. Poor guy! He really needs it. When a man is suffering from a broken heart, only a woman can truly help him—and he hasn't had anyone to comfort him."

[P077] He was handling his own grief so bravely that my heart ached for him. I noticed the stack of typed pages in his hand, and I knew that once he read them, he would realize exactly how much I knew about his past with Lucy. So, I said to him:

[P078] "I wish I could bring comfort to everyone suffering from a broken heart. Will you let me be your friend, and will you come to me for comfort if you ever need it? You will understand why I'm saying this later." He saw how serious I was. He leaned down, took my hand, and gently kissed it. That seemed like such a small comfort for such a brave and selfless man, so, acting on impulse, I leaned forward and kissed his forehead. Tears filled his eyes, and he choked up for a second before saying very calmly:

[P079] "Little girl, I promise you will never regret showing such genuine kindness, for as long as you live!" Then he turned and walked into the study to join his friend.

[P080] "Little girl!"—those were the exact same words he used to describe Lucy. And oh, he certainly proved himself to be a true friend!
