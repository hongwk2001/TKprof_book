CHAPTER XXVII

MINA HARKER'S JOURNAL

1 November.—All day long we have traveled, and at a good pace. The horses seem to know they are being well cared for, as they willingly run their full distance at top speed. We have changed horses many times (a common practice during long carriage journeys to replace exhausted animals), and the constant kindness we receive gives us hope that the journey will be smooth. Dr. Van Helsing is quiet; he simply tells the farmers that he must hurry to Bistritz and pays them generously for the fresh horses. We grab hot soup, coffee, or tea, and set off again. It is a beautiful countryside, full of every imaginable charm, and the people are brave, strong, simple, and seem to have good hearts. However, they are incredibly superstitious. At the first house where we stopped, the woman serving us saw the scar on my forehead, crossed herself, and pointed two fingers at me to ward off the evil eye (a curse believed to be cast by a malevolent glare). I suspect they even went out of their way to put extra garlic in our food, and I absolutely despise garlic. Since then, I have been careful not to take off my hat or veil to avoid their suspicion. We are moving quickly, and without a hired driver to gossip, we stay ahead of any rumors. Yet, I am sure the fear of the evil eye will closely follow us the entire way. The Professor seems tireless; he refused to rest all day, though he made me sleep for a long stretch. At sunset, he hypnotized me, and he said I gave the usual answer: "darkness, lapping water, and creaking wood." This means our enemy is still on the river. I am terrified when I think of Jonathan (Mina's husband), but somehow I no longer feel fear for him or myself. I am writing this while we wait in a farmhouse for the horses to be readied. Dr. Van Helsing is asleep. Poor dear, he looks so tired, old, and gray, but his mouth is set as firmly as a conqueror's; even in his sleep, he radiates determination. Once we get going, I must insist he rests while I drive. I will remind him that we still have days ahead of us, and he cannot break down when his strength will be needed most.... Everything is ready; we are leaving shortly.

2 November, morning.—I succeeded, and we took turns driving throughout the night. Now it is morning, bright but cold. There is a strange heaviness in the air—I call it heaviness for lack of a better word; it feels like an oppressive weight on us both. It is freezing, and only our thick furs (winter clothing of the time) keep us warm. At dawn, Van Helsing hypnotized me. He said I answered, "darkness, creaking wood, and roaring water," which suggests the river is growing rougher as they head upstream. I pray my darling husband faces no more danger than absolutely necessary, but we are in God's hands now.

2 November, night.—We have been driving all day. The landscape grows wilder as we progress, and the massive ridges of the Carpathian Mountains, which seemed so low and distant on the horizon at Veresti, now appear to surround us and tower ahead. We both seem to be in good spirits; I think we are each trying to cheer the other up, and in doing so, we comfort ourselves. Dr. Van Helsing says we will reach the Borgo Pass by morning. There are very few houses here now, and the Professor says we will likely have to keep our current horses for the rest of the journey, as we may not find replacements. He managed to secure two extra horses along with the two we exchanged, so we are now driving a rough four-in-hand (a carriage pulled by four horses). The good horses are patient and gentle, causing us no trouble. With no other travelers around, even I can take the reins. We plan to reach the Pass during daylight; we do not want to arrive any earlier. So, we are taking it easy and resting in shifts. Oh, what will tomorrow bring? We are heading to the very place where my poor darling suffered so terribly. May God guide us safely, and may He watch over my husband and all our loved ones who are in such deadly peril. As for me, I am unworthy in His eyes. Alas! I am unclean (having been bitten by the vampire) and will remain so until He allows me to stand before Him as one who has not drawn His wrath.

Memorandum by Abraham Van Helsing.

4 November.—I am leaving this for my old and true friend, Dr. John Seward of Purfleet, London, just in case I do not survive to see him again. It may help explain things. It is morning, and I am writing by a fire I kept burning all night with Madam Mina's help. It is bitterly cold; the heavy, gray sky is loaded with snow, and when it falls, it will stay for the winter as the ground is freezing solid. The cold seems to have affected Madam Mina. Her head was so heavy yesterday that she did not act like herself. She sleeps, and sleeps, and sleeps! Normally so sharp and alert, she did absolutely nothing all day and even lost her appetite. She didn't write a single word in her little diary, which she usually updates at every spare moment. Something tells me that things are going wrong. However, tonight she is much more lively. Her long day of sleep seems to have refreshed her, and she is as sweet and bright as ever. At sunset, I tried to hypnotize her, but alas, it didn't work. The power of the hypnosis has been fading every day, and tonight it failed completely. Well, God's will be done—whatever it is, and wherever it leads!

Now for the record. Since Madam Mina is not writing in her shorthand (a method of rapid writing), I must use my clumsy, old-fashioned writing so that none of our days go unrecorded.

We reached the Borgo Pass just after sunrise yesterday morning. When I saw dawn approaching, I prepared for the hypnosis. We stopped the carriage and got out to avoid any disturbance. I made a bed of furs, and Madam Mina lay down and entered the hypnotic sleep as usual, though it took longer and lasted for a shorter time. The answer was the same: "darkness and swirling water." Then she woke up, bright and radiant, and we continued on, soon reaching the Pass. At that exact time and place, she was suddenly filled with intense passion. Some new guiding force seemed to awaken in her, and she pointed to a road, saying:

"This is the way."

"How do you know?" I asked.

"Of course I know," she replied, pausing before adding, "Didn't my Jonathan travel this road and write about it?"

At first, I thought it was strange, but I soon realized there was only one such side road. It was rarely used, very different from the wide, hard, and busy coach road leading from Bukovina to Bistritz.

So we took this road. Whenever it intersected with other paths—sometimes it was hard to even tell if they were roads because they were neglected and lightly covered in snow—only the horses seemed to know the way. I let them guide us, and they moved forward patiently. Eventually, we began to recognize the landmarks Jonathan had described in his remarkable diary. We traveled for hours upon hours. At first, I told Madam Mina to sleep, and she managed to do so. She slept continuously until I grew suspicious and tried to wake her. But she stayed asleep, and nothing I did could rouse her. I didn't want to try too hard for fear of hurting her; I know she has suffered deeply, and sleep is sometimes the only comfort she has. I must have dozed off myself, for I suddenly woke with a start, feeling guilty as if I had made a terrible mistake. I was sitting straight up, reins in hand, while the good horses steadily jogged along. I looked down and saw Madam Mina still sleeping. It was nearing sunset, and the bright yellow sunlight reflected off the snow, casting long shadows against the steep mountains. We were climbing higher and higher into a landscape so wild and rocky it felt like the end of the world.

Then I woke Madam Mina. She woke up fairly easily this time, and I tried to put her into a hypnotic trance. But she wouldn't sleep, acting as if I wasn't even there. I kept trying until suddenly we found ourselves in the dark; I looked around and realized the sun had set. Madam Mina laughed, and I turned to look at her. She was fully awake and looked healthier than I had seen her since that night at the Carfax estate when we first entered the Count's home. I was amazed and uneasy, but she was so bright, tender, and caring toward me that I forgot my fears. I lit a fire with the wood we brought, and she prepared food while I unharnessed the horses, tied them up in a sheltered spot, and fed them. When I returned to the fire, my supper was ready. I offered to help her, but she smiled and said she was so hungry she couldn't wait and had already eaten. I didn't like this and had serious doubts, but I stayed quiet, not wanting to frighten her. She kept me company while I ate alone. Afterward, we wrapped ourselves in furs by the fire. I told her to sleep while I kept watch. But soon, I forgot all about watching. When I suddenly remembered I was supposed to be on guard, I found her lying quietly, wide awake, staring at me with unnervingly bright eyes. This happened a couple more times, and I ended up sleeping quite a bit before morning. When I woke up, I tried to hypnotize her again. She obediently closed her eyes, but she couldn't sleep. The sun rose higher and higher, and sleep finally claimed her, but it was so deep she wouldn't wake up. I had to harness the horses, pack everything, and physically lift her sleeping body into the carriage. She continued to sleep, looking healthier and having more color in her face than before. I didn't like it. I am terrified, truly terrified! I fear everything—even my own thoughts—but I must press on. We are playing for life and death, perhaps even more, and we cannot back down.

5 November, morning.—I need to write everything down accurately. Even though you and I have seen bizarre things together, you might initially think that I, Van Helsing, have finally lost my mind—that the countless horrors and the prolonged strain have finally broken me.

We traveled all day yesterday, getting closer to the mountains and moving into increasingly wild and desolate terrain. There were massive, intimidating cliffs and roaring waterfalls; it looked as if nature itself had once thrown a violent carnival here. Madam Mina just slept and slept. Even when I got hungry and ate, I couldn't wake her to feed her. I started to worry that the dark magic of this place was affecting her, corrupted as she is by that vampire baptism (having drank the Count's blood). "Well," I told myself, "if she sleeps all day, then I must not sleep at night." As we bumped along the rough, ancient, and broken road, my head dropped and I fell asleep. I woke up again with a sense of guilt and lost time, finding Madam Mina still asleep and the sun low in the sky. But the scenery had completely changed. The looming mountains seemed further away, and we were near the top of a steep hill. At the summit stood the very castle Jonathan described in his diary. I was filled with both triumph and terror; for better or worse, the end was near.

We traveled all day yesterday, getting closer to the mountains and moving into increasingly wild and desolate terrain. There were massive, intimidating cliffs and roaring waterfalls; it looked as if nature itself had once thrown a violent carnival here. Madam Mina just slept and slept. Even when I got hungry and ate, I couldn't wake her to feed her. I started to worry that the dark magic of this place was affecting her, corrupted as she is by that vampire baptism (having drank the Count's blood). "Well," I told myself, "if she sleeps all day, then I must not sleep at night." As we bumped along the rough, ancient, and broken road, my head dropped and I fell asleep. I woke up again with a sense of guilt and lost time, finding Madam Mina still asleep and the sun low in the sky. But the scenery had completely changed. The looming mountains seemed further away, and we were near the top of a steep hill. At the summit stood the very castle Jonathan described in his diary. I was filled with both triumph and terror; for better or worse, the end was near.

I woke Madam Mina and tried to hypnotize her again, but alas, it was no use and far too late. Then, before total darkness fell—for even after sunset, the sky reflected the lingering light on the snow, creating a bright twilight for a time—I unhitched the horses and fed them in whatever shelter I could find. Then I built a fire and made Madam Mina, now fully awake and more charming than ever, sit comfortably among her blankets. I prepared food, but she refused to eat, simply saying she wasn't hungry. I didn't push her, knowing it would be useless. But I forced myself to eat because I knew I needed to be strong for whatever was coming. Then, filled with dread about what might happen, I drew a large circle around where Madam Mina was sitting for her comfort and safety. Over the line of the circle, I crumbled pieces of the Holy Wafer (consecrated bread used in Catholic Communion), ensuring she was completely protected. She sat completely still the entire time—as still as a corpse. She grew paler and paler until she was whiter than the snow around us, and she didn't speak a single word. But when I stepped closer, she clung to me, and I could feel the poor soul trembling from head to toe in agonizing fear. When she had calmed down a bit, I asked her:

"Won't you come over to the fire?" I wanted to test what she was capable of doing. She obediently stood up, but after taking one step, she froze as if struck by paralysis.

"Why not keep going?" I asked. She shook her head, went back, and sat down in her place. Then, looking at me with wide eyes like someone freshly awoken from a deep sleep, she simply said:

"I cannot!" and fell silent. I rejoiced because I knew that if she couldn't cross the barrier, neither could any of the monsters we feared. Even if her physical body was in danger, her soul was safe!

Suddenly, the horses began to scream and wildly pulled at their tethers. I rushed over and calmed them down. When they felt my hands on them, they whinnied softly as if relieved, licked my hands, and quieted down for a while. Throughout the night, I had to go to them repeatedly, right up until that freezing hour before dawn when all of nature is at its lowest point. Every time I went to them, they settled down. During that coldest hour, the fire began to die out, and I was just about to step outside the circle to add more wood when a fierce snowstorm blew in, accompanied by a chilling mist. Despite the darkness, the snow cast a strange glow. The swirling snow and mist seemed to take the shape of women trailing long gowns. Everything was dead silent except for the horses, who cowered and whinnied in pure terror. I began to feel an overwhelming, horrible fear, but then the safety of the circle I stood in comforted me. I tried to convince myself that my imagination was running wild because of the night, the gloom, and the immense anxiety I had been enduring. It felt as though Jonathan's horrific memories were playing tricks on me because the snowflakes and mist continued to swirl until I could clearly make out the shadowy silhouettes of the very women who had tried to kiss him. The horses crouched even lower, groaning in pain and terror. They were so paralyzed by fear that they couldn't even attempt to break free. As these weird figures drew closer and circled us, I worried for my dear Madam Mina. I looked at her, but she sat calmly and smiled at me. When I tried to step toward the fire to add wood, she grabbed me, pulled me back, and whispered in a voice so low it sounded like something from a dream:

"No! No! Do not go outside. You are safe here!" I turned to her, looked into her eyes, and said:

"But what about you? It is you I am afraid for!" She let out a low, unsettling laugh and replied:

"Fear for me? Why fear for me? No one in the world is safer from them than I am." As I wondered what she meant, a gust of wind made the fire flare up, and I clearly saw the red scar on her forehead. Then, alas, I understood. Even if I hadn't realized it then, I would have soon enough, because the swirling figures of mist and snow moved closer, staying just outside the Holy circle. They began to materialize until—unless God had completely taken my sanity, for I saw it with my own eyes—the very same three women Jonathan had seen in the castle room stood before me in the flesh. I recognized their swaying bodies, bright, piercing eyes, white teeth, flushed skin, and voluptuous lips. They kept smiling at poor, dear Madam Mina. As their laughter cut through the silence of the night, they linked their arms, pointed at her, and spoke in those unbearable, sweet, tingling tones Jonathan described, sounding like musical glasses:

"Come, sister. Come to us. Come! Come!" In absolute terror, I turned to my poor Madam Mina, but my heart leaped with sudden joy. The terror, repulsion, and sheer horror in her sweet eyes told me everything I needed to know. Thank God, she was not yet one of them. I grabbed a piece of firewood next to me and, holding out a piece of the Holy Wafer, marched toward them and the fire. They shrank back, laughing their low, horrifying laugh. I stoked the fire, no longer afraid, knowing we were safe inside our protected circle. Armed as I was, they couldn't come near me, nor could they reach Madam Mina as long as she stayed inside the ring—which she couldn't leave any more than they could enter. The horses had stopped groaning and lay dead still on the ground; the snow gently covered them, turning them white. I knew the poor beasts would never feel terror again.

We stayed like that until the red light of dawn finally broke through the snowy gloom. I was exhausted, terrified, and full of sorrow, but when the beautiful sun started to climb above the horizon, I felt alive again. At the very first sign of dawn, the horrific figures melted back into the swirling mist and snow; the transparent, ghostly shapes drifted away toward the castle and vanished.

As day broke, I instinctively turned to Madam Mina, intending to hypnotize her. But she had fallen into a deep, sudden sleep from which I couldn't wake her. I tried to hypnotize her while she slept, but she didn't respond at all. The day had officially begun. I am still too afraid to move. I built up the fire and checked the horses; they are all dead. I have a lot of work to do here today, and I am waiting until the sun is high in the sky. Some of the places I need to go will only be safe if the sunlight—even if obscured by snow and mist—can reach me.

I will eat breakfast to build my strength, and then I must begin my terrible work. Madam Mina is still sleeping, and thank God, she looks peaceful....

Dr. Van Helsing's Memorandum.

5 November, afternoon.—At least I am still sane. I thank God for that mercy, even though proving it has been an absolute nightmare. After leaving Madam Mina sleeping safely inside the Holy circle, I made my way to the castle. The blacksmith's hammer I brought from Veresti proved very useful. Although the doors were all open, I smashed them off their rusty hinges just in case some evil trap or accident closed them and trapped me inside. Jonathan's bitter experience served me well here. Relying on my memory of his diary, I found my way to the old chapel, knowing my grim task awaited me there. The air was suffocating; it smelled strongly of sulfur, which made me dizzy at times. I couldn't tell if my ears were ringing or if I was hearing the distant howl of wolves. Then I thought of my dear Madam Mina, and I found myself in a terrible dilemma.

I hadn't dared bring her into this foul place, leaving her safely away from the Vampires in the Holy circle. But even there, she was vulnerable to the wolves! I decided my duty was here and that we would simply have to leave the wolves in God's hands. At worst, death would bring her freedom. So I made the choice for her. If it were only me, the choice would have been easy: it's better to rest in the belly of a wolf than in a Vampire's tomb! So I resolved to continue my work.

I knew there were at least three occupied tombs I needed to find. I searched and searched, and finally found one of them. She lay there in her Vampire sleep, radiating so much life and voluptuous beauty that I shuddered, feeling as if I had come to commit cold-blooded murder. Ah, I have no doubt that in the old days, many men who set out to do what I must do lost their nerve at the very last moment. They would delay and hesitate until the sheer beauty and seductive charm of the wanton Un-Dead hypnotized them. They would linger until sunset when the Vampire sleep ended. Then, the beautiful eyes of the fair woman would open with feigned love, her sensuous lips would offer a kiss—and man is weak. Another victim would join the Vampire's fold, swelling the gruesome ranks of the Un-Dead!...

There is definitely a dark magic at play. Even though she lay in a dusty, ancient tomb reeking of the Count's foul lair, I was moved by her mere presence. Yes, I—Van Helsing, armed with purpose and burning hatred—was moved. A sudden urge to delay washed over me, paralyzing my mind and clogging my very soul. Perhaps my exhaustion or the strange, oppressive air was finally getting to me. Whatever the reason, I was slipping into an open-eyed trance, yielding to a sickeningly sweet fascination. Just then, piercing through the quiet snowy air, came a long, low wail. It was so full of sorrow and pity that it snapped me awake like a bugle call. It was the voice of my dear Madam Mina.

I braced myself for the gruesome task and, prying open more tombs, found the second sister, the dark-haired one. I didn't dare look at her as long as I had the first, terrified I might fall under their spell again. I kept searching until I found a massive, elevated tomb, clearly meant for someone deeply loved. Inside was the third sister—the breathtakingly fair one Jonathan had seen materialize from the mist. She was so radiantly beautiful and exquisitely seductive that every protective, masculine instinct in me flared up, making my head spin with conflicting emotions. But thank God, Madam Mina's soul-wrenching cry still echoed in my ears. Before the monster's spell could take hold, I forced myself to begin my brutal work. By then, I had searched all the tombs I could find in the chapel. Since we only saw three Un-Dead phantoms last night, I assumed there were no other active Vampires here. But there was one massive tomb, far grander than the rest. It was huge and perfectly proportioned. Only one word was carved into it:

DRACULA.

So this was the resting place of the King-Vampire himself, the monster responsible for all the others. Its emptiness confirmed what I already knew. Before I began my awful task of returning these women to true death, I placed pieces of the Holy Wafer inside Dracula's tomb, banishing him from it forever.

Then my terrible task began, and I dreaded every second of it. If there had only been one, it might have been relatively easy. But three! To force myself to repeat the horrific act twice more... It was hard enough with sweet Miss Lucy, but how much worse would it be with these ancient creatures, strengthened by centuries of blood? I knew they would fight fiercely for their unnatural lives if they could.

Oh, my friend John, it was butcher's work. If I hadn't been driven by the memory of the dead and the desperate need to protect the living from this nightmare, I could never have gone through with it. I am still trembling. Thank God my nerves held until it was over. If I hadn't seen the peaceful relief that washed over their faces the moment before they crumbled to dust—the realization that their souls were finally free—I couldn't have continued the slaughter. The horrific shrieking as the stake drove through their hearts, the violent thrashing, the bloody foam bubbling from their lips... I would have fled in terror and left the job unfinished. But it is done! Now I can pity those poor souls and weep for them, remembering how they looked perfectly at peace in their true death for just a moment before fading away. Because, friend John, barely had my knife severed their heads before their entire bodies began to melt and crumble into dust. It was as if the death that should have claimed them centuries ago finally woke up and shouted, "I am here!"

Before leaving the castle, I sealed the entrances so the Count can never return there as an Un-Dead.

When I stepped back into the circle where Madam Mina slept, she woke up, looked at me, and cried out in pain, sensing that I had endured too much.

"Come!" she said. "Let's leave this awful place! We must go meet my husband. I know he is coming toward us." She looked thin, pale, and weak, but her eyes were clear and glowed with fiery determination. I was actually relieved to see her pale and sickly because my mind was still haunted by the disturbing, ruddy glow of the Vampires' sleep.

And so, filled with trust and hope, yet trembling with fear, we head east to meet our friends—and the monster—who, according to Madam Mina, are all rushing toward us.

Mina Harker's Journal.

6 November.—It was late in the afternoon when the Professor and I headed east, the direction I knew Jonathan was coming from. We couldn't travel quickly because the path was a steep downhill, and we had to carry heavy blankets and coats; we couldn't risk being caught in the freezing snow without warmth. We also had to bring some of our food supplies since we were in total isolation, and through the heavy snowfall, there wasn't a single sign of human life. After walking for about a mile, the heavy load exhausted me, and I sat down to rest. Looking back, we saw the sharp outline of Dracula's castle against the sky. We had descended so far down the hill it was built on that the surrounding Carpathian mountains appeared much lower from our perspective. We could see the castle in all its terrifying glory, perched a thousand feet up on the edge of a sheer cliff, completely separated by massive drops from the neighboring mountains on every side. There was something wild and deeply unsettling about the place. Far off in the distance, we could hear the howling of wolves. They were far away, but even muffled by the heavy snowfall, the sound was full of terror. Seeing Dr. Van Helsing scanning the area, I knew he was looking for a strategic defensive position where we would be less exposed if attacked. The rough road continued downward, and we could still trace its path through the snowdrifts.

Shortly after, the Professor signaled to me, so I got up and went to him. He had found an excellent hiding spot—a natural hollow inside a rock with an entrance shaped like a doorway between two large boulders. He took my hand and pulled me inside. "Look!" he said. "You'll be safe here. If the wolves come, I can fight them off one by one at the entrance." He brought in our furs, made a cozy nest for me, unpacked some food, and urged me to eat. But I couldn't stomach it; the mere thought of eating was repulsive. As much as I wanted to please him, I just couldn't do it. He looked incredibly sad but didn't scold me. Pulling his binoculars from their case, he climbed to the top of the rock and began to scan the horizon. Suddenly, he shouted:

"Look! Madam Mina, look! Look!" I jumped up and stood beside him on the rock; he handed me the binoculars and pointed. A fierce wind had kicked up, swirling the snow violently as it fell heavier than before. However, during brief pauses in the snow squalls, I could see quite far. From our elevated position, the visibility was vast. Far away, past the endless white wasteland, I could see the river winding and twisting like a black ribbon. Directly ahead of us, and surprisingly close—so close I was shocked we hadn't noticed them sooner—was a group of men on horseback rushing toward us. In the middle of their group was a long, wooden wagon that fishtailed wildly, swinging like a dog's tail with every bump in the rough road. Silhouetted against the snow, I could tell by their clothes that the men were peasants or some kind of gypsies.

On the wagon sat a massive, square chest. My heart skipped a beat when I saw it; I knew the end was near. Evening was quickly approaching, and I knew all too well that at sunset, the Thing (the Vampire Count) locked inside that box would gain its freedom. It could change into countless forms and escape its pursuers. Terrified, I turned to the Professor, but to my shock, he was gone. A second later, I saw him down below. He had drawn a protective circle around the rock, just like the one that kept us safe the night before. Once he finished, he climbed back up beside me and said:

"At the very least, you will be safe from him here!" He took the binoculars back and, during the next break in the snow, scanned the plain below. "Look," he said. "They are moving fast. They are whipping the horses and galloping as hard as they possibly can." He paused and added in a hollow voice:

"They are racing against the sunset. We might be too late. God's will be done!" Another blinding sheet of snow swept over us, completely erasing the landscape. When it finally passed, his binoculars were fixed on the plain again. Then he let out a sudden shout:

"Look! Look! Look! See there? Two horsemen are chasing them fast, coming up from the south. It has to be Quincey and John. Take the glasses. Look before the snow hides them again!" I took the binoculars and looked. The two men certainly looked like Dr. Seward and Mr. Morris. I knew for a fact that neither of them was Jonathan. But I also felt that Jonathan was close. Scanning the area, I spotted two more men riding at breakneck speed on the northern side of the gypsy group. I immediately recognized one as Jonathan, and the other had to be Lord Godalming. They were also fiercely pursuing the wagon. When I told the Professor, he cheered with the pure joy of a schoolboy. After watching intently until the snow blocked our view again, he rested his Winchester rifle against the boulder at the entrance of our shelter, ready to fire. "They are all converging," he said. "When the moment comes, the gypsies will be surrounded on all sides." I took out my revolver and kept it ready, because as we spoke, the howling of the wolves grew much louder and closer. When the snowstorm paused for a moment, we looked out again. It was a bizarre sight: heavy snowflakes were dumping down on us, while in the distance, the sun shone brighter and brighter as it sank toward the distant mountain peaks. Sweeping the binoculars around, I spotted dark dots moving here and there—one, two, three, and then larger packs. The wolves were gathering for the kill.

Every passing second felt like an eternity as we waited. The wind now howled in violent bursts, driving the snow furiously in swirling tornadoes. Sometimes we couldn't even see an arm's length in front of us. But when the hollow-sounding wind swept past, it momentarily cleared the air, allowing us to see far into the distance. We had become so used to tracking sunrises and sunsets recently that we knew exactly when the sun would go down—and we knew it would happen very soon. It was hard to believe that, according to our watches, we had been waiting in that rocky cave for less than an hour before all the different groups began to close in on us. The north wind grew even more vicious and biting. It seemed to have blown the main snow clouds away, so it only snowed in occasional bursts now. We could clearly make out the faces of everyone—both the pursuers and the pursued. Strangely, the gypsies didn't seem to realize they were being hunted, or perhaps they just didn't care. However, they did seem to push their horses even faster as the sun dropped lower and lower toward the mountaintops.

They drew closer and closer. The Professor and I crouched behind our rock, weapons drawn; I could tell he was absolutely determined not to let them pass. None of them had any idea we were hiding there.

Suddenly, two voices roared, "Halt!" One was my Jonathan's, screaming in a high pitch of absolute fury; the other was Mr. Morris's, ringing out in a strong, steady tone of command. The gypsies might not have understood English, but there was no mistaking the authority in those voices. They instinctively pulled their horses to a stop. At that exact second, Lord Godalming and Jonathan charged in from one side, while Dr. Seward and Mr. Morris cut them off from the other. The gypsy leader, a magnificent-looking man who rode his horse as if he were part of it, waved the Englishmen away and barked a fierce order to his men to keep moving. They whipped their horses to surge forward, but our four men raised their Winchester rifles, delivering an unmistakable threat to stop. At the same moment, Dr. Van Helsing and I stood up from behind the rock and aimed our weapons at them. Realizing they were completely surrounded, the gypsies tightened their reins and stopped. The leader turned to his men and shouted a command. Instantly, every gypsy drew a weapon—a knife or a pistol—and prepared to attack. The battle began in a flash.

With a quick snap of the reins, the gypsy leader urged his horse forward. Pointing first to the sun—which was now hovering right above the mountain peaks—and then to the castle, he yelled something I couldn't understand. In response, all four of our men leaped off their horses and charged straight for the wagon. I should have been paralyzed with fear seeing Jonathan in such deadly danger, but the adrenaline of battle must have taken over me as well. I felt no fear, only a wild, desperate urge to do something, anything. Seeing our men attack, the gypsy leader shouted another order. His men instantly swarmed the wagon in a chaotic defense, shoving and pushing each other in their rush to obey.

In the middle of the chaos, I saw Jonathan fighting his way through the human wall on one side, while Quincey did the same on the other. It was obvious they were determined to finish the job before the sun went down. Nothing could stop or even slow them down. They completely ignored the guns aimed at their faces, the flashing gypsy knives, and the terrifying howling of the wolves closing in from behind. Jonathan's sheer ferocity and singular focus seemed to terrify the men in front of him; they instinctively cowered and stepped aside, letting him through. In a flash, he leaped onto the wagon and, with unbelievable strength, lifted the massive box and hurled it over the wheel, crashing it to the ground. Meanwhile, Mr. Morris had to fight his way through the gypsies on his side. While I was breathlessly watching Jonathan, I caught Quincey pushing desperately forward out of the corner of my eye. I saw gypsy knives flash as he hacked his way through, and they slashed at him. He blocked their strikes with his massive Bowie knife, and at first, I thought he had made it through unharmed. But as he jumped up next to Jonathan, who had leaped down from the wagon, I saw Quincey clutching his side with his left hand, blood spurting heavily between his fingers. Despite his severe wound, he didn't hesitate. As Jonathan frantically attacked one end of the chest, trying to pry the lid off with his huge Kukri knife, Quincey desperately hacked at the other end with his Bowie. Under the combined force of the two men, the lid started to give way. The nails ripped out with a sharp screech, and the top of the box flew off.

By this time, the gypsies realized they were outgunned by the Winchesters and at the mercy of Lord Godalming and Dr. Seward, so they surrendered and stopped fighting. The sun had almost completely disappeared behind the mountains, casting long shadows of the entire group across the snow. I saw the Count lying on the dirt inside the box, covered in soil that had spilled over him when the chest was violently thrown from the wagon. He looked deathly pale, like a wax figure, but his red eyes burned with that horrifying, vengeful glare I knew all too well.

As I watched, his eyes darted to the setting sun, and the hatred in them shifted into a look of triumph.

But at that exact second, Jonathan's massive knife swung down in a silver blur. I screamed as the blade sliced cleanly through the Count's throat, while at the very same moment, Mr. Morris's Bowie knife plunged deep into his heart.

It was a miracle. Right before our eyes, in the time it takes to draw a single breath, the Count's entire body crumbled into dust and vanished completely.

I will be grateful for the rest of my life that, in his final moment of destruction, a look of profound peace washed over the Count's face—a peace I never would have believed possible for such a monster.

Dracula's Castle now loomed against the blood-red sky, every stone of its ruined towers sharply outlined by the dying light of the setting sun.

The gypsies, likely assuming we used dark magic to make the dead man vanish, turned around without a word and galloped away as if the devil himself were chasing them. The men without horses leaped onto the wagon, screaming at the riders not to leave them behind. The wolves, which had stayed at a safe distance, chased after them, leaving us completely alone.

Mr. Morris collapsed to the ground, propping himself up on one elbow while pressing his hand hard against his side. The blood was still pouring through his fingers. The Holy circle no longer held me back, so I ran to him, followed quickly by the two doctors. Jonathan knelt behind him, and the dying man leaned his head back onto Jonathan's shoulder. With a heavy sigh and a weak effort, he took my hand in his clean one. He must have seen the utter heartbreak on my face, because he smiled and said:

"I am just so happy to have been of some help! Oh, God!" he suddenly cried out, fighting to sit up and pointing directly at me. "Dying for this was worth it! Look! Look!"

The sun had just dipped below the mountaintop, and the last red rays bathed my face in a warm, rosy light. Acting as one, all the men dropped to their knees, and a deep, heartfelt "Amen" echoed from their lips as they followed the direction of his pointing finger. The dying man spoke his final words:

"Thank God it wasn't all for nothing! Look! The snow itself isn't as pure as her forehead! The curse is finally gone!"

And there, to our immense sorrow, the brave and gallant gentleman died with a peaceful smile and in total silence.

NOTE (7 Years Later)

Seven years ago, we all walked through the fire. The happiness some of us have found since then makes all the pain we endured entirely worth it. It brings Mina and me extra joy that our son's birthday falls on the exact same day Quincey Morris died. I know his mother secretly believes that a piece of our brave friend's spirit lives on in our boy. His long string of middle names honors our entire little group of men, but we simply call him Quincey.

This past summer, we traveled back to Transylvania and retraced our steps across the land that holds such vivid and terrifying memories for us. It was almost impossible to believe that the things we saw and heard with our own senses were actually real. Every trace of what had happened was completely erased. The castle still stands as it always did, towering over a desolate wasteland.

When we returned home, we talked about the old days. We can finally look back on them without feeling despair, as both Godalming and Seward are happily married now. I pulled our old documents from the safe where they had sat untouched since we got back all those years ago. We were struck by the fact that, out of the massive pile of papers making up our record, there is barely a single piece of official proof. It's just a mountain of typewritten pages, aside from the later handwritten diaries of Mina, Seward, myself, and Van Helsing's notes. Even if we wanted to, we couldn't realistically expect anyone to accept these papers as proof of such an insane story. Van Helsing summed it up perfectly as he bounced our boy on his knee:

"We don't want proof; we don't ask anyone to believe us! One day, this boy will learn what a brave and incredible woman his mother is. He already knows how sweet and caring she is, but later, he'll understand that there were men who loved her so deeply they were willing to risk everything for her."

JONATHAN HARKER.
