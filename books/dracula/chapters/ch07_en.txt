[P004] From a Correspondent.

[P005_006] Whitby.

[P006b] One of the greatest and most sudden storms on record has just hit Whitby, a port town on the Yorkshire coast, bringing with it some very strange and unusual events. The weather had been somewhat sultry, though not unusually so for August. Saturday evening was as clear as usual, and many vacationers were planning trips the next day to nearby spots like Mulgrave Woods, Robin Hood's Bay, Rig Mill, Runswick, and Staithes. The steamboats Emma and Scarborough bustled along the coast, and short excursions for tourists visiting Whitby were exceptionally busy. As the clear weather continued into the afternoon, people gathered in the East Cliff churchyard to enjoy the open views of the north and east sea, pointing to the sudden appearance of 'mare's tail' clouds high in the northwest sky. These cirrus clouds were a sign of coming wind and rain. At the time, a gentle breeze was blowing from the southwest, described in meteorological terms as a "force two light breeze." The coastguard on duty immediately reported the situation, and an experienced fisherman who had read the weather on the East Cliff for over half a century firmly predicted an approaching squall. Near sunset, a magnificent display of colorfully tinted clouds began to gather. A fairly large crowd flocked to the cliff walk of the old churchyard to enjoy the breathtaking view. Before the sun completely disappeared behind the towering black cliffs of Kettleness in the western sky, the sunset painted the clouds with brilliant golds, flames, purples, pinks, greens, and violets. Amidst these colors, small but pitch-black masses of clouds drifted in various shapes, drawing clear silhouettes. This intense scenery naturally caught the attention of painters, and surely a few sketches titled 'Prelude to a Great Storm' will decorate the exhibition walls of the Royal Academy and the Royal Institute of Painters in Water Colours next May. The sailors sensed the danger and acted immediately. Captains decisively chose to keep their small fishing boats, such as the flat-bottomed 'cobles' and 'mules,' securely tied up in the harbor until the storm passed.

[P006c] The wind died down as evening fell, but around midnight, a suffocating heat and an oppressive silence settled in. It was the overwhelming heaviness that sensitive people often feel right before a thunderstorm. Not a single light was visible on the sea. Even the coastal passenger ships that normally sailed close to the shore had sought refuge far out at sea, and the fishing boats at work had also vanished. The only thing in sight was a strange foreign schooner, with all its sails fully set, heading westward. While the ship remained in view, people chattered about whether its officers were reckless or simply ignorant. Some tried to send warning signals, urging them to reduce their sails by any means. Before the darkness completely descended, the ship could be seen dimly, floating precariously on the rolling swells with its sails flapping helplessly.

[P007] "As idle as a painted ship upon a painted ocean."

[P008] Shortly before ten o'clock at night, the stillness of the air grew quite oppressive. The silence was so dense that the bleating of sheep inland or the barking of dogs in the village could be heard clearly. The cheerful French music drifting from the pier seemed to clash discordantly with the heavy silence created by nature. A little past midnight, a strange sound echoed from across the sea, and a faint yet hollow, eerie roar began to resound in the vast empty sky overhead.

[P009] Then, without any warning, the storm struck like lightning. Nature seemed to throw a sudden fit, transforming in an instant. The waves grew fiercer, surging up as if to swallow each other, and the sea, which had been as calm as glass just minutes before, turned into a roaring monster devouring everything in its path. The white, crashing waves struck the flat sandy beach madly, soon sweeping up over the gentle cliffs. Some waves completely jumped the breakwater, covering the glass lanterns of the lighthouses standing at both ends of Whitby harbor with sea foam. The howling wind roared like thunder, blowing so hard that even strong men struggled to stay on their feet and had to cling to iron posts to survive. If the onlookers had not been quickly evacuated from the pier, the death toll that night would easily have multiplied. What made the chaos even more terrifying was the thick sea fog rolling inland. The damp, cold white clouds swept past like ghosts, leaving an eerie sensation as if the souls of those lost at sea were touching the living with their hands of death. Every time the fog swirled by, people shuddered in horror. Occasionally, when the fog lifted and lightning struck, visibility stretched quite far out to sea. Now the lightning flashed incessantly, followed by ear-splitting thunder that shook the heavens. The entire sky above seemed to churn, crushed under the trampling feet of the storm.

[P010] Some of the scenes revealed by the lightning were truly awe-inspiring. Mountainous waves spat massive foam into the sky, and the storm snatched it up, scattering it wildly into the air. Fishing boats with their sails torn to shreds raced frantically for shelter from the squall, and the white wings of seabirds swept up in the storm occasionally flashed through the pitch-black darkness. A new searchlight had been installed at the top of the East Cliff, but it had not yet been tested. The officers in charge hurried to turn it on, scanning the sea whenever the thick fog rolled in. It proved quite effective a time or two. One fishing boat, rushing precariously toward the harbor with its sides almost submerged, managed to avoid a disastrous crash into the breakwater thanks to the searchlight's beam. Whenever the boats safely reached the harbor, the people on the shore erupted in cheers. The shouting seemed to pierce the storm for a moment, only to be swept away and vanish into the fierce wind.

[P011a] Before long, the searchlight caught a schooner far out at sea with all its sails fully set. It was the very same ship that had been the talk of the town earlier in the evening. By then, the wind had shifted to the east, and the people on the cliff shuddered as they realized the ship was in terrible danger. Between the ship and the harbor lay a massive, flat reef that had smashed countless fine vessels to pieces. With the current wind direction, it seemed impossible for the ship to even reach the harbor entrance. It was near high tide, but the waves were churning so violently that the bottom of the shore was clearly exposed through the deep troughs between the waves. Yet, the schooner with all its sails set was rushing forward at a mad speed, destined to end up—as an old sailor put it—"smashed somewhere, whether in hell or anywhere else." Just then, a sea fog much larger than before rolled in. The damp mass of fog swallowed everything like a gray shroud, blinding the people. Only the roar of the storm, the thunder, and the howling of the massive waves pierced the damp darkness and struck their ears. The searchlight's beam was fixed on the harbor entrance just beyond the east breakwater, where the collision was expected, and people held their breath. Suddenly, the wind shifted to the northeast, blowing the sea fog away. And miraculously, that strange schooner, leaping over the waves, broke through the breakwaters! It had safely entered the harbor with all its sails fully unfurled against the fierce squall.

[P011b] The searchlight immediately tracked the ship, and a chill ran down the spine of everyone who witnessed its deck. A corpse was tied to the ship's steering wheel. The body, with its head slumped down, swung back and forth hideously every time the ship pitched. There was not a single sign of life anywhere else on the deck. Everyone was left in absolute shock, realizing the ship had miraculously found the harbor guided only by the hands of a dead man. All this happened in an instant. Without stopping, the schooner charged across the harbor and crashed violently into the pile of sand and gravel at 'Tate Hill Pier,' the southeast corner of the breakwater below the East Cliff.

[P012] Because it charged into the pile of sand, the collision was tremendous. The masts, ropes, and stays were pulled taut as if they would snap, and some of the rigging at the top of the masts collapsed with a loud crash. But the strangest scene occurred the very moment the ship touched the shore. As if bouncing off from the shock of the collision, an enormous dog leaped up from below deck, bounded off the bow, and jumped onto the sandy beach. Running straight toward the steep cliff, the dog instantly vanished into the thick darkness.

[P013] Since the residents near Tate Hill Pier had all gone to bed or evacuated to higher ground, the breakwater was completely empty. The first person to board the ship was the east coastguard on duty, who immediately ran down. The searchlight stopped scanning the harbor entrance and fixed its beam on the abandoned derelict. The coastguard, who ran to the stern and approached the steering wheel, leaned over to check something, then jumped back as if he had been electrocuted. Curious onlookers began to run over one by one. Being a rather fast runner, I, the correspondent, pushed through the crowd and arrived before anyone else. However, people had already gathered on the breakwater, and the coastguard and police were controlling access. Fortunately, thanks to the consideration of the chief boatman, I was allowed onto the deck and became one of the few people to witness the dead sailor tied to the wheel firsthand.

[P014] It was no wonder the coastguard had been so startled. It was a gruesome sight, the kind one rarely sees in a lifetime. The man was tied securely to the spokes of the steering wheel with his hands crossed. A crucifix was wedged between his inner hand and the wood, and the beads of a rosary attached to the cross tightly wrapped around his wrists and the wheel. All of this was bound once more with ropes. This poor sailor might have been sitting at first. However, every time the sails flapped and the rudder pitched, the ropes violently dragged his body back and forth like a beast, cutting so deeply into his flesh that his bones were clearly exposed. The scene was carefully documented, and Dr. Caffyn, who arrived soon after, examined the body and determined that the man had been dead for at least two days. In the man's pocket was a small glass bottle, tightly stoppered with cork, and inside was a small roll of paper that appeared to be the ship's log. The coastguard assumed the man had tied his own hands and tightened the knots with his teeth. The fact that the coastguard was the first to board might spare the Admiralty Court some headache, as it eliminates any claim for salvage rewards. However, the legal experts are already buzzing with debate. One young law student was loudly arguing that since a dead man holding the tiller violates the laws of dead hands, the ownership is forfeited. Regardless, the dead helmsman was handled as an honorable hero who stayed at his post until the very end, and his body was taken to the morgue.

[P015] The storm is already dying down. People are scattering to their homes, and a red morning sun rises over the Yorkshire moors. I will continue to report detailed news about this ghost ship, which miraculously found the harbor, in our next issue.

[P016_017] Whitby

[P017b] 9 August. — The follow-up news surrounding the ship that drifted alone in the storm last night is just as bizarre as its arrival. The schooner has been identified as the 'Demeter,' a Russian vessel that set sail from Varna, Bulgaria. The ship carried no significant cargo other than several large wooden boxes filled with earth, and silver sand loaded to serve as ballast. This cargo was consigned to Mr. Billington, a solicitor in Whitby, who officially took delivery of it this morning. The Russian Consul also took charge of the vessel on behalf of the charterer and paid the harbor dues. The whole town is talking about nothing but this mysterious ship. Officials from the Board of Trade are making a huge fuss, thoroughly investigating whether any regulations were broken. They likely want to ensure no one can find fault later over what might just be a flash-in-the-pan topic for a few days. Much attention was also given to the massive dog that jumped out when the ship hit the shore. Members of the Whitby Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals stepped forward to rescue the beast, but the dog has completely vanished from the town, hiding who knows where. It probably fled into the moors out of fear, but quite a few people are worried that the fierce beast might cause terrible trouble later. Early this morning, a large mastiff mix belonging to a coal merchant near Tate Hill Pier was found brutally killed. It had clearly fought a highly ferocious predator. The dog's neck was savagely torn off, and its belly was sliced open by sharp claws.

[P017c] *       *       *       *       *

[P018_019] Later. — Thanks to the consideration of the Board of Trade inspector, I was able to catch a glimpse of the Demeter's log. Up until the entry from three days ago, there was nothing particularly remarkable other than the missing crewmen. But the 'paper in the glass bottle,' which was admitted as evidence at the coroner's inquest, was the climax. The story created by combining these two records is the most chilling I have ever heard in my life. Since there is no reason to hide it, I am sending a copy here, with only the navigational details removed. The captain seemed to be gripped by some delusion even before the voyage truly began, and the symptoms apparently worsened throughout the journey. Please take this with a grain of salt, as it is a transcribed translation hastily done by a clerk at the Russian Consulate.

[P020a] Log of the Demeter.

[P020b] Varna to Whitby.

[P021_022] Written 18 July. Unbelievably strange things are happening. I intend to carefully record everything that happens from now until we reach land.

[P022b] *       *       *       *       *

[P023_024] 6 July. Finished loading our cargo of silver sand and boxes of earth, and set sail at noon with a fresh east wind. The crew consists of nine men in total, including myself, the first mate, the second mate, the cook, and five deckhands.

[P024b] *       *       *       *       *

[P025_026] 11 July. Entered the Bosphorus at dawn. Slipped the Turkish customs officers a little bribe (baksheesh) and passed without any trouble. Resumed our voyage at 4 p.m.

[P026b] *       *       *       *       *

[P027_028] 12 July. Passed through the Dardanelles. More customs officers and patrol boats approached, so we used bribes again. Their inspection was thorough, but after taking the money, they seemed eager for us to leave quickly. Entered the Aegean Sea as it grew dark.

[P028b] *       *       *       *       *

[P029_030] 13 July. Passed Cape Matapan. The crew is gripped by fear and harboring complaints. However, they don't say anything openly.

[P030b] *       *       *       *       *

[P031_032] 14 July. The state of the crew is very anxious. They are all reliable lads who have sailed with me for a long time. Even the mate couldn't find a clear reason. They simply cross themselves, saying, "There is something on the ship." Today, the mate lost his temper and threw a punch at one of them. I thought a big fight would break out, but everyone remained dead silent.

[P032b] *       *       *       *       *

[P033_034] 16 July. In the morning, the mate reported that the sailor Petrofsky had vanished without a trace. His whereabouts are unknown. He took the port watch at midnight last night and even completed his shift, but he never returned to his bunk. The crew is more dispirited than ever. They all look as if they expected this to happen, but they keep their mouths shut, only repeating, "There is something on the ship." The mate is increasingly losing patience with them, and I am terribly worried that some disaster might occur.

[P034b] *       *       *       *       *

[P035_036] 17 July. Yesterday, the sailor Olgaren came to my cabin, pale with terror, and poured his heart out. He claimed he was certain a strange man was on board. According to him, while hiding behind the deckhouse to escape the rain during his watch, a tall, gaunt man who resembled none of the crew came up the companionway, went toward the forward deck, and vanished. He cautiously followed him to the bow, but no one was there, and all the hatchway doors were closed. Olgaren was half out of his mind, gripped by superstitious terror. I am afraid this fear will spread to the other sailors. To calm their anxiety, I plan to search the entire ship thoroughly today.

[P036b] *       *       *       *       *

[P037_038] Later today, I gathered the crew and declared that since they seemed to believe someone was hiding on the ship, we would turn the vessel upside down from bow to stern. The first mate got angry, calling it madness. He said playing along with such silly delusions would only ruin the crew's discipline. He picked up an iron bar, threatening to keep watch and ensure the men tried no nonsense. I left the steering wheel to him, and alongside the rest of the crew, holding lanterns, we began to comb through every corner like hunting for lice. We searched thoroughly without missing a spot, but there was nothing except the large wooden boxes, and nowhere for a person to hide. Once the search ended, the sailors looked relieved and went back to work with lighter hearts. The first mate frowned in displeasure but kept his mouth shut.

[P038b] *       *       *       *       *

[P039_040] 22 July. — Over the past three days, everyone has been too busy handling the sails due to the rough weather to even have time to tremble in fear. The crew seems to have completely forgotten that terrible terror. The mate has become cheerful again, and the atmosphere has grown quite friendly. I praised the sailors for suffering silently through the harsh weather. We passed Gibraltar and exited the strait. Everyone is safe.

[P040b] *       *       *       *       *

[P041_042] 24 July. — This ship is surely cursed with doom. As if being short-handed wasn't enough, we entered the Bay of Biscay where the weather was brutal. To make matters worse, another sailor vanished without a trace last night. Just like the first missing man, he completely disappeared without a trace after coming down from his watch. The crew is entirely gripped by extreme terror and in a complete panic. They even submitted a petition asking to stand watch in pairs, saying they are too terrified to be alone. The mate was furious. I am afraid that the mate or one of the sailors might swing a fist and cause a terrible tragedy at this rate.

[P042b] *       *       *       *       *

[P043_044] 28 July. — Tossed about by the violently swirling sea, we endured four days in a hellish storm. Not a single person could catch a wink of sleep, and the crew is completely exhausted. There is no one ready to stand watch immediately, making it a loss on how to organize the shifts. The second mate volunteered to handle the helm and the watch, kindly allowing the crew to get even just a few hours of sleep. The wind has died down somewhat, but the waves remain terrifying, as if ready to swallow us whole. Fortunately, the ship has regained some stability and the rocking has lessened.

[P044b] *       *       *       *       *

[P045_046] 29 July. — Another tragedy has struck. Because the crew was too exhausted to form pairs, we had no choice but to set a single watch last night. When I went up to the deck for the morning shift change, not a single soul was in sight except the helmsman. When I yelled at the top of my lungs, they all rushed out onto the deck. We searched thoroughly, but the missing man never appeared. Now we have lost even the second mate, and the crew has lost their minds. The mate and I have agreed to stay armed from now on and wait for the culprit behind these horrific events to reveal even a hint of themselves.

[P046b] *       *       *       *       *

[P047_048] 30 July. — Last night, I was finally overjoyed that England was close. The weather was sunny, and the sails were fully unfurled. I went to bed exhausted and fell fast asleep, but the mate shook me awake. He said the watchman and the helmsman had both vanished at the same time. Now, the only people left to manage this large ship are me, the mate, and two deckhands.

[P048b] *       *       *       *       *

[P049_050] 1 August. — We have been trapped in a thick fog for two whole days, unable to catch a glimpse of even the shadow of another ship. I had hoped we could send a distress signal or crash into any port once we entered the English Channel. But with no hands left to manage the sails, we are helplessly drifting wherever the wind takes us. Since there is no way to raise the sails again, we don't even dare lower them. We feel like we are drifting helplessly toward massive doom. The mate has now lost his reason even more than the crew, completely breaking down. It seems his tough nature has instead eaten away at himself. On the other hand, the sailors have transcended fear and are silently holding on and working, as if prepared for the worst end. They are Russians, and he is Romanian.

[P050b] *       *       *       *       *

[P051_052] 2 August, midnight. — I had drifted off to sleep, only to be startled awake by a tearing scream right outside my cabin window. The fog was so thick I couldn't see an inch ahead. I dashed out onto the deck and ran straight into the mate. He too had run over after hearing the scream, but there was no trace of the watchman anywhere. Another man evaporated. Lord, look down upon us! The mate said that in the brief moment when he heard the scream, he caught a glimpse of the North Foreland, meaning we must have already passed the Strait of Dover. If so, we are now out in the middle of the North Sea. Only God can guide us through this fog that seems to cling together and move with us. But it seems even God has turned His back on us.

[P052b] *       *       *       *       *

[P053_054] 3 August. — At midnight, I went to relieve the helmsman, but there was no one there. The wind was a steady tailwind, so the ship hadn't strayed off course. I couldn't let go of the wheel, so I called out for the mate at the top of my lungs. A few seconds later, he rushed up onto the deck in his underwear. His eyes gleamed with madness, and his appearance was terribly haggard. A chill ran down my spine, fearing his sanity had finally snapped. He came close to me and, as if afraid even the air might eavesdrop, whispered hoarsely into my ear. "It's here. I finally know. I saw it clearly when I was on watch last night. It was a tall, gaunt man, terrifyingly pale. He was standing tall at the bow, glaring out. I snuck up on him and stabbed him hard with my dagger. But the blade just passed right through him, as if cutting through empty air." Finishing his words, he drew his knife and frantically stabbed at the empty air, throwing a fit. Then he panted and muttered again. "But he is definitely here. I swear I'll find him. He must be hiding in the cargo hold. In one of those large boxes! I'll unscrew the boxes one by one and expose them all, so you just hold tight to that steering wheel for dear life." He placed a finger on his lips in a 'shh' gesture, gave me a murderous look of warning, and went below.

[P054b] The waves began to grow rough, so I couldn't take my hands off the wheel. Soon after, I saw him come out onto the deck with a toolbox and a lantern, crawling down toward the forward hatchway. He is mad. Completely raving mad. It won't do any good even if I try to stop him. Fortunately, the lad won't be able to do much harm to those large boxes. The manifest clearly says 'clay,' so digging through a pile of dirt is probably the tamest thing he can do right now. So I remain here, defending the wheel and writing this log. I simply trust in God and wait for the fog to lift. If we still can't enter a port riding the current wind, I'll have to slash all the sails, stop the ship, and send out a distress signal....

[P054c] *       *       *       *       *

[P055_056] It is all over now. I heard frantic banging coming from the cargo hold, and I hoped he would at least vent his anger doing that and come up calmer. Then suddenly, a terrifying scream that made my blood freeze erupted from the hatchway. Immediately after, the mate shot up onto the deck like a cannonball. He was the very picture of a raving lunatic throwing a fit, his eyes rolled back in terror and his face distorted. "Save me! Save me!" he howled like a beast, looking wildly around in the thick blanket-like fog. Soon the terror turned to deep despair, and he muttered in a sickeningly calm voice. "Captain, you'd better follow me before it's too late. He is right there. I know the secret now. The sea will save me from that devil bastard. It's the only way left!" Before I could even open my mouth or step forward to grab him, he leaped over the railing and threw himself into the sea without hesitation. Perhaps I know the secret now too. It was that mad mate who devoured my sailors one by one, and now he himself has followed them to the bottom of the sea. God, save me! If I reach a port, how on earth am I supposed to explain this horrific mess? If I safely reach a port, that is! Will such a day ever come?

[P056b] *       *       *       *       *

[P057_058] 4 August. — The fog remains so thick that not even the sunrise can break through it. The fact that the sun has risen is something I only know through my sixth sense as a lifelong sailor. I don't dare go below, and I couldn't let go of the wheel, so I stood glued to this spot all night long. And in the pitch-black darkness, I saw it—that devil bastard!—clearly. May God forgive me. The mate made a wise choice throwing himself into the sea. It was a hundred times better to die like a man. No one would fault a sailor for meeting his end in the blue sea like a true seaman. But I am the captain, and I cannot abandon my ship. Whether this thing is a devil or a monster, I will fight it to the end. When my strength begins to fail, I will tie my hands securely to the steering wheel and bind myself with sacred objects that he—that terrible monster!—dares not touch. Then, whether a tailwind or a headwind blows, I will at least be able to protect my soul and my honor as a captain. My strength is gradually failing, and the night is approaching again. If he appears before my eyes again, I might be doomed before I can even react.... If we are shipwrecked, someone might find this glass bottle and understand my story. Even if they don't... at least the fact that I fulfilled my duty to the very end will be known to the whole world. God, the Virgin Mary, and all the saints, look down upon this poor, ignorant soul who tries to do his duty to the very end....

[P059_060] Of course, the verdict was left inconclusive. There was no evidence to present, and there was not a single mouth left to say whether the man had taken his own life or was murdered. The townspeople almost unanimously revere the captain as a hero, and a grand public funeral is planned in his honor. It has already been confirmed that his body will be escorted by a procession of boats up the River Esk, then return to Tate Hill Pier and climb the '199 steps'. This is because he is to be buried in the old churchyard on the cliff. Already, over a hundred shipowners have stepped forward offering to launch their boats to send off the hero on his final journey.

[P061] No trace of that massive beast ever appeared. Many people are grieving, for given the current mood, the whole town would have stepped up to take in and raise the dog. When the funeral ends tomorrow, yet another bizarre mystery embraced by the sea will be closed forever.

[P062] Mina Murray's Journal.

[P063] 8 August. — Lucy tossed and turned so badly all night that I stayed up all night with my eyes wide open. A terrible storm raged outside, and every time a roaring sound echoed down the chimney, I shuddered. When fierce gusts of wind struck the windows, it sounded like cannons firing in the distance. Strangely, Lucy didn't wake up, but she sprang out of bed twice in her sleep and got dressed. Fortunately, I woke up in time on both occasions and was able to gently undress her and lay her back in bed without waking her. The more I see it, the stranger this sleepwalking is. Just by holding her body and physically stopping her, the initial action she tried to take (if there ever was an intention to begin with) simply fades away, and she docilely returns to her original state as if nothing happened.

[P064a] Early in the morning, we got out of bed and went down to the harbor to see what the storm had done the night before. The streets were quiet, the sun was bright, and the air was crisp. However, fierce and massive waves covered in pure white foam were violently forcing their way into the narrow harbor entrance. They looked like thugs roughly pushing through a gentle crowd. For some reason, I was relieved to know that Jonathan was on land last night, not out on that terrifying sea. But, oh, how could I even know whether he is on land or at sea? Where is he now, and how is he doing? My heart is terribly heavy with worry for him. If only I knew what to do, if only there was something I could do!

[P064b] *       *       *       *       *

[P065_066] 10 August. — The funeral for the poor captain today was truly moving enough to bring tears to my eyes. It felt as if every boat in the harbor had turned out, and captains carried the coffin on their shoulders all the way from Tate Hill Pier to the churchyard. Lucy went along too, and we took our seats early on the bench we always sit on. Meanwhile, the funeral procession of boats went up the river to the viaduct and came back down. Our spot had a completely open view, so we could take in the sight of the funeral procession winding past at a glance. Since the poor sailor was to be buried right near our bench, we stood up on the bench to watch when it was time for the burial. Poor Lucy looked extremely distressed. She was restless and anxious the entire time, and those dreams she suffers from every night must be completely draining her. What is truly strange is that Lucy never tells me why she is anxious. Or rather, she might not even know the reason herself. On top of that, the incident this morning where old Mr. Swales was found dead on our bench with a broken neck must have also unsettled her. The doctor said he must have been so terribly startled by something that he fell backward over the bench. There was such terrible terror and shock on the old man's face that even strong men shuddered at the sight of it. Such a kind and friendly old man! Could his dying eyes have witnessed a terrible death itself!

[P066b] Lucy is so soft-hearted and sensitive that she reacts far more sharply to the atmosphere around her than others do. Just a little while ago, she was severely shocked by a very trivial thing that I brushed off without much thought (even though I am quite fond of animals too). One of the men who often comes up here to look out for his boat brought his dog along. The dog always follows its owner around closely. Both of them are so gentle that I had never seen the owner get angry or the dog bark. Yet, during the funeral, the dog wouldn't come near its owner sitting on the bench and started barking frantically from a few yards away. The owner tried to coax it gently at first, then scolded it harshly, and finally grew furiously angry, but the creature wouldn't approach and wouldn't stop barking loudly. The beast was furiously enraged, its eyes glowing with murderous intent, and the hair all over its body was standing straight up like a cat's tail when it fights. Finally, losing his temper, the man jumped down, kicked the dog in a fit of rage, grabbed it by the scruff of its neck, dragged it over, and slammed it down onto the tombstone where the bench was fixed. The moment its body touched the cold stone, the poor animal grew quiet as if it were a lie and began to tremble violently like an aspen leaf. It just cowered and shrank back, shivering without even thinking of running away. It was so pitifully terrified that I approached and tried to soothe it, knowing it would be useless. Lucy, too, didn't know what to do out of pity, but she couldn't bring herself to touch the dog and just watched with a pained expression.

[P066c] I am terribly worried about how Lucy, being so overly sensitive, will navigate this harsh world in the future. She will definitely have a nightmare about this tonight as well. A ship charging into the harbor guided by a dead man's hands, a corpse tied to the steering wheel with a crucifix and rosary, a funeral that touched the heart, a dog that went frantic and then became paralyzed with terror... All these shocking events will tangle together and horribly decorate her dreams.

[P067] Since it might be better for her to go to bed completely physically exhausted, we plan to take a long walk along the cliff all the way to Robin Hood's Bay. If we tire her body out completely, there won't be any room for her sleepwalking to act up.
