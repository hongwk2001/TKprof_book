1: CHAPTER 26
2: DR. SEWARD'S DIARY
3: October 29.—I am writing this on the train from Varna to Galatz. Last night, we all gathered a little before sunset. Everyone had completed their tasks to the best of their abilities. As far as our planning, effort, and opportunities allowed, we were fully prepared for the rest of our journey and the work waiting for us in Galatz. When the usual time approached, Mrs. Harker got ready for the hypnosis. Van Helsing had to put in a much longer and more intense effort than usual before she finally slipped into the trance. Usually, she speaks with just a prompt, but this time the Professor had to ask her several firm questions before we learned anything. Finally, she answered:
4: "I can't see anything. We are entirely still. There are no lapping waves, only a steady swirling sound of water softly brushing against the mooring cable. I hear men's voices calling out, both near and far, along with the roll and creak of oars in their locks. A gun goes off somewhere, its echo sounding distant. Footsteps are tramping overhead, and ropes and chains are being dragged. What's this? A sliver of light appears, and I can feel a breeze blowing on me."
5: She stopped there. Moving impulsively, she stood up from the sofa and raised both hands, palms facing up, as if lifting something heavy. Van Helsing and I exchanged a knowing look. Quincey arched his eyebrows and watched her closely, while Harker's hand instinctively gripped the hilt of his Kukri knife. A long silence followed. We all knew her window to speak was closing, but we felt it was pointless to say anything. Suddenly, she sat up straight, opened her eyes, and said sweetly:
6: "Would any of you like a cup of tea? You must all be exhausted!" We wanted to keep her happy, so we agreed. She hurried off to make the tea. Once she was gone, Van Helsing spoke up:
7: "You see, my friends, he is close to land. He has left his earth-filled chest, but he hasn't reached the shore yet. He might hide somewhere during the night, but if he isn't carried ashore or if the ship doesn't dock, he cannot step on land. If it's night, he could change his shape and jump or fly ashore, like he did in Whitby. But if morning comes before he reaches land, he's trapped unless someone carries him. And if someone does carry him, customs officers might discover what's inside the box. Ultimately, if he doesn't escape to the shore tonight or before dawn, he loses an entire day. That means we might arrive in time. If he doesn't escape tonight, we will catch him during the day, trapped in his box and completely at our mercy. He wouldn't dare wake up in his true form for fear of being discovered."
8: There was nothing left to discuss, so we waited patiently until dawn, hoping to learn more from Mrs. Harker then.
9: Early this morning, we waited with bated breath for her answers under hypnosis. It took even longer for her to enter the trance than before. By the time she did, the time left until full sunrise was so short that we started to lose hope. Van Helsing seemed to pour his entire soul into the effort. Finally, responding to his willpower, she replied:
10: "Everything is dark. I hear water lapping right at my level, and the creaking sound of wood rubbing against wood." She paused, and then the red sun rose. We have to wait until tonight.
11: And so we are traveling toward Galatz, consumed by an agonizing wait. We were supposed to arrive between 2:00 and 3:00 a.m., but we are already three hours delayed at Bucharest, meaning we won't get there until well after sunrise. That gives us two more chances for hypnotic messages from Mrs. Harker. Either one could potentially shed more light on what is happening.
12: *       *       *       *       *
13: Later.—Sunset has come and gone. Luckily, it happened during a quiet moment on the train; if we had been stopped at a station, we might not have had the peace and isolation we needed. Mrs. Harker resisted the hypnosis even more than she did this morning. I'm terrified that her ability to read the Count's sensations might fade away exactly when we need it most. It feels like her own imagination is starting to bleed into her visions. Until now, she has only reported the most basic facts during her trances. If this shift continues, it could eventually lead us in the wrong direction. I would be happy if I knew the Count's control over her was fading along with her telepathic connection, but I fear that isn't the case. When she finally spoke, her words were a riddle:
14: "Something is leaving. I can feel it brush past me like a cold breeze. Far away, I hear chaotic noises—men talking in strange languages, the rush of fiercely falling water, and wolves howling." She stopped talking, and a shudder ripped through her body. It grew stronger for a few seconds until she was shaking uncontrollably, as if she were having a seizure. She refused to say another word, even when the Professor sharply demanded answers. When she woke up, she was cold, exhausted, and weak, but her mind was perfectly sharp. She couldn't remember anything and asked us what she had said. After we told her, she sat in total silence for a long time, thinking deeply.
15: *       *       *       *       *
\n16: October 30, 7 a.m.—We are close to Galatz now, and I might not have time to write later. We all anxiously awaited sunrise this morning. Knowing how difficult it was becoming to induce the hypnotic trance, Van Helsing started his hand motions earlier than usual. It had no effect, however, until the exact scheduled time. Even then, she resisted fiercely, only giving in a single minute before the sun peeked over the horizon. The Professor immediately began questioning her, and her reply came just as swiftly:
17: "Everything is dark. I hear water rushing past, level with my ears, and the sound of wood creaking against wood. Cows are mooing in the distance. There is another sound, a strange one like—" She stopped, her face turning pale, and then even paler.
18: "Keep going! Continue! Speak, I command you!" Van Helsing cried out in an agonizing tone. His eyes showed despair because the rising sun was already casting a red glow on Mrs. Harker's pale face. She opened her eyes, and we all flinched as she spoke—sweetly and seemingly without a care in the world:
19: "Oh, Professor, why are you asking me to do something you know I can't? I don't remember a thing." Noticing the shock on our faces, her expression shifted to concern. She looked at us, bewildered, and said:
20: "What did I say? What did I do? I don't know anything, except that I was lying here half-asleep and heard you say, 'Keep going! Speak, I command you!' It felt so strange to hear you ordering me around like I was a naughty child!"
21: "Oh, Madam Mina," he replied sadly, "if you needed proof of how much I love and respect you, this is it. A word spoken purely for your own good, spoken more urgently than ever, seems strange to you because I am ordering someone I am normally so proud to obey!"
22: The train whistles are blowing; we are arriving in Galatz. We are burning with anxiety and eagerness.
23: Mina Harker's Journal.
24: October 30.—Mr. Morris brought me to the hotel rooms we booked by telegram. He was the most logical choice to escort me, since he doesn't speak any foreign languages and could easily be spared. We divided our forces much like we did in Varna. The only difference was that Lord Godalming went to the Vice-Consul. Since we were in a massive rush, we hoped his aristocratic title would act as an immediate guarantee for the officials. Jonathan and the two doctors went to the shipping agent to get the details on the Czarina Catherine's arrival.
25: *       *       *       *       *
26: Later.—Lord Godalming is back. The Consul is out of town, and the Vice-Consul is sick, so a clerk was handling the daily routine. The clerk was very accommodating and offered to do whatever he could to help.
27: Jonathan Harker's Journal.
28: October 30.—At 9:00 a.m., Dr. Van Helsing, Dr. Seward, and I visited Mackenzie & Steinkoff, the local agents for the London-based Hapgood firm. They had already received a telegram from London responding to Lord Godalming's request, instructing them to give us any assistance we needed. They were incredibly kind and polite, immediately taking us aboard the Czarina Catherine, which was anchored out in the river harbor. There, we met the captain, a man named Donelson, who told us about his voyage. He swore he had never seen such favorable sailing conditions in his entire life.
29: "Man!" he exclaimed. "It actually scared us. We kept expecting some terrible stroke of bad luck to hit and even out the average. It's downright unnatural to sail from London to the Black Sea with a wind right behind you the whole way, as if the Devil himself were blowing on the sails for his own reasons. And the entire time, we couldn't see a single thing! Whenever we got near another ship, a port, or a headland, a thick fog would drop down and travel with us. By the time it lifted and we looked around, there wasn't a damn thing in sight. We sailed past Gibraltar without even being able to signal. Until we hit the Dardanelles and had to wait for our passage permit, we never even came within shouting distance of anyone. At first, I wanted to drop the sails and wait for the fog to clear. But then I figured that if the Devil was dead set on pushing us into the Black Sea quickly, he was going to do it whether we liked it or not. A fast voyage wouldn't hurt our reputation with the owners or ruin our cargo. I figured the Old Man, having gotten what he wanted, would be grateful enough that we didn't get in his way." This bizarre mix of simplicity, cunning, superstition, and raw business logic caught Van Helsing's attention. He replied:
30: "My friend, that Devil is much smarter than some give him credit for, and he knows when he has met his match!" The captain seemed rather pleased by the compliment and continued:
31: "Once we passed the Bosphorus, the crew started complaining. A few of the Romanian sailors came up to me and asked me to toss a massive wooden box overboard. A strange-looking old man had loaded it onto the ship just before we left London. I'd noticed the men glaring at him and flashing the two-finger sign to ward off the 'evil eye' when they saw him. I tell you, the superstition of foreigners is absolutely ridiculous! I told them to get back to work immediately. But shortly after, when the fog rolled in on us, I started feeling a little bit like they did. I wouldn't blame it on the big box, though. Anyway, we kept going. Since the fog didn't break for five days, I just let the wind push us. If the Devil wanted to get somewhere, well, he'd get there just fine. If not, we'd keep a sharp lookout anyway. Sure enough, we had a clear path and deep water the entire way. Two days ago, when the morning sun broke through the mist, we found ourselves right in the river across from Galatz. The Romanians lost their minds. They didn't care about right or wrong; they just wanted me to take the box and throw it into the river. I had to argue with them using a heavy wooden spike. By the time the last of them picked himself up off the deck holding his bleeding head, I had convinced them that, 'evil eye' or not, my owners' property and trust were safer in my hands than at the bottom of the Danube. Keep in mind, they had already hauled the box up to the deck to toss it. Since it was marked 'Galatz via Varna,' I figured I'd let it sit there until we unloaded at the port and got rid of it entirely. We didn't do much unloading that day and had to spend the night anchored. But early the next morning, a full hour before sunrise, a man came aboard with a written order from England to pick up a box marked for a Count Dracula. It worked out perfectly since the thing was already sitting on the deck. His paperwork was in order, and I was thrilled to get rid of the damn thing. Honestly, I was starting to feel uneasy about it myself. If the Devil did have luggage on my ship, I'm betting it was that exact box!"
32: "What was the name of the man who took it?" Van Helsing asked, barely containing his excitement.
33: "I'll tell you right now!" the captain answered. He stepped down into his cabin and brought back a receipt signed by "Immanuel Hildesheim." The address listed was 16 Burgen-strasse. We quickly realized this was all the Captain knew, so we thanked him and left.
34: We found Hildesheim in his office. He was a Jewish man who looked like a caricature from an Adelphi Theatre play, complete with a sheep-like nose and a fez. His cooperation required cash—we were forced to punctuate every sentence with coins—but after some haggling, he told us what he knew. The information was simple but critical. He had received a letter from a Mr. de Ville in London, instructing him to receive a box arriving at Galatz on the Czarina Catherine. He was told to pick it up before sunrise, if possible, to bypass customs. Once he had the box, he was to hand it over to a man named Petrof Skinsky, who did business with the Slovak traders running down the river to the port. Hildesheim was paid with an English banknote, which he had already cashed for gold at the Danube International Bank. When Skinsky showed up, Hildesheim took him straight to the ship and handed the box over directly to save on porter fees. That was everything he knew.
35: We immediately started looking for Skinsky, but we couldn't find him. One of his neighbors, who clearly didn't like him very much, said he had left two days ago, and no one knew where he went. His landlord backed up this story, saying a messenger had dropped off Skinsky's house key and his past-due rent—paid in British currency—between 10:00 and 11:00 the previous night. We were at a dead end once again.
36: While we were talking, a man ran up, gasping for breath. He blurted out that Skinsky's body had just been found inside the graveyard wall of St. Peter's church. His throat had been ripped wide open, as if by a wild animal. The people we had been talking to rushed off to see the gruesome sight, with the women screaming, "This is the work of a Slovak!" We hurried away in the opposite direction, terrified of being dragged into the investigation and delayed.
37: We couldn't reach any solid conclusions on the way home. We were all completely convinced that the box was moving somewhere by water, but we still had to figure out where. We returned to the hotel and to Mina with heavy hearts.
38: Once we gathered, the first order of business was deciding whether to bring Mina back into our confidence. Things were getting desperate. It was a risky move, but it gave us a fighting chance. As a first step, I formally released myself from the promise I made to her to keep her in the dark.
39: Mina Harker's Journal.
40: October 30, evening.—The men were so tired, worn out, and depressed that it was useless to try and plan anything until they got some sleep. I asked them all to lie down for half an hour while I typed out everything that had happened up to this point. I am so incredibly grateful to the person who invented the "Traveller's" typewriter, and to Mr. Morris for buying this one for me. I would have been totally lost trying to do all this work with just a pen...
41: The typing is finished. My poor, dear Jonathan—the things he must have suffered, and what he must be suffering right now. He is lying on the sofa, barely looking like he's breathing, his entire body seemingly collapsed. His eyebrows are pulled together, and his face is tight with pain. Poor man, maybe he's dreaming, because his face is all scrunched up as if he is concentrating deeply on something. Oh! If only there was some way I could help... I will do everything I possibly can.
42: I asked Dr. Van Helsing for all the papers and journals I haven't seen yet... While the men are resting, I am going to read through everything very carefully. Maybe I can figure something out. I'm going to try to follow the Professor's example and look at the facts objectively, without any preconceived notions...

44: I truly believe that, by the grace of God, I have found something. I am going to get the maps and look them over...
