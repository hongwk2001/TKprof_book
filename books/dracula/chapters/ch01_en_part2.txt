As darkness fell, we arrived in Bistritz. It was a very old and fascinating town. Because it is near the border leading straight to Bukovina through the Borgo Pass, this town has endured a very stormy history, and the scars are still visible. Fifty years ago, terrible fires swept through the town five times. In the early 17th century, a three-week siege claimed the lives of 13,000 people. Alongside those who died in battle, countless others were struck down by disease and starvation.

Count Dracula had directed me to the Golden Krone Hotel. Fortunately, it had preserved its old-fashioned charm, which I liked very much, as I wanted to see as much of the local culture as possible. It was obvious that the people were expecting me. When I walked to the door, a cheerful older woman wearing an apron with bright, colorful patches greeted me. Her clothes were a bit too tight to look modest, but it was a typical peasant outfit. As I approached, she bowed and asked, "Are you the English gentleman?" "Yes, I am Jonathan Harker." She smiled and said something to an older man in his shirtsleeves who had followed her to the door. He went inside and quickly returned with a letter.

"My friend. Welcome to the Carpathian Mountains. I have been eagerly waiting for you. Rest well tonight. The stagecoach for Bukovina leaves tomorrow at 3 PM, and I have reserved a seat for you. When you reach the Borgo Pass, my carriage will be waiting to bring you here. I hope your journey from London was safe, and I hope you enjoy your entire stay in my beautiful country.

"Your friend,

"Dracula."

May 4. I found out that the hotel owner had received a letter from the Count and had pre-booked the best seat on the stagecoach for me. But when I asked him for more details, he kept his mouth shut and pretended not to understand my German. This made no sense at all. Just moments before, he had been answering all my questions easily. He and his wife (the old woman who had greeted me) exchanged frightened looks. He mumbled that the letter only contained the money for the coach fare and that he knew nothing more.

When I asked if they knew Count Dracula or had heard anything about his castle, the couple quickly crossed themselves. They insisted they knew nothing and refused to speak to me any further. The coach was about to leave, so I didn't have time to ask anyone else, but the whole situation felt somewhat creepy and ominous.

Right before I left, the old woman rushed up to my room and pleaded with a very anxious voice.

"Must you really go? Oh! Young sir, must you go?" She was so agitated that she seemed to have forgotten the little German she knew. She spoke in a confusing mix of unfamiliar languages that I couldn't understand at all. I had to ask her repeatedly before I could barely guess what she meant. When I explained that I had important business and had to leave right away, she asked again.

"Do you know what day it is today?" I answered that it was May 4th. She shook her head and spoke again.

"Oh, yes! I know that! Not the date on the calendar. I'm asking if you know what tonight is." When I looked confused, she continued.

"Tonight is the eve of St. George's Day. Don't you know that when the clock strikes midnight, all the evil things in this world will roam free? Do you have any idea where you are going right now?" She was in such distress that I tried to comfort her, but it was no use. She finally fell to her knees and tearfully begged me not to leave, or at least to stay a day or two longer. It sounded like nonsense, but honestly, I didn't feel comfortable. However, I had an official duty to fulfill, and nothing could stand in my way.

I helped her to her feet. I firmly told her that I appreciated her concern, but my business could not be delayed and I absolutely had to go. Only then did she stand up and wipe her tears. She took the cross from her own neck and offered it to me. I didn't know what to do. As a member of the Church of England, I was raised to view such crosses as somewhat idolatrous. But it felt too cruel to reject the kind gesture of a terrified old woman. Perhaps she saw the hesitation on my face, for she placed the rosary around my neck herself. "Accept this for the sake of your mother," she said. With those final words, she hurried out of the room.

As I write in my journal while waiting for the inevitably delayed coach, the cross is still around my neck. I don't know if it's the old woman's trembling voice, the ghost stories scattered around this town, or the energy coming from the cross itself. But my heart feels unusually heavy and cold. If this journal reaches Mina before I do, this might be my last goodbye. Oh, here comes the coach!
