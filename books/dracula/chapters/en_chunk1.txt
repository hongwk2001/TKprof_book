CHAPTER 12

DR. SEWARD'S DIARY

September 18.—I drove straight to Hillingham and arrived early. Leaving my cab at the gate, I walked up the driveway alone. I knocked gently and rang the bell as quietly as possible, hoping to only alert a servant so I wouldn't disturb Lucy or her mother. After waiting a while and getting no response, I knocked and rang again, but still no one answered. I cursed the laziness of the servants for sleeping in so late—it was already ten o'clock—and knocked and rang again, this time more impatiently, but the house remained completely silent. Until then, I had only blamed the staff, but a terrible fear began to wash over me. Was this eerie silence just another link in the chain of doom that seemed to be tightening around us? Had I arrived too late to a house of death? I knew that even a few seconds of delay could put Lucy in grave danger if she had suffered another one of her terrifying relapses. I ran around the house, hoping to find any way inside.

I couldn't find a way in. Every window and door was securely locked, and I returned to the front porch feeling defeated. Just then, I heard the rapid, rhythmic sound of horse hooves approaching quickly. A carriage stopped at the gate, and moments later, I saw Professor Van Helsing running up the driveway. When he spotted me, he gasped for breath and asked:

"So it was you, and you just got here. How is she? Are we too late? Didn't you receive my telegram?"

I answered him as quickly and clearly as I could, explaining that I had only received his telegram earlier that morning, had rushed over immediately, and couldn't get anyone inside the house to answer the door. He paused, solemnly lifted his hat, and said:

"Then I'm afraid we are too late. God's will be done!" But with his usual quick recovery and energy, he added: "Come on. If there's no open way in, we'll have to make one. Time is everything right now."

We went around to the back of the house where the kitchen window was located. The Professor took a small surgical saw from his bag, handed it to me, and pointed to the iron bars protecting the window. I went to work immediately and quickly sawed through three of them. Then, using a long, thin knife, we pushed back the window latch and opened the sash. I helped the Professor climb inside and followed right behind him. The kitchen and the nearby servants' quarters were completely empty. We checked every room as we moved through the house. When we reached the dining room, which was dimly lit by sunlight slipping through the window shutters, we found four maids lying unconscious on the floor. We didn't have to wonder if they were dead; their heavy, rattling breathing and the sharp smell of liquid opium in the air made their drugged condition obvious. Van Helsing and I exchanged a look, and as we hurried away, he said, "We can take care of them later." We immediately headed upstairs to Lucy's bedroom. We paused outside the door for a moment to listen, but it was completely silent inside. With pale faces and trembling hands, we slowly opened the door and stepped in.

How can I even begin to describe the scene? Two women lay on the bed: Lucy and her mother. Mrs. Westenra was lying on the far side, covered with a white sheet. The wind blowing through a broken window had pushed the sheet back, revealing her pale, drawn face, permanently frozen in a look of absolute terror. Lucy lay beside her, her face even whiter and more hollow. The protective wreath of garlic flowers that had been around Lucy's neck was now resting on her mother's chest. Lucy's throat was exposed, and the two small puncture wounds we had seen before were now horribly white and mangled. Without saying a word, the Professor leaned over the bed until his head almost touched poor Lucy's chest. Suddenly, he jerked his head up like a man straining to hear something, jumped to his feet, and shouted:

"It's not too late! Hurry! Get the brandy!"

I sprinted downstairs and grabbed it, making sure to smell and taste it first just in case it had been drugged like the bottle of sherry I spotted on the table. The maids were still breathing, though they seemed more restless now, suggesting the drug was beginning to wear off. I didn't stick around to check; I rushed back to Van Helsing. Just like he had done before, he rubbed the brandy on Lucy's lips, gums, wrists, and palms. Then he turned to me and said:

"I can handle this—it's all we can do for her right now. You go downstairs and wake those maids. Slap their faces with a wet towel, and hit them hard. Make them start a fire and draw a warm bath. This poor girl is almost as cold as her dead mother. We have to raise her body temperature before we can attempt anything else."

I rushed back down and easily woke three of the women. The fourth was just a young girl who was clearly much more affected by the drug, so I carried her to the sofa and let her sleep it off. The other three were disoriented at first, but as they started to remember what happened, they broke down into hysterical sobbing. I had to be harsh with them, refusing to let them talk. I told them that losing one life was tragedy enough, and if they wasted any more time, Miss Lucy would die too. Crying and still half-dressed, they scrambled to their tasks, getting the fires going and heating water. Fortunately, the kitchen and boiler fires hadn't completely died out, so we had plenty of hot water soon enough. We brought up a bathtub, lifted Lucy just as she was, and placed her inside. While we were frantically rubbing her limbs to warm her up, someone knocked at the front door. One of the maids threw on some more clothes and ran to answer it. She came back and whispered that a gentleman had arrived with a message from Mr. Holmwood. I simply told her to make him wait, as we couldn't see anyone at the moment. She left to deliver the message, and I was so consumed by our desperate work that I completely forgot about the man.

In all my years as a doctor, I had never seen the Professor work with such intense, desperate focus. We both knew this was a direct fight against death, and during a brief pause, I told him as much. He gave me an incredibly stern look and replied in a way I didn't entirely understand:

"If that were the only issue, I would stop right now and let her peacefully pass away, because I see no light left in her future." With that, he threw himself back into the work with even more frantic, fierce energy.

Soon, we both noticed the heat was finally having an effect. Through my stethoscope, Lucy's heartbeat sounded slightly stronger, and we could visibly see her lungs moving. Van Helsing's face lit up with relief. As we lifted her out of the bath and wrapped her in a warm sheet to dry off, he turned to me and said:

"We've won the first round! Check to the King!"

We carried Lucy into another bedroom that the maids had prepared, tucked her into bed, and forced a few drops of brandy down her throat. I noticed Van Helsing carefully tying a soft silk handkerchief around her neck to cover the wounds. She was still completely unconscious and looked just as terrible, if not worse, than she had during her previous relapses.

Van Helsing called one of the maids into the room, ordered her to sit by the bed and never take her eyes off Lucy until we came back, and then motioned for me to follow him out.

"We need to figure out our next move," he said as we walked downstairs. In the hallway, he opened the dining room door, ushered me in, and carefully shut it behind us. The window shutters were open, but the maids had already drawn the blinds—a traditional sign of mourning that working-class British women always strictly follow. Because of this, the room was quite dark, though we had enough light to see each other. Van Helsing's intense expression was now mixed with deep confusion. He was clearly agonizing over something, so I waited quietly until he finally spoke:

"What do we do now? Where can we find help? We have to give her another blood transfusion immediately, or that poor girl won't live another hour. You are already exhausted, and so am I. Even if those maids were brave enough to volunteer, I wouldn't trust their blood right now. Where are we going to find someone willing to open their veins for her?"

"What's wrong with using me?"

The voice came from a sofa on the other side of the room. Hearing it filled my heart with overwhelming relief and joy, because it belonged to Quincey Morris. Van Helsing jumped angrily at the sudden noise, but his face immediately softened into a glad smile when I shouted, "Quincey Morris!" and ran toward him with my hands outstretched.

"What are you doing here?" I asked as we shook hands.

"I think Art is the reason."

He handed me a telegram that read:

"I haven't heard from Seward in three days and I'm terrified. I can't leave because my father's condition hasn't changed. Let me know how Lucy is. Don't wait.—HOLMWOOD."
