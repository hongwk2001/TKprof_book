CHAPTER XIII

DR. SEWARD’S DIARY—continued.

The funeral was scheduled for the very next day so that Lucy and her mother could be buried together. I took care of all the gruesome paperwork, and the smooth-talking undertaker showed that his staff shared his own overly polite and submissive manner. Even the woman who prepared the body for burial spoke to me confidentially, like a fellow professional, when she came out of the room:

"She makes a beautiful corpse, sir. It's a privilege to work on her. It's no exaggeration to say she will do our establishment proud!"

I noticed that Van Helsing stayed close by. The chaotic state of the household made this easy. There were no relatives around, and since Arthur had to return the next day for his father's funeral, we couldn't notify anyone who normally should have been invited. Under these conditions, Van Helsing and I took it upon ourselves to go through her papers. He insisted on checking Lucy's documents personally. I asked him why, worrying that as a foreigner he might not understand English legal requirements and could accidentally cause trouble. He replied:

"I know, I know. You forget I am a lawyer as well as a doctor. But this isn't just about the law. You knew that when you bypassed the coroner. I have more to avoid than just him. There might be more papers—like this one."

He took out the note from his pocket that Lucy had hidden in her dress and torn while asleep.

"If you find the name of the late Mrs. Westenra's lawyer, seal all her papers and write to him tonight. As for me, I'll stay awake and keep watch in this room and Lucy's old room all night, searching for anything else. It's better that her most private thoughts don't fall into the hands of strangers."

I continued my work, and within half an hour, I found the lawyer's address and wrote to him. The poor woman's affairs were well-organized, with clear instructions for her burial. I had just sealed the envelope when Van Helsing surprised me by walking in.

"Can I help you, friend John? I'm free, and I’d like to offer my services."

"Did you find what you were looking for?" I asked.

"I wasn't looking for anything specific," he said. "I just hoped to find whatever was there—and I did. It's only a few letters, some notes, and a recently started diary. I have them here, but we'll keep quiet about them for now. I'll see the poor young man tomorrow evening and, with his permission, use some of them."

When we finished our tasks, he told me:

"Now, friend John, we should go to bed. We both need sleep and rest to recover. We have a lot to do tomorrow, but we aren't needed tonight. Alas!"

Before turning in, we went to see poor Lucy. The undertaker had done an excellent job, turning the room into a small shrine. There were beautiful white flowers everywhere, making death look as gentle as possible. The shroud covered her face, but when the Professor gently pulled it back, we were both stunned by her beauty in the candlelight. All of Lucy's loveliness had returned in death. Instead of decaying, the passing hours had somehow restored her vibrant beauty. I honestly couldn't believe I was looking at a dead body.

The Professor looked grim and serious. He hadn't loved her like I did, so he didn't need to cry. He told me to wait and left the room. He returned with a handful of wild garlic from a box in the hall and scattered it among the flowers on and around the bed. Then, he took a small gold crucifix from inside his collar and placed it over her mouth. He pulled the shroud back over her face, and we left.

I was getting undressed in my room when he knocked and immediately walked in.

"Before tomorrow night, I need you to bring me a set of autopsy knives," he said.

"Are we doing an autopsy?" I asked.

"Yes and no. I need to operate, but not the way you think. I'll tell you now, but keep it a secret. I want to cut off her head and remove her heart. Ah! You're a surgeon, yet you look shocked! I've seen you perform terrifying life-and-death surgeries without a single tremor. But I haven't forgotten that you loved her, my friend. That's why I will do the surgery, and you will only assist. I’d do it tonight, but we must wait for Arthur. He’ll be free after his father’s funeral tomorrow, and he’ll want to see her—to see the body. Then, after she is placed in the coffin tomorrow night and everyone is asleep, we will unscrew the lid, perform the operation, and put everything back so no one but us will ever know."

"But why do it at all?" I argued. "The girl is dead. Why needlessly mutilate her body? If there's no medical need and no benefit to her, to us, or to science, why do it? It’s monstrous without a valid reason."

He placed his hand on my shoulder and replied with immense tenderness:

"Friend John, I pity your broken heart, and I love you even more because you care so much. If I could, I would carry this burden for you. But there are things you don't know yet. When you finally understand, you will thank me, even though these aren't pleasant things. John, my boy, we've been friends for years—have you ever known me to act without a good reason? I make mistakes because I'm only human, but I believe in what I do. Didn't you call me because of these very same reasons when trouble started? Weren't you horrified when I violently pulled Arthur away from kissing his dying love? Yes! And yet, didn't you see how she thanked me with her beautiful eyes and weak voice, kissing my rough hand? Yes! Didn't you hear me swear a promise to her so she could close her eyes in peace? Yes!

"I have a good reason for everything I'm asking. You've trusted me for years, and you've trusted me over these past few bizarre weeks when you easily could have doubted me. Trust me a little longer. If you can't, I will have to tell you what I suspect, and that might not be a good idea. Even if you don't trust me, I will still do what I must. But working without my friend's support will make me heavy-hearted and terribly lonely when I need courage the most." He paused and added solemnly, "Friend John, strange and terrible days are coming. We must stand united to see this through. Will you have faith in me?"

I took his hand and promised I would. I held my door open as he left and watched him go into his room. While I stood there, I noticed one of the maids quietly walking down the hall. Her back was to me, so she didn't see me as she slipped into the room where Lucy lay. It moved me. True devotion is rare, and it's a blessing when people offer it unprompted to those we love. This poor girl had put aside her natural fear of death to keep watch over her beloved mistress, ensuring she wouldn't be lonely before her final rest.

* * * * *

I must have slept long and deeply, because it was broad daylight when Van Helsing walked into my room and woke me up. He came to the side of my bed and said:

"Don't worry about getting the knives. We won't be doing it."

"Why not?" I asked, as his intense seriousness from the night before had left a strong impression on me.

"Because," he said sternly, "it is either too late—or too early. Look!" He held up the small gold crucifix. "This was stolen during the night."

"How could it be stolen," I asked in confusion, "if you have it right now?"

"Because I took it back from the miserable wretch who stole it—the woman who robbed both the dead and the living. She will get her punishment, but not from me. She didn't fully understand what she was doing; she simply acted out of ignorance and greed. Now, we just have to wait."

He left right after saying that, leaving me with yet another strange mystery to figure out.

The morning dragged on drearily, but at noon, Mrs. Westenra's lawyer arrived: Mr. Marquand, from the firm Wholeman, Sons, Marquand & Lidderdale. He was very pleasant and appreciative of everything we had done, taking all the practical details off our hands. During lunch, he explained that Mrs. Westenra had been anticipating sudden heart failure for a while and had put all her affairs in perfect order. He told us that aside from some specific family property of Lucy's father—which would now pass to distant relatives since there was no direct heir—her entire estate, including property and personal assets, was left entirely to Arthur Holmwood. After delivering that news, he elaborated:

"To be completely honest, we tried our best to talk her out of this will. We pointed out that such an arrangement could leave her daughter with nothing or restrict her independence if she were to marry. We argued so strongly that we almost had a falling out. She outright asked us if we were willing to follow her instructions or not. At that point, we had no choice but to comply. On principle, we were right, and ninety-nine times out of a hundred, events would have proven our legal judgment sound. However, I have to admit that in this particular tragedy, any other arrangement would have ruined her intentions. Because she died before her daughter, Lucy inherited the estate. Even if Lucy had survived her mother by only five minutes, she would have died without a will—making one under those circumstances was practically impossible. Under the law of intestacy, Lord Godalming, despite being her closest confidant, would have had no legal claim whatsoever. The distant relatives who stood to inherit wouldn't have given up their legal rights out of mere sentimentality for a total stranger. I promise you, gentlemen, I am thrilled with this outcome. Perfectly thrilled."

He was a nice enough man, but seeing him rejoice over his one small professional victory amidst such a massive tragedy was a stark reminder of how narrow people's empathy can sometimes be.

He didn’t stay long, but promised to drop by later in the afternoon to speak with Lord Godalming. Still, his visit brought us some relief; it meant we wouldn't face any hostile legal scrutiny over how we had handled the situation. Arthur was expected at five o’clock, so shortly before then, we went to check on the room holding the bodies. It truly felt like a tomb now, holding both mother and daughter. The undertaker, eager to show off his work, had staged the room elaborately, giving the space a heavy, depressing atmosphere. Van Helsing immediately ordered him to return the room to how it looked before. He explained that since Lord Godalming was arriving shortly, it would be less traumatizing if he saw only his fiancée alone without the extra morbid decorations. The undertaker seemed embarrassed by his own lack of tact and scrambled to restore the simple setup we had left the night before, sparing Arthur any unnecessary additional shock.

Poor guy! He looked utterly heartbroken and exhausted. Even his strong, manly posture seemed to have wilted under the crushing weight of his emotions. I knew he was deeply devoted to his father, and losing him at a time like this was a devastating blow. He was as warm as ever with me, and perfectly polite to Van Helsing, but I couldn't help noticing a bit of tension. The Professor noticed it too and motioned for me to take Arthur upstairs. I led him up and intended to leave him at the door, figuring he'd want to be alone with her, but he grabbed my arm and pulled me inside. His voice was hoarse as he spoke:

"You loved her too, old friend. She told me everything, and there was no friend closer to her heart than you. I don't know how to thank you for everything you did for her. I can't even think straight right now...."

Suddenly, he broke down. He threw his arms around my shoulders, buried his face against my chest, and sobbed.

"Oh, Jack! Jack! What am I going to do? It feels like my entire life has been ripped away all at once. There's nothing left in the world for me to live for."

I comforted him as best I could. Men don't need many words in situations like this. A firm handshake, an arm thrown over the shoulder, or crying together—these are the expressions of sympathy that matter most. I stood there silently until his sobbing quieted down, and then I said softly:

"Come and look at her."

We walked over to the bed together, and I lifted the cloth from her face. God! She was so beautiful. Every passing hour seemed to make her even more stunning. It frankly scared and amazed me. As for Arthur, he started shaking and was suddenly overcome with a chilling doubt. After a long silence, he whispered faintly:

"Jack, is she really dead?"

I sadly assured him that she was. Wanting to crush that horrible doubt immediately, I quickly explained that faces often soften after death, returning to their youthful beauty—especially after long or severe suffering. This explanation seemed to satisfy him. He knelt beside the couch for a long time, gazing at her with deep love, before finally turning away. I gently reminded him that it was time to say goodbye so the coffin could be prepared. He went back, took her cold hand, kissed it, and bent down to kiss her forehead. As he walked out of the room, he kept looking back at her over his shoulder with longing.

I left him in the drawing room and informed Van Helsing that he had said his goodbyes. The Professor went to the kitchen to tell the undertaker's men to finish their preparations and seal the coffin. When he returned, I mentioned Arthur's question.

"I'm not surprised," he replied. "I even doubted it myself for a moment just now!"

We all ate dinner together, and I could tell poor Arthur was trying his best to hold it together. Van Helsing had been quiet through the meal, but when we lit our cigars, he spoke up.

"Lord—"

Arthur quickly cut him off.

"No, no, please, not that! Not yet, anyway. I'm sorry, sir, I don't mean to be rude. It's just that the loss is so fresh."

The Professor answered very gently:

"I only used that title because I wasn't sure how to address you. I shouldn't call you 'Mr.,' and I've grown to care for you like a son—yes, my dear boy. I want to call you Arthur."

Arthur reached out and warmly shook the old man's hand.

"Call me whatever you like," he said. "I hope I can always consider you a friend. I don't have the words to thank you for all the kindness you showed my poor darling." He paused for a moment before continuing. "I know she understood your good intentions better than I did. If I was rude or disrespectful back when you acted so... intensely—you know what I mean—you have to forgive me."

The Professor nodded and answered with solemn kindness:

"I know it was hard for you to trust me then. To trust such violent actions, you need to understand them, and I accept that you still don't—and can't—fully trust me now because you still don't understand. There may be more times ahead when I will need your trust even though you cannot, may not, and must not understand yet. But a day will come when you will trust me completely, and everything will be as clear as sunlight. Then you will thank me—for your own sake, for the sake of others, and for the sake of the woman I swore to protect."

"Sir, I promise I will trust you completely," Arthur said sincerely. "I know you have a noble heart. You're Jack's friend, and you were hers. Do whatever you think is right."

The Professor cleared his throat a few times before finally asking:

"May I ask you a favor now?"

"Of course."

"You know that Mrs. Westenra left her entire estate to you?"

"No, poor woman. That never even crossed my mind."

"Since it's all yours, you have the right to do what you want with it. I need your permission to read all of Miss Lucy's papers and letters. I promise it isn't just idle curiosity. I have a very good reason—one she would have approved of. I already have the papers here. I gathered them before we knew everything belonged to you, just to make sure no stranger could read her most private thoughts. Let me hold onto them. Even you shouldn't see them just yet, but I will keep them safe. Nothing will be lost, and when the time is right, I'll return them to you. It's a massive request, I know, but will you do it for Lucy's sake?"

Arthur answered openly, sounding more like his old self:

"Dr. Van Helsing, do whatever you need to do. I feel certain that my dear Lucy would have wanted this. I won't bother you with any questions until you're ready."

The old Professor stood up and said solemnly:

"You're making the right choice. This will bring us all pain, but it won't only be pain, and it won't be the last of it. We—and especially you, my boy—have to wade through bitter waters before we find the sweet ones. But if we stay brave, unselfish, and do our duty, everything will be alright!"

I slept on a sofa in Arthur's room that night. Van Helsing didn't sleep at all. He paced back and forth, patrolling the house, never letting the room where Lucy lay in her coffin out of his sight. The room was scattered with wild garlic flowers, whose heavy, overwhelming scent cut through the smell of lilies and roses into the night air.

Mina Harker’s Journal.

September 22.—On the train to Exeter. Jonathan is asleep.

It feels like just yesterday I made my last entry, yet so much has happened since Whitby and my old life. Jonathan had been away, and I heard nothing from him. Now, we're married. He's a lawyer, a full partner, wealthy, and running his own firm. Mr. Hawkins has passed away and been buried, and Jonathan is suffering another attack that I worry might seriously harm him. He might ask me about it someday, so I'm writing everything down. My shorthand is rusty—it goes to show what sudden prosperity does to a person—so I might as well brush up on it as an exercise...

The funeral service was simple and very serious. It was just us, the servants, a couple of his old friends from Exeter, his London agent, and a representative for Sir John Paxton, the President of the Incorporated Law Society. Jonathan and I stood hand in hand, feeling the deep loss of our best and dearest friend...

We quietly returned to London, taking a bus to Hyde Park Corner. Jonathan thought I might enjoy walking along Rotten Row for a bit, so we sat down. However, it was almost empty, and seeing so many vacant chairs felt sad and lonely. It reminded us of the empty chair waiting at home, so we got up and walked down Piccadilly. Jonathan held my arm just like he used to before I went away to school. I felt it was a bit inappropriate—you can't teach etiquette and manners to young girls for years without becoming a bit of a stickler yourself—but it was Jonathan, my husband. We didn't know anyone who saw us, and we honestly wouldn't have cared if they did, so we kept walking. I was admiring a gorgeous young woman in a huge cart-wheel hat sitting in a carriage outside Guiliano’s restaurant when Jonathan suddenly gripped my arm so tightly it hurt. Under his breath, he gasped, "My God!" I'm always worried that another nervous breakdown might strike him, so I quickly turned and asked him what was wrong.

He was deathly pale. His eyes bulged with a mix of terror and shock as he stared at a tall, thin man with a hooked nose, a black mustache, and a pointed beard. The man was also watching the beautiful girl. He was staring at her so intensely that he didn't notice us at all, giving me a perfect view of him. He had a cruel, hard, and overly sensual face. His large white teeth—which looked even whiter against his bright red lips—were pointed like an animal's. Jonathan kept staring at him until I began to worry the man would notice. He looked so fierce and nasty that I was afraid he'd take offense. I asked Jonathan why he was so upset, and he answered, clearly assuming I knew exactly what he was talking about: "Do you see who that is?"

"No, dear," I said. "I don't know him. Who is it?" His response shocked and terrified me, mostly because he spoke as if he had completely forgotten he was talking to me.

"It's the man himself!"

My poor husband was absolutely terrified of something. I genuinely believe that if he hadn't been leaning on me for support, he would have collapsed. He kept staring. A man came out of a shop, handed a small package to the lady, and she drove off. The dark man never took his eyes off her, and as her carriage moved down Piccadilly, he hailed a cab to follow her. Jonathan stared after him and muttered to himself:

"I believe it's the Count, but he looks younger. My God, if it's really him! Oh, my God! If I only knew! If I only knew for sure!" He was upsetting himself so much that I was afraid to ask any questions and keep his mind focused on it, so I stayed quiet. I gently pulled him away, and he followed easily, still holding my arm. We walked a little further and then sat down in Green Park. It was unusually hot for an autumn day, but we found a comfortable, shady bench. After staring blankly for a few minutes, Jonathan closed his eyes and fell quietly asleep with his head on my shoulder. I figured it was exactly what he needed, so I let him rest. About twenty minutes later, he woke up and said cheerfully:

"Wow, Mina, did I fall asleep? I'm so sorry for being rude. Come on, let's go find a cup of tea." He had completely forgotten the dark stranger, just as his illness had erased everything else related to whatever trauma that man reminded him of. I don't like these sudden memory lapses; I worry they might be causing or hiding brain damage. I can't ask him about it in case I do more harm than good, but I desperately need to find out what happened on his trip abroad. I think the time has come for me to finally open that package and read what he wrote. Oh, Jonathan, I know you will forgive me if I'm doing the wrong thing, but I'm only doing it for your own good.

* * * * *

Later.—A terribly sad homecoming all around. The house feels empty without the dear man who was so good to us. Jonathan is still pale and dizzy from a slight relapse of his illness. And now, a telegram just arrived from a Van Helsing, whoever that is:

"You will be grieved to hear that Mrs. Westenra died five days ago, and that Lucy died the day before yesterday. They were both buried today."

So much tragedy packed into a few words! Poor Mrs. Westenra! Poor Lucy! Gone, never to return. And poor Arthur, losing such a sweet light from his life! May God give us all the strength to bear our troubles.

Dr. Seward’s Diary.

September 22.—It is completely finished. Arthur has gone back to Ring and taken Quincey Morris with him. What a remarkable man Quincey is! I truly believe he suffered over Lucy's death just as much as the rest of us, but he endured it like a stoic Viking. If America keeps producing men like him, it will truly become a global powerhouse. Van Helsing is resting before his trip. He's traveling to Amsterdam tonight but says he'll be back tomorrow evening; he just has some arrangements he must handle in person. He plans to stay with me when he returns, mentioning that he has work in London that might take a while. Poor old guy! I'm afraid the stress of the past week has finally broken through even his iron endurance. I could tell he was holding himself together with incredible effort throughout the entire burial. When it was finally over, we stood next to Arthur. The poor man was talking about the blood transfusion, saying he felt that giving Lucy his blood had practically married them, and that in God's eyes, she was now his wife. None of us mentioned the other transfusions, and none of us ever will. After Arthur and Quincey left for the train station, Van Helsing and I headed back here. The moment we were alone in the carriage, he completely lost it in a fit of hysterics. He has since denied it was hysterics, insisting it was just his sense of humor breaking through under extreme stress. He laughed until he cried, and I had to pull down the carriage blinds so nobody would see us and jump to conclusions. Then he cried until he started laughing again, doing both at the same time like an overwhelmed woman. I tried to speak to him sternly, the way you would handle a woman in that state, but it didn't work at all. Men and women really do handle nervous breakdowns differently! Once his face finally settled back into a serious expression, I asked him why on earth he was laughing at a time like this. His answer was strange, powerful, and perfectly characteristic of him:

"Ah, you don't understand, friend John. Don't think I'm not heartbroken just because I laugh. Look, I cry even when the laughter chokes me. But don't assume I'm purely miserable when I cry, because the laugh comes along with it. Always remember that the laughter that politely knocks and asks, 'May I come in?' is not real laughter. No! Real laughter is a king, and he arrives whenever and however he pleases. He asks no one and cares nothing for good timing. He simply says, 'I am here.' For example, my heart is breaking for that sweet girl. I gave her my blood even though I'm old and tired. I gave her my time, my skill, and my sleep, neglecting my other patients so she could have everything. And yet, I can stand at her grave and laugh. I laugh when the dirt from the gravedigger's spade hits her coffin with a 'Thud! Thud!' that echoes in my chest and drains the blood from my face. My heart bleeds for that dear boy—he's the exact age my own son would be if he had lived, with the exact same hair and eyes. That's why I love him so much. Yet, when he says things that pierce my heart as a husband, and make me ache for him as a father—even more than I care for you, friend John, since we are more like peers than father and son—King Laugh still crashes into my ear and shouts, 'Here I am!' until the blood rushes back to my face and brings a little sunshine. Oh, John, it's a strange, sad world full of misery and trouble. But when King Laugh arrives, he makes all that suffering dance to his tune. Broken hearts, dry bones in a graveyard, and burning tears—they all dance together to the music he plays without even smiling. And trust me, John, it is a blessing when he comes. Men and women are like ropes pulled tight by stress. Then tears fall, acting like rain to brace the ropes, until the tension becomes too great and we snap. But King Laugh comes like sunshine to ease the tension, allowing us to carry on with our heavy work, whatever it may be."

I didn't want to hurt his feelings by pretending I didn't get his point, but I still didn't understand exactly *what* had triggered his laughter, so I asked him directly. His face hardened, and his tone completely changed:

"Oh, it was the grim irony of it all. This lovely woman, decorated with flowers, looking so beautiful that we all caught ourselves wondering if she was actually dead. She's placed in a grand marble tomb in an isolated graveyard with her mother. The church bell rings 'Toll! Toll! Toll!' so slowly and sadly. And those holy men in white robes pretend to read from their books, even though they never actually look at the pages. And all of us standing there with our heads bowed. And for what? She's dead, right? Isn't she?"

"Well, Professor," I said, "for the life of me, I can't see what's funny about any of that. Your explanation just makes it more confusing. Even if the funeral itself was slightly absurd, what about poor Arthur's grief? His heart was literally breaking."

"Exactly. Didn't he say that giving her his blood made her truly his bride?"

"Yes, and it gave him a lot of comfort."

"Indeed. But there's a problem, friend John. If that's true, then what about the rest of you? Ha! That makes this sweet girl a polyandrist with multiple husbands. And as for me—whose poor wife is dead to my daily life but still legally alive according to the Church, though her mind is gone—it makes me, a faithful husband, a bigamist."

"I don't see the joke in that either," I said. I wasn't particularly happy with him for saying such things. He put his hand on my arm and said:

"Friend John, forgive me if I hurt you. I didn't show my feelings to the others because it would have wounded them. I only showed it to you, my trusted old friend. If you could have looked into my heart right when I needed to laugh, or when the laughter actually hit me, or even now, when King Laugh has packed up and left me for a long, long time—you would probably pity me more than anyone else."

His tender tone moved me, and I asked him why.

"Because I know what's really happening!"

And now, we are all scattered. Loneliness will hang over our homes for a long time. Lucy rests in her family's grand tomb in an isolated graveyard, far from the busy streets of London, where the air is clean, the sun rises over Hampstead Hill, and wildflowers grow freely.

So, I can finally finish this diary. God only knows if I will ever start another one. If I do, or if I ever open this one again, it will be to write about different people and different things. For now, at the end of the most dramatic chapter of my life, before I return to my regular work, I write this sadly and without hope:

"THE END."

"The Westminster Gazette," September 25.

A HAMPSTEAD MYSTERY.

The Hampstead area is currently gripped by a series of strange events similar to what the papers previously dubbed "The Kensington Horror," "The Stabbing Woman," or "The Woman in Black." Over the last two or three days, several young children have wandered away from home or failed to return from playing on the Heath. The children were all too young to explain clearly what happened to them, but they all share the same excuse: they were playing with a "beautiful lady." They always went missing late in the evening, and in two cases, they weren't found until early the next morning. Locals generally believe that because the first missing child claimed a "beautiful lady" invited him for a walk, the others simply copied the phrase as an excuse. This makes sense since the kids' favorite game right now is pretending to lure each other away. A correspondent told us it’s actually incredibly funny to see these tiny toddlers pretending to be the "beautiful lady." He suggested that political cartoonists could learn a lot about grotesque irony by comparing the kids' innocent play to the dark reality. It's human nature that the "beautiful lady" has become the most popular character in these outdoor games. Our correspondent joked that even the famous actress Ellen Terry couldn't be as charmingly persuasive as these grubby-faced little kids pretend—and imagine themselves—to be.

There is, however, a potentially serious side to this. Some of the children—specifically, all the ones who went missing overnight—were found with small scratches or wounds on their throats. The injuries look like they could have been made by a rat or a small dog. While minor on their own, the consistent injuries suggest that whatever animal is attacking them has a specific pattern. The local police have been ordered to keep a sharp lookout for lost toddlers around Hampstead Heath, as well as any stray dogs in the area.

"The Westminster Gazette," September 25.

Extra Special Edition.

THE HAMPSTEAD HORROR.

ANOTHER CHILD INJURED.

The “Beautiful Lady.”

We have just received reports that another child, who went missing last night, was found late this morning hiding under a bush near Shooter’s Hill on Hampstead Heath, a less crowded area. The child had the same tiny throat wound seen in the previous cases. The child was severely weak and looked completely drained. After partially recovering, the child told the exact same story: being lured away by the "beautiful lady."
