"I think I arrived exactly when I was needed. You know you just have to tell me what to do."

Van Helsing stepped forward, looked him straight in the eyes, and grabbed his hand as he said:

"A brave man's blood is the greatest gift in the world when a woman is in trouble. You are a true man, without a doubt. The devil might be working against us with all his power, but God sends us good men right when we need them most."

Once again, we performed that gruesome procedure. I don't have the heart to write down all the details again. Lucy's body had been through a terrible shock, and the strain was showing more than ever before. Even though we pumped plenty of fresh blood into her veins, her body didn't respond nearly as well as it had during the previous transfusions. Watching and listening to her violent physical struggle to claw her way back to life was terrifying. Eventually, however, her heart and lungs started working better, and Van Helsing gave her another shot of morphine under the skin, which helped calm her down. Her faint turned into a deep, heavy sleep. The Professor stayed to watch over her while Quincey and I went downstairs. I sent one of the maids out to pay the waiting cab driver. I made Quincey drink a glass of wine and lie down, and then I told the cook to prepare a large, hearty breakfast. Suddenly remembering something, I hurried back upstairs to Lucy's room. When I slipped quietly inside, I saw Van Helsing holding a piece or two of stationery. He had clearly just finished reading it and was sitting with his hand pressed against his forehead, deep in thought. There was a look of grim satisfaction on his face, like a man who had finally solved a difficult puzzle. He handed the paper to me and simply said, "This fell out of Lucy's nightgown when we were carrying her to the bathtub."

After reading it, I stood there staring at the Professor. After a long silence, I finally asked, "In God's name, what does this all mean? Was she out of her mind when she wrote this, or is she actually facing some kind of unimaginable, horrific danger?" I was so overwhelmed and confused that I literally didn't know what else to say. Van Helsing reached out, took the paper back from me, and said:

"Don't worry about this right now. Put it out of your mind. You will know and understand everything when the time is right, but that time is not today. Now, what was it you originally came up here to tell me?" His practical question snapped me back to reality, and I regained my professional composure.

"I came to discuss the death certificate. If we aren't extremely careful and smart about how we handle this, there will be an official police inquest, and we'd be forced to submit this note as evidence. I'm desperately hoping we can avoid an inquest. Even if nothing else killed poor Lucy, the stress of a police investigation certainly would. I know, you know, and her regular doctor knows that Mrs. Westenra suffered from severe heart disease, so we can legally certify that she died from heart failure. Let's fill out the death certificate right now. I'll personally take it down to the registrar's office and then stop by the undertaker."

"Excellent, my dear friend John! That is very smart thinking. Poor Miss Lucy may be surrounded by terrible enemies, but she is at least blessed with friends who truly love her. One, two, three strong men—plus one old man—have all gladly opened their veins to save her. Oh yes, I know what you all did, friend John; I'm not blind! And I love you all even more for it. Now, go take care of the paperwork."

Down in the hallway, I ran into Quincey Morris holding a telegram he had drafted for Arthur. It explained that Mrs. Westenra had passed away, that Lucy had been very sick but was doing better, and that Van Helsing and I were staying with her. I told Quincey where I was heading, and he practically pushed me out the door to hurry me along, but just as I was leaving, he said:

"When you get back, Jack, can we have a private word?" I nodded and left. Registering the death went smoothly, and I arranged for the local undertaker to come by the house that evening to measure the body for the coffin and finalize the funeral plans.

Quincey was waiting for me when I returned. I told him we would talk as soon as I checked on Lucy, and I headed upstairs. She was still fast asleep, and the Professor looked like he hadn't moved an inch from his chair beside her bed. He put his finger to his lips, signaling that he expected her to wake up soon and didn't want us to accidentally disturb her natural rest. So I went back downstairs, found Quincey, and brought him into the breakfast room. The blinds were open in there, making it feel a bit brighter—or at least less depressing—than the rest of the house. Once we were completely alone, he turned to me and said:

"Jack, I don't want to stick my nose in where it doesn't belong, but this obviously isn't a normal medical situation. You know I loved Lucy and wanted to marry her. Even though she chose someone else, I still care deeply about her and I'm terrified for her safety. What exactly is wrong with her? That Dutchman—and he seems like a hell of a good man, I can tell you that much—said when you guys walked in that you needed another blood transfusion because you were both totally tapped out. Now, I know you doctors have your secrets and regular folks aren't supposed to ask what you discuss behind closed doors. But this is way beyond normal, and whatever the hell is happening, I've already bled for it. Right?"

"Right," I agreed, and he continued:

"I'm assuming that both you and Van Helsing already did exactly what I just did today. Right?"

"Right."

"And I'm guessing Art gave blood too. I saw him four days ago at his country estate, and he looked like walking death. I haven't seen a living creature drained that fast since I was riding in the Pampas and had a favorite horse drop dead overnight. A massive vampire bat got to her while she slept, and between what it drank and the blood she lost from the open vein, she was too weak to stand. I had to shoot her where she lay. Jack, answer me this, and I promise it won't betray any medical confidence: Arthur was the first to give blood, wasn't he?" The poor guy looked absolutely tortured as he asked. He was trapped in an agonizing suspense over the woman he loved, and being kept in the dark about the horrifying mystery surrounding her was only making his pain worse. It was breaking his heart, and it was taking every ounce of his considerable willpower just to keep from crying. I hesitated. I didn't want to reveal anything the Professor wanted kept secret, but Quincey had already figured out so much on his own that there was no point in denying it. So, I answered him the exact same way: "Right."

"How long has this been happening to her?"

"About ten days."

"Ten days! Good God, Jack, you're telling me that this sweet, beautiful girl we all love has been pumped full of blood from four grown men in less than two weeks? Man, her body physically couldn't hold that much blood!" He leaned in close to my face and demanded in a harsh whisper, "Where the hell did it all go?"

I just shook my head. "That," I replied, "is the million-dollar question. Van Helsing is losing his mind over it, and I'm completely stumped. I can't even make an educated guess. A bunch of little mistakes and accidents ruined our plans to keep a proper watch over her. But I swear, it won't happen again. We aren't leaving this house until she either recovers completely—or dies." Quincey held out his hand to me. "Count me in," he said firmly. "You and the Dutchman just tell me what you need, and I'll do it."

When Lucy finally woke up late in the afternoon, her very first instinct was to reach for her chest. To my shock, she pulled out the very note Van Helsing had shown me earlier. The careful Professor had tucked it back into her clothes so she wouldn't panic when she realized it was missing. Her eyes found Van Helsing, then me, and a look of relief washed over her face. But then she looked around the room, realized where she was, and began to tremble. She let out a loud, heartbroken wail and covered her pale face with her thin hands. Both of us knew exactly what it meant—she had fully remembered her mother's death. We did everything we could to comfort her. Our sympathy seemed to help a little, but she remained deeply depressed and exhausted, weeping quietly for a long time. We promised her that one or both of us would stay by her side every second of the day, which seemed to bring her some peace. As evening approached, she drifted off into a light sleep. Then, something very strange happened. While she was still completely asleep, she pulled the note out of her nightgown and tore it in half. Van Helsing quickly stepped forward and took the pieces away from her. Even with the paper gone, her hands continued making the tearing motions, as if she were still ripping it up. Finally, she raised her hands and flicked her fingers open, mimicking the action of throwing the torn shreds away. Van Helsing looked surprised and furrowed his brow as if trying to solve a riddle, but he kept his thoughts to himself.

*       *       *       *       *

September 19.—She slept terribly all through the night. She was terrified of falling asleep, and every time she woke up, she seemed slightly weaker than before. The Professor and I took turns sitting watch, never leaving her alone for a single second. Quincey Morris hadn't told us his exact plans, but I knew he spent the entire night patrolling circles around the outside of the house.

When morning came, the bright daylight revealed just how much the ordeal had destroyed poor Lucy's body. She barely had the strength to turn her head on the pillow, and the small amounts of food we managed to feed her didn't seem to help at all. She drifted in and out of sleep, and both Van Helsing and I noticed a bizarre difference in her appearance depending on whether she was awake or asleep. While she was sleeping, she actually looked physically stronger, though her face seemed more gaunt and her breathing was smoother. Her mouth would hang open, revealing that her pale gums had receded from her teeth, which made her teeth look noticeably longer and sharper than normal. But the moment she woke up, the gentle look in her eyes completely changed her face back to normal—she looked like the sweet Lucy we knew, just a dying version of her. In the afternoon, she asked to see Arthur, so we sent him an urgent telegram. Quincey went to the train station to pick him up.

Arthur arrived just before six o'clock. The sun was setting, casting a warm, red glow through the bedroom window that brought a little bit of color back to Lucy's pale cheeks. When Arthur saw her condition, he was so choked up with grief he couldn't even speak, and the rest of us were just as speechless. As the day had gone on, her periods of sleep—or whatever coma-like state she was falling into—had become much more frequent, leaving her with less and less time to actually talk. But Arthur's arrival seemed to give her a sudden burst of energy. She rallied slightly and spoke to him more cheerfully than she had since we found her. He forced himself to pull it together and acted as upbeat as possible, making the best of a terrible situation.

It's almost one o'clock in the morning now, and Arthur and Van Helsing are sitting with her. I'm supposed to take over for them in fifteen minutes, so I'm recording this entry into my phonograph now. They are going to try and sleep until six o'clock. I'm terrified that tomorrow will be the end of our vigil. The shock of everything has just been too much for her body to handle; the poor girl simply can't recover. God help us all.

Letter, Mina Harker to Lucy Westenra.

(Lucy never opened this letter.)

"It feels like a lifetime since I last heard from you, or even since I wrote to you myself. I know you'll forgive me for being such a bad pen pal once you read all my news. Well, I finally brought my husband back safely! When we arrived at the train station in Exeter, a carriage was waiting for us, and Mr. Hawkins was inside, even though he's currently suffering from a bad case of gout. He brought us to his house, where he had a beautiful, comfortable set of rooms prepared for us, and we all had dinner together. After dinner, Mr. Hawkins raised a glass and said:

"‘My dears, I want to toast to your health and success, and pray that every blessing finds you both. I've known you both since you were children, and I've watched you grow up with so much love and pride. I want you to live here with me permanently. I don't have any family or children of my own left; they've all passed on. So, I've rewritten my will to leave everything I own to the two of you.' I burst into tears, dear Lucy, as Jonathan and the sweet old man shook hands. We had a wonderfully happy evening together.

"So now we are officially living in this gorgeous historic house. From both my bedroom and the living room windows, I can see the huge elm trees surrounding the cathedral courtyard. Their thick black trunks look so striking against the ancient yellow stone of the cathedral walls. I can hear the crows nesting up high, cawing and gossiping with each other all day long—they really do act just like people! I don't even need to tell you how busy I've been unpacking and getting the house organized. Jonathan and Mr. Hawkins have been working non-stop every day, too. Now that Jonathan is officially a partner in the firm, Mr. Hawkins insists on briefing him on every single client.

I have way too much to do here, and Jonathan still needs someone to take care of him. He's finally starting to gain some weight back, but that long illness left him incredibly weak. Even now, he'll sometimes wake up in a blind panic, shaking uncontrollably, and I have to stay up and soothe him until he calms down again. Thank God those panic attacks are happening less often as time goes on, and I'm confident they'll eventually stop entirely.
