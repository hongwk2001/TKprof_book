Chapter 15
Dr. Seward's Diary — continued.
For a moment, sheer anger overwhelmed me. It felt as if he had slapped Lucy across the face while she was alive. I slammed my hand hard on the table and stood up, saying:
"Dr. Van Helsing, are you insane?" He looked up at me, and somehow, the gentle expression on his face instantly calmed me down. "I wish I were!" he said. "Losing my mind would be far easier to bear than facing a truth like this. Oh, my friend, why do you think I beat around the bush, taking so long to tell you something so straightforward? Was it because I've secretly hated you all my life? Did I want to cause you pain? Or was I seeking revenge, after all this time, for when you saved my life from a horrific death? Ah, certainly not!"
"Forgive me," I said. He continued:
"My friend, it was because I know how much you loved that sweet lady, and I wanted to break the news to you as gently as possible. Even now, I don't expect you to believe it. It's difficult enough to accept an abstract concept that contradicts everything we've always known to be impossible. It's infinitely harder to accept such a tragic, concrete reality, especially when it concerns someone like Miss Lucy. Tonight, I am going to prove it. Do you have the courage to come with me?"
I was stunned. No man wants to prove a truth that horrific—except, perhaps, when driven by jealousy, as Byron once noted.
"And prove the very truth he most abhorred."
Seeing my hesitation, he spoke again:
"The logic is quite simple, and this isn't the rambling logic of a madman lost in a foggy swamp. If it isn't true, finding out will bring us relief; at the very worst, it won't do any harm. But if it is true! Ah, that's where the real terror lies. Yet, that very dread should help my cause, because facing it requires a leap of faith. Here is what I propose: first, let's go right now to see that child in the hospital. Dr. Vincent at the North Hospital—where the papers say the child is staying—is a friend of mine, and likely yours too, from when we all studied in Amsterdam. He will allow two scientists to examine his patient, even if he wouldn't let two friends do it. We won't tell him our suspicions; we'll simply say we wish to observe. And then—"
"And then what?" He pulled a key from his pocket and held it up. "And then, you and I will spend the night in the cemetery where Lucy rests. This is the key to her family's vault. I got it from the undertaker to pass along to Arthur." My heart sank. I knew some terrifying ordeal lay ahead of us. However, there was nothing I could do but gather whatever courage I had left. I told him we had better hurry, as the afternoon was already slipping away....
We found the child awake. He had slept and eaten, and overall, he was recovering well. Dr. Vincent removed the bandage from the child's neck and showed us the puncture wounds. There was no denying it—they were exactly like the ones on Lucy's throat. The only difference was that these marks were smaller and looked more recent. We asked Dr. Vincent what he thought had caused them. He replied that it must have been the bite of an animal, perhaps a rat, but personally, he suspected one of the bats that are so common in the northern hills of London. "Among all the harmless ones," he suggested, "there might be a wild, aggressive species from the South. A sailor could have brought one back and it escaped, or maybe a young one got loose from the Zoo, or even bred there from a vampire bat. These things happen, you know. Just ten days ago, a wolf escaped and was reportedly tracked heading this way. For a week afterward, the neighborhood children played nothing but 'Little Red Riding Hood' on the Heath and in the alleys—until this 'bloofer lady' scare started. Ever since then, it's been a massive game for them. Even this poor little guy asked the nurse if he could go out when he woke up today. When she asked him why he wanted to leave, he said he wanted to play with the 'bloofer lady.'"
"I strongly suggest," Van Helsing told him, "that when you discharge the child, you warn his parents to keep a very close eye on him. This urge to wander off is extremely dangerous. If the child were to spend another night outside, it would likely kill him. But I assume you won't be sending him home for a few days anyway?"
"Certainly not. We'll keep him for at least a week, and longer if the wound hasn't fully healed."
Our visit to the hospital took much longer than we expected, and the sun had already set by the time we left. Seeing the darkness, Van Helsing said:
"There's no rush now. It's later than I thought. Come on, let's find a place to eat before we continue on our way."
We had dinner at an inn called "Jack Straw's Castle," surrounded by a small, loudly cheerful crowd of cyclists and locals. We finally headed out around ten o'clock. It was pitch black outside, and the scattered streetlamps only seemed to deepen the darkness once you stepped past their pool of light. The Professor clearly knew the route, walking with confidence, but I was completely disoriented. The further we went, the fewer people we saw, until we were actually surprised to run into the mounted police on their usual suburban patrol. At last, we reached the cemetery wall and climbed over it. We struggled a bit in the dark, unfamiliar surroundings, but eventually, we located the Westenra family tomb. The Professor took out the key, unlocked the creaking door, and politely—though entirely unconsciously—stepped back, motioning for me to go in first. There was a grim irony in his courtly manners during such a macabre situation. I stepped inside, and he quickly followed, pulling the door shut behind us after making absolutely sure the lock operated on a falling latch, not a spring catch. If it had been a spring lock, we would have trapped ourselves. He then rummaged in his bag, pulled out a matchbox and a stub of candle, and lit it. Even during the day, decorated with fresh flowers, the tomb had looked grim enough. Now, days later, it was unimaginably wretched. The flowers were dead and drooping, their whites turning rusty and their greens fading to brown. Spiders and beetles had reclaimed the space. The candle's feeble light flickered off stained stone, dusty mortar, damp rust, tarnished brass, and clouded silver plating. The whole scene carried an overwhelming sense of decay, proving that animal life is not the only thing that rots away.
Van Helsing went to work with methodical focus. He held the candle close to read the coffin nameplates, letting the hot wax drip and harden in white patches on the metal until he was certain which one was Lucy's. He reached into his bag again and pulled out a screwdriver.
"What are you doing?" I asked.
"I am going to open the coffin. You need to see this for yourself." He immediately began unscrewing the wooden lid and finally lifted it away, revealing the inner lead casing. Watching this was almost more than I could handle. It felt like a profound violation of the dead, no different than stripping a living woman in her sleep. I actually grabbed his hand to stop him. He merely said, "You will see," and reached into his bag for a tiny fretsaw. He drove the screwdriver hard through the lead casing with a swift downward stab that made me flinch, creating a hole just big enough for the saw blade. I braced myself, expecting a rush of putrid gas from the week-old corpse. As doctors, we are trained to handle such hazards, but I instinctively backed away toward the door. The Professor, however, didn't hesitate for a second. He sawed two feet down one side of the lead, cut across, and then sawed back up the other side. Gripping the loose flap of metal, he bent it back toward the foot of the coffin. He held the candle over the opening and gestured for me to look.
I stepped closer and peered inside. The coffin was empty.
It was a massive shock to me, but Van Helsing didn't look surprised at all. If anything, it confirmed his theory, giving him the confidence to press on. "Are you satisfied now, friend John?" he asked.
Every ounce of my natural skepticism flared up as I answered:
"I am satisfied that Lucy's body is not in that coffin. But that only proves one thing."
"And what is that, friend John?"
"That it's not in there."
"That is sound logic," he replied, "as far as it goes. But how do you—how can you—explain the fact that it is not in there?"
"Perhaps it was grave robbers," I suggested. "Some of the undertaker's men might have stolen the body." I knew I was grasping at straws, but it was the only rational explanation I could offer. The Professor sighed. "Ah, well!" he said. "It seems we need more proof. Come with me."
He replaced the coffin lid, gathered all his tools and put them in his bag, blew out the candle, and packed that away too. We opened the door and stepped outside. He pulled the door shut behind us and locked it. Handing me the key, he said, "Would you like to keep this? It might put your mind at ease." I laughed—a forced, joyless laugh, I must admit—and gestured for him to hold onto it. "A key doesn't prove anything," I said. "There could be duplicates, and a lock like that is easy enough to pick anyway." He didn't argue, simply dropping the key into his pocket. He then instructed me to keep watch on one side of the cemetery while he took the other. I positioned myself behind a yew tree, watching his dark silhouette move away until the gravestones and trees swallowed him up.
It was a lonely watch. Shortly after taking my post, I heard a distant clock strike midnight, followed eventually by one and two o'clock. I was shivering, anxious, and deeply annoyed—both at the Professor for bringing me on this absurd errand, and at myself for agreeing to come. I was too cold and tired to stay properly alert, but not quite exhausted enough to fall asleep and abandon my post entirely. It was a bleak, miserable wait.
Suddenly, glancing around, I thought I caught a glimpse of something white darting between two dark yew trees on the far side of the graveyard, furthest from the tomb. At the exact same moment, a dark shape emerged from the Professor's side of the grounds and hurried toward the white flash. I started moving too, but I had to navigate around headstones and iron fences, stumbling over graves in the dark. The sky was deeply overcast, and somewhere in the distance, an early rooster crowed. A little way off, just past a line of sparse juniper trees that marked the path to the church, a dim white figure flitted toward the tomb. The tomb itself was obscured by trees, so I couldn't see where the figure actually went. But I heard the distinct rustle of movement near where I'd first spotted the white shape. When I hurried over, I found the Professor holding a tiny child in his arms. Seeing me, he held the child out and said:
"Are you satisfied now?"
"No," I replied, my tone sharper than I intended.
"Do you not see the child?"
"Yes, it's a child. But who brought it here? And is it hurt?" I asked.
"We will find out," the Professor said. Acting on instinct, we quickly left the cemetery together, with him carrying the sleeping toddler.
Once we were a safe distance away, we ducked into a small grove of trees. He struck a match, and we examined the child's neck. There wasn't a single scratch or scar.
"Was I right?" I asked, feeling a surge of triumph.
"We were just in time," the Professor said with profound relief.
We now had to figure out what to do with the child. If we took him to a police station, we'd have to explain what we were doing out all night, or at the very least, make up a story about how we found him. Ultimately, we decided to carry him to Hampstead Heath. We planned to wait until we heard a patrolman approaching, leave the boy where he was guaranteed to be spotted, and then head home as fast as possible. The plan worked perfectly. At the edge of the Heath, we heard the heavy footsteps of a policeman. We laid the child on the path and hid in the shadows, watching until the officer swung his lantern around and spotted him. Hearing his shout of surprise, we slipped away into the night. By sheer luck, we managed to catch a cab near the "Spaniards" inn and rode back into the city.
I am too wired to sleep, so I am writing this down. But I have to try and get at least a few hours of rest, as Van Helsing is coming to get me at noon. He is insisting that I accompany him on yet another expedition.
* * * * *
September 27. — It was nearly two o'clock before we found a safe window for our operation. The noon funeral had completely finished, the last lingering mourners had slowly wandered off, and, watching from behind a clump of alder trees, we saw the gravedigger lock the main gate behind him. We knew we were safe until morning if we needed to be, though the Professor assured me this wouldn't take more than an hour. Once again, the sheer reality of the situation crushed any sense of the surreal; I was acutely aware of the serious laws we were breaking with this grim task. Furthermore, it all felt so incredibly pointless. As insane as it was to open a lead coffin just to verify if a woman who had been dead for a week was actually dead, it felt like absolute madness to crack open the tomb a second time when we already knew, from our own eyes, that the coffin was empty. But I just shrugged and kept my mouth shut. Van Helsing has a way of doing exactly what he wants, regardless of anyone's objections. He took the key, unlocked the vault, and again, with flawless courtesy, gestured for me to go first. The crypt didn't feel quite as terrifying as it had in the dark, but in the harsh daylight streaming in, it looked incredibly cheap and neglected. Van Helsing marched straight to Lucy's coffin, and I followed. He leaned over and peeled back the lead casing once more. A jolt of pure shock and horror went through me.
There lay Lucy, looking exactly as she had the night before her funeral. In fact, she looked even more radiantly beautiful than ever. It was impossible to believe she was dead. Her lips were red—redder than before—and a soft, healthy flush colored her cheeks.
"Is this some kind of trick?" I demanded.
"Are you convinced now?" the Professor asked. As he spoke, he reached down—and in a move that made my skin crawl—pulled back her lifeless lips to expose her white teeth.
"Look," he urged, "look closely. They are even sharper than before. With these"—he tapped one of her prominent canines, then the tooth beneath it—"she can easily pierce a child's throat. Do you believe me now, friend John?" Once again, my stubborn need to argue kicked in. I simply couldn't accept the horrifying reality he was presenting. So, clinging to an argument that embarrassed me even as I voiced it, I said:
"Someone might have put the body back here since last night."
"Really? And who exactly would do that?"
"I don't know. But someone did."
"And yet, she has been dead for a week. Most bodies don't look like this after that much time." I had no rebuttal for this, so I stayed quiet. Van Helsing didn't seem to care about my silence; he certainly didn't gloat or act triumphant. He stared intensely at the dead woman's face, pulling back her eyelids to check her eyes, and then forcing her lips apart once more to inspect her teeth. Finally, he turned back to me and said:
"There is something happening here that defies all medical history; it's a bizarre dual existence that isn't normal death. She was bitten by the vampire while she was in a trance, sleepwalking—oh, you're shocked; you didn't know that part, friend John, but you'll understand everything soon enough. The trance made her an easy target for him to drain her blood. She died while in that trance, and so she exists in that same trance-like state now as the Un-Dead. That is why she's different from the rest. Usually, when the Un-Dead sleep in their resting places"—he swept his arm around the tomb to indicate a vampire's 'home'—"their faces reveal their true, monstrous nature. But because she was so purely sweet in life, she reverts to looking like an ordinary corpse when she sleeps. There is no evil visible on her face, and that makes it incredibly hard for me to do what I must do—kill her in her sleep." This made my blood run cold. I realized I was actually starting to believe Van Helsing's insane theories. But if she was truly just a corpse, why did the thought of 'killing' her fill me with such horror? He looked up, evidently catching the shift in my expression, and said almost joyfully:
"Ah, you believe me now?"
I replied, "Don't push me too fast. I am willing to keep an open mind. How exactly do you plan to do this gruesome work?"
"I will cut off her head, fill her mouth with garlic, and drive a wooden stake through her body." I shuddered at the thought of mutilating the body of a woman I had deeply loved. But surprisingly, the revulsion wasn't as overwhelming as I would have expected. Truth be told, I was beginning to feel a creeping dread and disgust toward this thing in the coffin—this "Un-Dead" creature, as Van Helsing called it. Does that mean love is entirely conditional, or purely based on what is in front of us?
I stood there for a long time waiting for Van Helsing to start, but he remained frozen, lost in thought. Finally, he snapped his bag shut and said:
"I have been thinking it over, and I've decided on the best course of action. If I followed my immediate instinct, I would finish the job right now. But there are complications ahead, things we don't fully understand that will be a thousand times harder to deal with. This part is easy. She hasn't killed anyone yet, though it's only a matter of time. Acting now would neutralize the danger she poses permanently. But we are going to need Arthur's help later on. How do we explain this to him? If you—who personally treated the wounds on Lucy's throat, saw the identical marks on the child at the hospital, saw an empty coffin last night and found it full today with a woman who has only grown more beautiful a week after dying, and witnessed the white phantom drop that child in the graveyard—if you still struggled to believe the evidence of your own eyes, how can we expect Arthur to believe it when he hasn't seen any of it? He already resented me for pulling him away from her final kiss when she was dying. I know he's forgiven me, assuming I had some misguided medical reason for denying him a proper goodbye. If we do this now, he might adopt the horrific idea that she was buried alive, and that we essentially murdered her through sheer incompetence. He would convince himself that our crazed theories killed the woman he loved, and it would ruin his life. The worst part is the uncertainty; he would never know the truth. He'd have nightmares about her waking up trapped in a coffin, imagining her suffering. Or worse, he might suspect we were right all along and that his beloved fiancee was actually a monster. No! I tried to warn him once, but I know so much more now. Knowing the absolute truth, I am absolutely certain he has to go through hell to find peace. That poor man has to endure a reality so dark it will shatter his world; only then can we put an end to this nightmare and give him closure. My mind is made up. Let's get out of here. You head back to your asylum for the night and make sure everything is in order. I will stay here and keep watch over this cemetery my own way. Tomorrow night at ten o'clock, come to my room at the Berkeley Hotel. I will summon Arthur and that brave American who also donated his blood. We have serious work to do later. I'll walk with you as far as Piccadilly so we can get dinner, but I need to be back here before sunset."
So we locked the tomb, climbed back over the cemetery wall—which wasn't too difficult—and caught a ride back to Piccadilly.

Dr. Seward's Diary.
September 28. — It's amazing what a solid night's sleep can do. Yesterday, I was practically ready to buy into Van Helsing's insane theories, but in the harsh light of day, they seem like utter nonsense. I have no doubt he genuinely believes it all. I'm starting to wonder if his mind has snapped. There has to be a rational explanation for all these bizarre events. Is it possible the Professor orchestrated the whole thing himself? He's a brilliant man; if he suffered a mental break, he could easily construct this elaborate scenario to support his delusions. I hate to even consider it, and honestly, the idea of Van Helsing losing his mind is almost as unbelievable as vampires existing. But I need to watch him very closely. I might figure out the real mystery here.
* * * * *
September 29, morning.... Last night, just before ten o'clock, Arthur and Quincey arrived at Van Helsing's room. He outlined his plan, directing his words entirely at Arthur, treating him as the ultimate decision-maker for the group. He started by saying he hoped we would all accompany him, "because," he explained, "there is a terrible duty we must perform. You were probably surprised by my letter?" He directed this question at Lord Godalming.
"I was. It actually upset me quite a bit. There's been so much tragedy in my life lately that I really don't need any more stress. And frankly, I've been trying to figure out what you meant by it. Quincey and I discussed it, but the more we talked, the more confused we got. Right now, I'm completely lost."
"Me too," Quincey Morris added simply.
"Ah," said the Professor, "then both of you are closer to a blank slate than friend John here, who has a lot of unlearning to do before he can even start to grasp the situation."
He had clearly picked up on my renewed skepticism without me saying a word. Turning back to Arthur and Quincey, his expression turned deadly serious.
"I need your permission to do what I believe is necessary tonight. I know it's a huge request, and you won't truly understand how massive a favor you're granting until you see what I plan to do. That's why I'm asking you to trust me blindly. You might be furious with me for a while afterward—I have to accept that possibility—but I need you to promise that you won't blame yourselves for whatever happens."
"Well, I appreciate the honesty," Quincey interrupted. "I'll vouch for the Professor. I don't entirely follow his logic yet, but I swear he's acting in good faith; and that's good enough for me."
"Thank you, sir," Van Helsing said with pride. "I am honored to count you as a trusting friend. Your support means a great deal to me." He offered his hand, and Quincey shook it.
Then Arthur spoke up:
"Dr. Van Helsing, I don't like going into things blind. If this scheme compromises my honor as a gentleman or my faith as a Christian, I simply cannot agree to it. If you can promise me that your intentions don't violate either of those principles, I'll give my consent right now. Even though, for the life of me, I have no idea what you're actually planning."
"I accept your conditions," Van Helsing said. "All I ask is that before you condemn anything I do, you carefully consider it and ensure it doesn't actually violate those boundaries you've set."
"Agreed!" Arthur said. "That's fair. Now that the negotiations are out of the way, can you please tell us what we're going to do?"
"I need you to come with me, in absolute secrecy, to the cemetery in Kingstead."
Arthur's expression darkened in shock.
"Where my poor Lucy is buried?" The Professor nodded. Arthur continued: "And what will we do there?"
"We will enter her tomb!" Arthur leaped to his feet.
"Professor, are you being serious, or is this some kind of sick joke? Forgive me—I can see you are serious." He sat back down, but he held himself rigidly, fully armored in his aristocratic dignity. There was a tense silence before he asked again:
"And once we are inside the tomb?"
"We will open the coffin."
"This is too much!" Arthur shouted, jumping up again, his face red with anger. "I am willing to be patient with anything reasonable, but this—this desecration of her grave—of a woman who—" He choked on his own outrage. The Professor looked at him with profound pity.
"My poor friend, if I could spare you a single moment of this pain," he said softly, "God knows I would. But tonight, we must walk a path of thorns; otherwise, the feet of the woman you love will walk through Hell forever!"
Arthur looked at him, his face pale and tense, and warned:
"Watch what you say, sir! Watch what you say!"
"Wouldn't it be better to just hear me out?" Van Helsing asked calmly. "At least then you will know exactly what I am proposing. May I continue?"
"Let him speak," Morris interjected.
After a long pause, Van Helsing continued, clearly choosing his words carefully:
"Miss Lucy is dead, isn't she? Yes! Therefore, nothing we do can actually hurt her. But if she is not entirely dead—"
Arthur bolted upright again.
"My God!" he cried out. "What are you saying? Was there a mistake? Was she buried alive?" He let out a groan of pure agony that didn't even carry a sliver of hope.
"I didn't say she was alive, my boy; I don't believe that for a second. I am only suggesting that she might be Un-Dead."
"Un-Dead? Not alive? What does that even mean? Is this all just a nightmare?"
"There are mysteries in this world that humans can only guess at, secrets we barely begin to unravel over centuries. Trust me, we are standing on the edge of one right now. But I haven't finished. I need to ask: may I sever the head of the deceased Miss Lucy?"
"Absolutely not!" Arthur roared in a sudden burst of rage. "I wouldn't consent to mutilating her corpse for all the money in the world! Dr. Van Helsing, you are pushing me way too far. What did I ever do to you that you would torture me like this? What did that poor, sweet girl do to deserve such disrespect in death? Are you insane for suggesting these things, or am I insane for standing here listening? Don't you dare think another thought about defiling her grave. I absolutely refuse to give you permission. It is my duty to protect her resting place from vandals, and by God, I will defend it!"
Van Helsing slowly stood up, looking grave and commanding.
"Lord Godalming, I also have a duty. A duty to others, a duty to you, and a duty to the dead. And by God, I will fulfill it! All I'm asking right now is that you come with me, watch, and listen. If, after you see the truth, you don't demand that I finish the job with even more urgency than I feel now, then I will carry out my duty regardless of your feelings. And afterward, out of respect for your position, I will surrender myself to the law and answer for my actions whenever and wherever you choose." His voice cracked slightly, and he finished with deep sympathy:
"But please, don't leave here furious with me. In a long life filled with difficult, heartbreaking decisions, I have never faced a task as devastating as this one. Believe me, if the moment comes when you finally understand why I'm doing this, a single forgiving look from you will erase all the pain of this argument. I am doing everything in my power to save you from a lifetime of sorrow. Just think about it. Why would I put myself through all this exhaustion and grief? I traveled all the way from my home country simply to help. First, to do a favor for my friend John, and then to try and save a sweet young woman whom I grew to love. For her—and it embarrasses me to admit this, but I say it purely out of affection—I gave exactly what you gave: the blood from my own veins. I gave it freely, and I wasn't her fiance like you; I was just her doctor and her friend. I dedicated my days and nights to her—both before and after she died. And if sacrificing my own life right now would save her soul from being this dead Un-Dead creature, I would gladly lay down my life for her." He spoke with quiet, dignified pride, and Arthur was visibly moved. He took the old man's hand and said in a choked voice:
"It is agonizing to think about, and I honestly don't understand any of it. But at the very least, I will come with you and wait to see what happens."
