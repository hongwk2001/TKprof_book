CHAPTER VII

CUTTING FROM “THE DAILYGRAPH,” 8 AUGUST

(Pasted in Mina Murray’s Journal.)

From a Correspondent.

Whitby.

One of the greatest and most sudden storms on record has just struck Whitby, a coastal town in Yorkshire, bringing strange and highly unusual consequences. The weather had been somewhat muggy, but not out of the ordinary for August. Saturday evening was as clear as anyone could remember, and countless vacationers had planned trips yesterday to Mulgrave Woods, Robin Hood’s Bay, Rig Mill, Runswick, Staithes, and various other spots around Whitby. The steamboats Emma and Scarborough traveled up and down the coast, and there was an unusually high number of tourists moving to and from the town. The day remained remarkably pleasant until the afternoon, when some of the local observers who regularly gather at the East Cliff churchyard—a high vantage point overlooking the vast stretch of sea to the north and east—pointed out a sudden formation of cirrus clouds, known as "mares'-tails," high in the northwestern sky, signaling incoming bad weather. The wind was blowing gently from the southwest, classified in meteorological terms as a "light breeze." The coastguard on duty immediately filed a report, and an elderly fisherman, who had spent over fifty years watching the weather from the East Cliff, confidently predicted that a sudden storm was coming. The approaching sunset was incredibly beautiful, forming grand clusters of brilliantly colored clouds, which drew quite a crowd along the cliffside path in the old churchyard to admire the view. Before the sun dipped below the dark, imposing headland of Kettleness on the western horizon, its descent painted the sky with countless clouds in every color of sunset—flame, purple, pink, green, violet, and gold. Mixed in were small but intensely black masses of clouds, distinctly outlined like massive silhouettes in various shapes. This striking scene did not go unnoticed by painters, and it is highly likely that some sketches of the "Prelude to the Great Storm" will be displayed at the Royal Academy and Royal Institute exhibitions next May. Several captains quickly decided that their "cobles" or "mules"—local terms for different types of small fishing boats—would stay safely in the harbor until the storm had passed. The wind completely died down during the evening, and by midnight, there was a suffocating calm, a stifling heat, and an intense atmospheric pressure that often affects sensitive individuals before a thunderstorm. Very few lights were visible at sea, as even the coastal steamboats, which usually hug the shoreline closely, kept far out into open water, and barely any fishing boats were in sight. The only visible vessel was a foreign schooner with all its sails fully set, apparently heading west. The recklessness or ignorance of its crew became a major topic of conversation as long as the ship was visible, and people tried to signal them to lower their sails in the face of such danger. Before darkness completely fell, the ship was seen rolling gently on the ocean's swelling waves, its sails flapping uselessly.

"As idle as a painted ship upon a painted ocean."

Shortly before ten o'clock, the stillness in the air became overwhelming. The silence was so deep that the bleating of a sheep inland or the barking of a dog in town could be heard clearly. The band playing lively French music on the pier sounded like a jarring note against the immense silence of nature. A little after midnight, a strange sound echoed from across the sea, and a bizarre, faint, hollow roaring began to travel high through the air overhead.

Then, without warning, the storm broke. The entire face of nature convulsed instantly, with a speed that seemed unbelievable at the time and impossible to fully grasp afterward. The waves surged with growing fury, each one climbing higher than the last, until the sea, which had been as smooth as glass just minutes earlier, turned into a roaring, devouring monster. White-crested waves battered the flat sands madly and rushed up the sloping cliffs. Other waves crashed over the piers, sweeping ocean spray completely over the lanterns of the lighthouses that stand at the ends of Whitby Harbor's twin piers. The wind roared like thunder, blowing with such immense force that even strong men struggled to stay on their feet, clinging desperately to iron railings. The piers had to be completely cleared of onlookers, otherwise the death toll that night would have been far higher. Adding to the danger and chaos, thick masses of sea fog drifted inland. These cold, wet, white clouds swept past like ghosts, so clammy and damp that it took little imagination to feel as though the spirits of those lost at sea were touching the living with the damp hands of death. Many shuddered as the swirling trails of mist rolled over them. Occasionally, the fog cleared, and the sea could be seen for some distance illuminated by the lightning, which was now flashing relentlessly. This was followed by such sudden, explosive peals of thunder that the entire sky above seemed to tremble beneath the heavy footsteps of the storm.

Some of the scenes revealed by the lightning were immeasurably grand and deeply mesmerizing. The ocean, swelling as high as mountains, violently hurled massive plumes of white foam into the sky with every crashing wave, which the storm then seemed to snatch and scatter into the wind. Here and there, a fishing boat with torn, ragged sails fled frantically toward shelter from the gale, while occasionally the white wings of a storm-battered seabird flashed in the dark. At the top of the East Cliff, the new searchlight was set up for testing but had not yet been used. The officers in charge quickly brought it into operation, sweeping its beam across the surface of the sea during brief gaps in the rolling mist. Its assistance proved incredibly effective a couple of times; in one instance, a fishing boat with its deck mostly underwater charged into the harbor and managed to avoid crashing into the piers by following the guiding light. As each boat reached the safety of the port, the massive crowd on the shore let out a roar of joy, a shout that briefly seemed to pierce the roaring gale before being swallowed up by the wind.

Before long, the searchlight spotted a schooner in the distance with all its sails set—apparently the same ship that had been seen earlier in the evening. By this time, the wind had shifted to the east, and a collective shudder ran through the spectators on the cliff as they realized the terrible danger the vessel was in. Between the ship and the safety of the port lay a vast, flat reef where many fine ships had been wrecked in the past. With the wind blowing from its current direction, it seemed completely impossible for the ship to reach the harbor entrance. It was near high tide, but the waves were so enormous that the shallow seabed was almost visible in the deep troughs between them. The schooner, its sails fully billowing, was rushing forward at such a terrifying speed that, as one old sailor put it, "she must crash into something, even if it's hell itself." Then came another wave of sea fog, thicker than any before it—a dense, clammy mist that enveloped everything like a gray shroud. It left people relying solely on their hearing, as the roar of the storm, the crashing thunder, and the booming of the massive waves echoed through the damp darkness even louder than before. The searchlight beam remained fixed on the harbor entrance just past the East Pier, where the collision was expected, and the crowd waited breathlessly. Suddenly, the wind shifted to the northeast, blowing away the last remnants of the sea fog. And then, miraculously, the strange schooner swept between the piers. Bounding from wave to wave at breakneck speed, driven hard by the gale with all sails set, it successfully reached the safety of the harbor. The searchlight tracked the ship, and a wave of horror swept over everyone who saw it: tied to the ship's steering wheel was a dead body. Its head slumped forward, swinging grotesquely back and forth with every violent lurch of the vessel. No one else was visible anywhere on the deck. A profound sense of awe fell over the crowd as they realized that the ship had somehow found the harbor by a miracle, guided only by the hands of a dead man! However, all of this happened much faster than it takes to describe it. The schooner didn't slow down; it sped across the harbor and violently ran aground on a large bank of sand and gravel. This bank, built up by countless tides and storms, sat in the southeast corner of the pier jutting out below the East Cliff, an area known locally as Tate Hill Pier.

There was, of course, a massive crash as the ship rammed into the sandbank. Every mast, rope, and rigging was stretched to the limit, and some of the heavy gear from the upper masts came crashing down. But the strangest thing of all happened the very second the ship hit the shore: a huge dog suddenly leapt up onto the deck from below, as if launched by the impact, ran forward, and jumped from the bow onto the sand. It made straight for the steep cliff where the churchyard hangs so precariously over the lane leading to the East Pier that some of the flat tombstones—known locally as "through-stones"—actually stick out over the edge where the cliff has eroded. The dog disappeared into the dense darkness just beyond the reach of the searchlight's beam.

As it happened, the Tate Hill Pier was completely empty at that moment, as everyone who lived nearby was either in bed or had gone up to the higher ground to watch the storm. Therefore, the coastguard on duty on the eastern side of the harbor, who immediately ran down to the small pier, was the first person to board the ship. The men operating the searchlight, after sweeping the harbor entrance and seeing nothing, aimed the beam at the abandoned ship and kept it there. The coastguard ran toward the back of the ship, and when he reached the steering wheel, he leaned over to inspect it—only to jump back instantly as if struck by a sudden shock. This sparked the crowd's curiosity, and a large number of people started running toward the pier. It is a fairly long way around from the West Cliff, over the drawbridge, and down to Tate Hill Pier, but as your correspondent is a decent runner, I arrived well ahead of the crowd. However, by the time I got there, a crowd had already formed on the pier, and the coastguard and police were refusing to let anyone on board. Thanks to the chief boatman's courtesy, I was allowed onto the deck in my capacity as a reporter. I was part of a small group who saw the dead sailor while he was still physically tied to the wheel.

It was no wonder the coastguard had been startled and even terrified, for it was a sight rarely seen. The man's hands were stacked one on top of the other and tied securely to one of the wheel's spokes. Tucked between his inner hand and the wood was a crucifix, and the rosary beads it was attached to were wrapped around both his wrists and the wheel, firmly held in place by the binding ropes. The poor man might have been sitting down initially, but the violent flapping of the sails and the jerking of the rudder had violently dragged him back and forth. As a result, the ropes binding him had sliced through his flesh right down to the bone. The exact state of the scene was carefully documented, and Dr. J. M. Caffyn, a surgeon from 33 East Elliot Place who arrived shortly after I did, examined the body and declared that the man had been dead for at least two days. Inside his pocket was a tightly corked bottle, empty except for a small roll of paper that turned out to be an addition to the ship's log. The coastguard noted that the man must have tied his own hands and tightened the knots with his teeth. The fact that a coastguard was the first to board might prevent some legal complications in the Admiralty Court later on, as coastguards cannot claim the salvage rights that normally belong to the first civilian to board an abandoned ship. Even so, the legal debates have already begun; one loud young law student is arguing that the owner's rights have been completely forfeited. He claims that because the tiller—a symbol, if not actual proof, of delegated possession—is held by a dead hand, it violates the statutes of mortmain (laws restricting property ownership by the dead). Needless to say, the dead steersman was respectfully untied from the post where he had honorably stood watch until the very end—showing a steadfast loyalty as noble as that of the young hero Casabianca—and taken to the mortuary to await an inquest.

The sudden storm is already passing, and its intensity is fading. The crowds are heading home, and the sky is beginning to glow red over the Yorkshire hills. I will send further details about the abandoned ship that miraculously found its way into the harbor in time for your next issue.

Whitby

August 9.—The aftermath of the derelict ship's strange arrival during last night's storm is even more shocking than the event itself. It turns out the schooner is a Russian vessel from Varna, named the Demeter. Its cargo mostly consists of silver sand used as ballast, along with a small shipment: several large wooden boxes filled with earth. This cargo was addressed to a Whitby solicitor, Mr. S. F. Billington of 7 The Crescent, who boarded the ship this morning and officially took possession of the goods. The Russian consul, acting on behalf of the shipping charter, also took formal possession of the vessel and paid all harbor fees. The bizarre coincidence is the only thing anyone in town is talking about today. Officials from the Board of Trade have been extremely strict, ensuring that every single regulation has been meticulously followed. Since this event is bound to be a major sensation for a few days, they are clearly determined to leave no room for future complaints. The dog that leaped ashore when the ship crashed has also drawn a lot of interest, and quite a few members of the local Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals (which has a strong presence in Whitby) have tried to find and help the animal. Unfortunately, to everyone's disappointment, the dog is nowhere to be found and seems to have vanished from the town entirely. It might have been terrified and fled to the moors, where it is still hiding in fear. Some people are worried about this, fearing that the beast—which is clearly vicious—might become a danger itself. Early this morning, a large dog, a mastiff mix belonging to a coal merchant near Tate Hill Pier, was found dead in the street right across from its owner's yard. It had clearly been in a savage fight, as its throat was ripped out and its stomach was sliced open, seemingly by a brutal claw.

*       *       *       *       *

Later.—Thanks to the Board of Trade inspector's kindness, I was allowed to read the Demeter's logbook. It was kept properly up until three days ago but contained nothing especially interesting, aside from the facts regarding the missing crewmen. The most fascinating piece of evidence, however, is the paper found inside the bottle, which was presented at the inquest today. Together, the log and the paper tell a story stranger than anything I have ever encountered. Since there is no reason to hide the contents, I have been given permission to share them. Therefore, I am sending you a transcript, leaving out only the technical maritime and cargo details. It almost appears as though the captain was struck by some sort of madness before the ship even reached the open ocean, and that this insanity worsened steadily throughout the trip. Of course, my report must be taken with a grain of salt, as I am writing from the dictation of a clerk from the Russian consulate who rushed to translate it for me.

LOG OF THE “DEMETER.”

Varna to Whitby.

Written 18 July. So many strange things have been happening that I will keep an accurate record from now until we reach land.

*       *       *       *       *

On 6 July, we finished loading our cargo, which consisted of silver sand and wooden boxes filled with earth. We set sail at noon with a fresh east wind. The crew consists of five deckhands, two mates, the cook, and myself (the captain), making nine in total.

*       *       *       *       *

On 11 July, at dawn, we entered the Bosphorus Strait. We were boarded by Turkish customs officers. Handed out backsheesh (bribes). Everything was in order. We resumed sailing at 4 p.m.

*       *       *       *       *

On 12 July, we passed through the Dardanelles. More customs officers and a guard squadron's flagboat stopped us. Handed out more backsheesh. The officers were thorough but quick; they wanted us on our way soon. By nightfall, we had entered the Aegean Sea.

*       *       *       *       *

On 13 July, we passed Cape Matapan. The crew seemed dissatisfied about something. They looked scared but wouldn't say what was bothering them.

*       *       *       *       *

On 14 July, I felt a bit anxious about the crew. They are all reliable men who have sailed with me before. The first mate couldn't figure out what was wrong; they just told him that "something" was on board and crossed themselves. The mate lost his temper and struck one of them today. I expected a fierce fight, but everyone remained quiet.

*       *       *       *       *

On 16 July, the mate reported in the morning that one of the crewmen, Petrofsky, was missing. Nobody could explain it. He had taken the portside watch at midnight and was relieved by Abramoff, but he never went back to his bunk. The men are more depressed than ever. They all said they had expected something like this to happen, but they still wouldn't say anything other than that "something" was on board. The mate is growing very impatient with them; I am afraid there might be trouble ahead.

*       *       *       *       *

On 17 July (yesterday), one of the men, Olgaren, came to my cabin and confided in me, in a terrified voice, that he thought there was a strange man aboard the ship. He said that during his watch, he had taken shelter behind the deckhouse to avoid a heavy rainstorm. From there, he saw a tall, thin man—unlike any of the crew—come up the stairway, walk forward along the deck, and disappear. He followed cautiously, but when he reached the bow, he found no one, and all the hatchways were firmly closed. He was in a panic of superstitious dread, and I fear this panic might spread. To calm everyone down, I plan to thoroughly search the entire ship from front to back today.

*       *       *       *       *

Later today, I gathered the entire crew and told them that since they clearly believed someone was hiding on the ship, we would search it from stem to stern. The first mate was furious; he called it foolish and argued that giving in to such ridiculous ideas would ruin the men's discipline. He claimed he could keep them out of trouble with a heavy iron bar. I let him take the steering wheel while the rest of us formed a line with lanterns and began a thorough search. We didn't leave a single corner unsearched. Since the cargo hold only contained those large wooden boxes, there were no hidden spots where a man could conceal himself. The men were visibly relieved when the search ended and cheerfully returned to work. The first mate glared but didn't say a word.

*       *       *       *       *

22 July.—The weather has been rough for the last three days, and all hands have been busy adjusting the sails—there's been no time to be afraid. The men seem to have forgotten their fears. The mate is in a good mood again, and everyone is getting along. I praised the men for their hard work during the bad weather. We passed Gibraltar and sailed out through the straits. Everything is fine.

*       *       *       *       *

24 July.—It feels like this ship is cursed. We were already short one man, and just as we were entering the Bay of Biscay with fierce storms ahead, another man vanished last night. Like the first, he finished his watch and was never seen again. The men are in an absolute panic. They sent a petition asking to double the watch because they are too terrified to be alone. The mate is furious. I'm worried there will be trouble, as either he or the men might resort to violence.

*       *       *       *       *

28 July.—Four days in hell, tossed around in a churning sea with gale-force winds. Nobody has slept. The men are completely exhausted. I hardly know how to assign watches, since no one is in any condition to work. The second mate volunteered to steer and keep watch so the men could grab a few hours of sleep. The wind is starting to die down, but the waves are still terrifying; however, the ship is handling them better, so we feel them less.

*       *       *       *       *

29 July.—Another tragedy. We had to rely on single watches tonight because the crew is too exhausted to double up. When the morning watch came on deck, no one was there except the steersman. We shouted and everyone rushed up. We conducted a thorough search but found no one. We are now without our second mate, and the crew is completely panicked. The first mate and I agreed to carry weapons from now on and wait for any clue as to what is causing this.

*       *       *       *       *

30 July.—Last night. We rejoiced because we were getting close to England. The weather was fine, and all sails were set. I went to bed completely worn out and slept deeply, only to be woken by the mate, who told me that both the watchman and the steersman had vanished. Now there is only myself, the mate, and two deckhands left to sail the ship.

*       *       *       *       *

1 August.—Two straight days of fog, and we haven't seen a single ship. I had hoped that once we reached the English Channel, we could signal for help or pull into a port somewhere. Since we don't have enough manpower to operate the sails, we just have to let the wind carry us. We don't dare lower the sails because we wouldn't be able to raise them again. We seem to be drifting toward some terrible doom. The mate is now more mentally broken than either of the remaining deckhands. His strong personality seems to have turned inward and destroyed him. The men have moved past fear and are working with quiet, stubborn patience, having accepted the worst. They are Russian, while he is Romanian.

*       *       *       *       *

2 August, midnight.—I woke up from a few minutes of sleep after hearing what sounded like a cry outside my porthole. The fog was too thick to see anything. I rushed up to the deck and bumped into the mate. He told me he also heard the cry and ran over, but there was no sign of the man on watch. Another one gone. Lord, help us! The mate said we must have passed the Strait of Dover because, during a brief moment when the fog lifted, he spotted the North Foreland just as he heard the man scream. If that's true, we are now out in the North Sea, and only God can guide us through this fog, which seems to follow us. But it feels like God has abandoned us.

*       *       *       *       *

3 August.—At midnight, I went to relieve the man at the wheel, but when I got there, it was empty. The wind was steady, and since we were sailing directly before it, the ship wasn't drifting off course. I couldn't leave the wheel, so I shouted for the mate. A few seconds later, he rushed onto the deck in his underwear. His eyes were wild, his face was gaunt, and I'm deeply afraid he has lost his mind. He leaned in close and whispered hoarsely into my ear, as if terrified the air itself might hear him: "It is here. I know it now. During my watch last night, I saw It—like a man, tall and thin, and deathly pale. It was at the front of the ship, looking out. I sneaked up behind It and stabbed It with my knife; but the blade went right through It, as if it were empty air." As he spoke, he drew his knife and stabbed savagely at the empty space around him. Then he continued: "But It is here, and I will find It. It's in the cargo hold, probably in one of those boxes. I'm going to unscrew them one by one to check. You stay here and steer." With a warning look and his finger pressed to his lips, he went below deck. A choppy wind was picking up, and I couldn't leave the wheel. I saw him come back on deck with a toolbox and a lantern before heading down the front hatchway. He is stark, raving mad, and there is no point in me trying to stop him. He can't do any damage to those massive boxes; the shipping invoice says they contain "clay," so tearing them apart is the most harmless thing he could do. So I will stay here, mind the wheel, and write these notes. All I can do is trust in God and wait for the fog to clear. Then, if I can't steer to a harbor with this wind, I will cut down the sails, stop the ship, and signal for help....

*       *       *       *       *

It's nearly all over now. Just as I started hoping the mate would emerge calmer—I could hear him smashing away at something in the hold, and physical work is good for him—a sudden, terrified scream echoed up the hatchway, making my blood run cold. He shot up onto the deck as if fired from a cannon—a raging lunatic, his eyes rolling wildly and his face twisted in absolute terror. "Save me! Save me!" he screamed, looking frantically into the thick blanket of fog. His horror quickly faded into despair, and with a disturbingly steady voice, he said: "You had better come too, Captain, before it is too late. He is down there. I know the secret now. The sea will save me from Him, and it is my only option left!" Before I could say a word or step forward to grab him, he vaulted onto the railing and deliberately threw himself into the ocean. I suppose I know the secret now, too. It was this madman who murdered the men one by one, and now he has killed himself. God help me! How am I ever going to explain all these horrors when I finally reach port? If I ever reach port! Will that day ever come?

*       *       *       *       *

4 August.—The fog is still so thick the sunrise cannot cut through it. I only know the sun has risen because I am a sailor; I don't know how else to explain it. I didn't dare go below deck, and I couldn't leave the wheel, so I stayed here all night. And in the darkness of the night, I saw It—Him! God forgive me, but the mate was right to jump overboard. It is better to die like a man; no one can blame a sailor for choosing to die in the open ocean. But I am the captain, and I cannot abandon my ship. However, I will thwart this fiend, this monster. When my strength starts to give out, I will tie my hands to the wheel, and along with them, I will bind the one thing that He—It!—does not dare to touch. And then, whether the wind is good or foul, I will save my soul and my honor as a captain. I am growing weaker, and night is falling. If He looks me in the face again, I might not have time to do what must be done.... If we wreck, maybe someone will find this bottle and understand. If not... well, then at least everyone will know that I stayed true to my duty. May God, the Blessed Virgin, and the saints help a poor, ignorant soul who is only trying to do his duty....


The verdict was, of course, inconclusive. There is no evidence to present, and with no one left to testify, it is impossible to know whether the man committed the murders himself. The locals here almost unanimously consider the captain a hero, and he will be given a public funeral. It has already been arranged that his body will be taken by a procession of boats a short way up the River Esk, then brought back to Tate Hill Pier and carried up the abbey steps to be buried in the cliffside churchyard. The owners of over a hundred boats have already signed up, wishing to follow him to his grave.

No trace of the giant dog has ever been found, which has caused much sadness. Given the current public sentiment, I believe the entire town would have adopted it. Tomorrow is the funeral, which will bring this latest "mystery of the sea" to a close.

Mina Murray's Journal.

August 8.—Lucy was very restless all night, and I couldn't sleep either. The storm was terrifying, and the loud booming sound it made as it blew through the chimneys made me shudder. Whenever a strong gust hit, it sounded like a distant cannon firing. Strangely enough, Lucy didn't wake up, but she got out of bed twice and dressed herself. Fortunately, I woke up in time on both occasions, managed to undress her without waking her, and got her back into bed. This sleepwalking is a very strange thing; as soon as her intent is physically blocked, whatever motivation she had disappears, and she falls right back into her normal routine.

Early in the morning, we both got up and walked down to the harbor to see if anything had happened during the night. Very few people were out. Though the sun was bright and the air clear and crisp, the massive, menacing waves—which looked dark because the foam capping them was as white as snow—were violently pushing their way through the narrow harbor entrance, like a bully forcing his way through a crowd. Somehow, I felt relieved that Jonathan was on land last night rather than at sea. But, oh, is he on land or at sea? Where is he, and how is he doing? I am growing terribly anxious about him. If only I knew what to do, and could actually do something!

*       *       *       *       *

August 10.—The funeral for the poor sea captain today was deeply touching. It seemed like every boat in the harbor was there, and a group of captains carried the coffin all the way from Tate Hill Pier up to the churchyard. Lucy came with me, and we went early to our usual seat while the boat procession traveled up the river to the Viaduct and back down. We had a wonderful view and watched the procession for nearly the entire route. The poor man was laid to rest very close to our seat, so when the time came, we stood on the bench and saw everything. Poor Lucy seemed deeply upset. She was restless and uneasy the entire time, and I can't help but think her intense dreaming at night is taking a toll on her. She is quite strange about one thing: she refuses to admit to me that there is any reason for her restlessness, or if there is, she doesn't understand it herself. There is another reason for her distress: poor old Mr. Swales was found dead this morning right on our favorite seat, with a broken neck. As the doctor said, he clearly fell backward in the seat out of sheer fright, because the men who found him said the look of absolute horror on his face made them shudder. Poor, dear old man! Perhaps he saw Death coming for him with his dying eyes! Lucy is so sweet and sensitive that she feels these things much more sharply than others do. Just a moment ago, she was quite shaken by a small incident that I didn't pay much attention to, even though I love animals myself. One of the men who often comes up here to watch the boats was followed by his dog. The dog is always with him; they are both quiet, and I had never seen the man angry or heard the dog bark. During the funeral service, the dog refused to go to its master, who was sitting on the seat with us. Instead, it stayed a few yards away, barking and howling. Its master spoke to it gently, then harshly, and finally angrily, but it wouldn't come closer or stop making noise. It was in a complete frenzy, its eyes wild, and all its fur standing on end like a cat preparing to fight. Eventually, the man lost his temper, jumped down, kicked the dog, grabbed it by the scruff of the neck, and half-dragged, half-threw it onto the tombstone where our seat is mounted. The moment the poor thing touched the stone, it went completely quiet and began to tremble violently. It didn't try to run away; it just crouched there, quivering and cowering in such a pitiful state of terror that I tried to comfort it, though it didn't help. Lucy was full of pity too, but she didn't try to touch the dog; she just looked at it with an expression of pure agony. I seriously worry that she is too hyper-sensitive to navigate this world without suffering. I'm certain she will dream about this tonight. The whole combination of events—the ship steered into port by a dead man tied to the wheel with a crucifix and rosary, the emotional funeral, and the dog swinging from fury to sheer terror—will undoubtedly fuel her dreams.

I think it would be best for her to go to bed completely exhausted physically, so I plan to take her on a long walk along the cliffs to Robin Hood's Bay and back. She shouldn't have much energy left for sleepwalking after that.
