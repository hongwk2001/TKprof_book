Chapter 14

Mina Harker's Journal

September 23.?Jonathan is feeling better after a difficult night. I am relieved that he has plenty of work to do, as it helps take his mind off the terrible things. I am also deeply glad that he is no longer burdened by the stress of his new position as partner. I knew he would stay true to himself, and I am so proud to see my Jonathan stepping up to his promotion and handling all his new duties perfectly. He will be out all day until late, as he mentioned he could not come home for lunch. My household chores are done, so I plan to take the journal he wrote in Transylvania, lock myself in my room, and read it....

September 24.?I could not bring myself to write last night; Jonathan's terrifying account shook me too deeply. My poor, dear husband! How much he must have suffered, whether those events were real or just his imagination. I wonder if there is any truth to it at all. Did he catch a severe stress-induced fever and write all those awful things, or did something actually cause it? I suppose I will never know, for I do not dare bring it up to him.... But what about the man we saw yesterday! Jonathan seemed so sure it was him.... Poor man! I guess Mr. Hawkins's funeral upset him and triggered some past trauma.... He genuinely believes it all. I remember what he said on our wedding day: "Unless some solemn duty comes upon me to go back to those bitter hours, asleep or awake, mad or sane." There seems to be a consistent thread running through it all.... That terrifying Count was coming to London.... If it is true, and he has arrived in London with his masses.... There might be a solemn duty awaiting us, and if it comes, we must not run from it.... I will be ready. I will get my typewriter right now and begin transcribing his journal. Then we will be prepared for others to read it if necessary. And if it is needed, my preparation might spare poor Jonathan from getting upset, because I can speak on his behalf and ensure he is never troubled or worried by it again. If Jonathan ever fully recovers from his nervous condition, he might want to tell me everything. Then I can ask him questions, understand what happened, and figure out how to comfort him.

Letter from Van Helsing to Mrs. Harker.

"September 24.

(Confidential)

"Dear Madam,?

"Please forgive me for writing to you, taking advantage of the fact that we are acquainted through my sending you the sad news of Miss Lucy Westenra's death. Thanks to the kindness of Lord Godalming, I have been given permission to read her letters and papers, because I am deeply concerned about certain highly important matters. Among them, I found some letters from you that show what great friends you were and how much you loved her. Oh, Madam Mina, I beg you, in the name of that love, please help me. I ask this for the good of others?to correct a grave wrong and to relieve many terrible troubles that may be far worse than you can imagine. Would it be possible for me to see you? You can trust me. I am a friend of Dr. John Seward and of Lord Godalming (who was Arthur to Miss Lucy). For now, I must keep this matter a secret from everyone. I would come to Exeter to see you immediately if you grant me permission and tell me when and where. I beg your pardon, madam. Having read your letters to poor Lucy, I know how kind you are and how much your husband is suffering. Therefore, I pray you, if possible, do not tell him about this, lest it cause him harm. Again, I ask your pardon and forgiveness.

"Van Helsing."

Telegram from Mrs. Harker to Van Helsing.

Mina Harker's Journal.

September 25.?I cannot help feeling terribly excited as the time draws near for Dr. Van Helsing's visit. Somehow I expect he will shed some light on Jonathan's sad experience. Also, since he attended to poor, dear Lucy during her final illness, he can tell me all about her. That must be why he is coming; it is about Lucy and her sleepwalking, not Jonathan. Then I guess I will never know the real truth now! How silly I am. That awful journal has taken hold of my imagination and is coloring everything with its own dark hue. Of course he is coming about Lucy. Her sleepwalking habit returned, and that awful night on the cliff must have made her sick. I had been so wrapped up in my own affairs that I almost forgot how ill she was afterward. She must have told him about her sleepwalking adventure on the cliff and that I knew all about it. Now he wants me to tell him what I know so that he can understand it better. I hope I did the right thing by not mentioning it to Mrs. Westenra. I could never forgive myself if any action of mine?even keeping silent?brought harm to poor, dear Lucy. I also hope Dr. Van Helsing will not blame me; I have had so much trouble and anxiety lately that I feel I cannot handle any more right now.

I suppose a good cry helps us all sometimes?it clears the air the way rain does. Perhaps reading Jonathan's journal yesterday upset me, and then this morning Jonathan left to be away for a whole day and night. It is the first time we have been apart since our marriage. I just hope my dear husband will take care of himself and that nothing will happen to upset him. It is two o'clock, and the doctor will be here soon. I will not mention Jonathan's journal unless he asks me. I am so glad I typed out my own journal; if he asks about Lucy, I can simply hand it to him. It will save a lot of questioning.

* * * * *

Later.?He came and went. Oh, what a strange meeting, and how it has made my head spin! I feel like I am in a dream. Could all of this, or even a part of it, possibly be real? If I had not read Jonathan's journal first, I never would have accepted even the possibility. Poor, poor, dear Jonathan! How much he must have suffered. Please, God, let this not upset him again. I will try to shield him from it, but perhaps it might actually comfort and help him?as terrible and awful as the consequences may be?to know for certain that his eyes, ears, and mind did not deceive him, and that it is all true. Maybe the doubt is what haunts him the most. If that doubt is removed, whether it proves he was awake or dreaming, he might feel more satisfied and better equipped to handle the shock. Dr. Van Helsing must be both a good and clever man if Arthur and Dr. Seward sent for him all the way from Holland to look after Lucy. Just from seeing him, I feel he is kind, good, and of a noble nature. When he returns tomorrow, I will ask him about Jonathan. Then, God willing, all this sorrow and anxiety might lead to a good resolution. I used to think I wanted to practice interviewing; Jonathan's friend from "The Exeter News" told him that memory is everything in such work. He said you must be able to record almost exactly every word spoken, even if you have to polish it later. This was a rare interview opportunity, and I will try to record it word for word.

It was half-past two when the knock came. I gathered all my courage and waited. A few minutes later, Mary opened the door and announced, "Dr. Van Helsing."

I stood up and bowed, and he walked toward me. He is a man of medium build but very sturdy, with his shoulders squared over a broad, deep chest. His neck is thick and perfectly balances his well-proportioned head. The way he holds his head immediately strikes you as a sign of thought and power. His head is noble and well-sized, broad and large behind the ears. His clean-shaven face features a strong, square chin, a large, determined, and expressive mouth, and a good-sized, fairly straight nose with quick, sensitive nostrils that seem to widen when his thick, bushy eyebrows furrow and his mouth tightens. His forehead is broad and fine; it rises almost straight up at first before sloping back over two prominent ridges that are set wide apart. It is the kind of forehead that forces his reddish hair to fall naturally back and to the sides, never tumbling over it. He has large, dark blue eyes that are set far apart; they can quickly shift from tender to stern depending on his mood. He said to me:?

"Mrs. Harker, is it not?" I nodded in agreement.

"You were Miss Mina Murray?" I agreed again.

"It is Mina Murray I came to see, the friend of that poor, dear child Lucy Westenra. Madam Mina, I have come on behalf of the dead."

"Sir," I replied, "you could have no better claim to my time than the fact that you were a friend and helper to Lucy Westenra." I held out my hand. He took it and said gently:?

"Oh, Madam Mina, I knew the friend of that poor, innocent girl must be good, but I have yet to learn??" He finished his sentence with a polite bow. I asked him what he wanted to see me about, and he got straight to the point:?

"I have read your letters to Miss Lucy. Forgive me, but I had to start inquiring somewhere, and there was no one else to ask. I know you were with her in Whitby. She occasionally kept a diary?you need not look surprised, Madam Mina. She started it after you left, imitating your habit. In that diary, she deduces certain details about a sleepwalking incident where she notes that you saved her. I come to you in great confusion and ask you, out of your great kindness, to tell me everything you can remember about it."

"I believe I can tell you all about it, Dr. Van Helsing."

"Ah, so you have a good memory for facts and details? That is not always the case with young ladies."

"No, Doctor, but I wrote it all down when it happened. I can show it to you if you like."

"Oh, Madam Mina, I would be very grateful. You would be doing me a huge favor." I couldn't resist the temptation to puzzle him a bit?I suppose a bit of mischievous nature still lingers in all of us?so I handed him my shorthand diary. He took it with a grateful bow and asked:?

"May I read it?"

"If you wish," I answered as politely as I could. He opened it, and for a moment his face fell. Then he stood up and bowed.

"Oh, you are such a clever woman!" he said. "I have known for a long time that Mr. Jonathan was a man with much to be thankful for, but I see his wife possesses all the good qualities. Would you do me the honor and help me by reading it to me? Alas, I do not know shorthand." By this point, my little joke was over, and I felt almost ashamed. So, I took the typewritten copy from my workbasket and handed it to him.

"Forgive me," I said. "I couldn't help myself. But I figured you would want to ask about dear Lucy, and to save you time?not for my sake, but because I know your time is precious?I typed it out for you."

He took it, and his eyes shone. "You are so kind," he said. "May I read it now? I might have some questions for you after I finish."

"By all means," I said. "Read it while I order lunch. Then you can ask me questions while we eat." He bowed, settled into a chair with his back to the light, and became completely absorbed in the papers. Meanwhile, I went to check on lunch, mostly to ensure he wouldn't be disturbed. When I returned, I found him pacing hurriedly around the room, his face flushed with excitement. He rushed over and grabbed both of my hands.

"Oh, Madam Mina," he said, "how can I ever tell you how much I owe you? This paper is like a ray of sunshine. It opens a door for me. I am dazed?I am dazzled by so much light, even though dark clouds still roll in behind that light every time. But that is something you do not, and cannot, understand. Oh, but I am so grateful to you, you clever woman. Madam"?he said this very seriously?"if ever Abraham Van Helsing can do anything for you or your family, I hope you will let me know. It would be my joy and pleasure to serve you as a friend. Not just as a friend, but I pledge that all I have ever learned and everything I can do will be at the service of you and those you love. There is darkness in life, and there is light; you are one of the lights. You will have a happy, good life, and your husband will be blessed because of you."

"But, Doctor, you praise me too much, and?and you do not really know me."

"I do not know you? I, an old man who has studied men and women his entire life? I, who have made the brain and everything that comes from it my specialty! I have read the diary you so kindly prepared for me, which breathes truth in every single line. I, who read your sweet letter to poor Lucy about your marriage and your deep trust?you say I do not know you! Oh, Madam Mina, good women reveal the truth of their lives every day, every hour, and every minute, so clearly that angels could read it. And we men who seek to understand have a bit of that angelic vision in us. Your husband has a noble nature, and so do you. You have trust, and trust cannot exist where there is a cruel or mean spirit. Now, tell me about your husband. Is he completely well? Is his fever completely gone, and is he strong and healthy again?" I saw this as the perfect opening to ask him about Jonathan, so I said:?

"He was almost fully recovered, but he was terribly upset by Mr. Hawkins's death." He interrupted:?

"Oh, yes, I know. I read your last two letters." I continued:?

"I think that upset him, because when we were in town last Thursday, he suffered a sort of shock."

"A shock? And so soon after brain fever! That is not good. What kind of shock was it?"

"He thought he saw someone who reminded him of something terrible?the very thing that caused his brain fever in the first place." At that moment, everything seemed to overwhelm me all at once. The pity I felt for Jonathan, the sheer horror he experienced, the terrifying mystery of his diary, and the fear that had been hanging over me ever since?it all came crashing down in a wave of emotion. I suppose I became hysterical, because I threw myself to my knees, held up my hands to him, and begged him to make my husband well again. He took my hands, gently raised me up, and sat me on the sofa. He sat beside me, holding my hand in his, and spoke to me with such incredible sweetness:?

"My life is barren and lonely, so consumed by work that I have not had much time for friendships. But since my friend John Seward called me here, I have met so many good people and witnessed such nobility that I feel the loneliness of my life more than ever?a feeling that has only grown as I get older. Believe me, then, when I say that I come to you with the utmost respect. You have given me hope?not necessarily hope for what I am investigating, but hope that there are still good women left to make life happy. Women whose lives and truths can serve as a great lesson for future children. I am so very glad that I might be of some use to you here. If your husband is suffering, his suffering falls within my field of study and experience. I promise you that I will gladly do everything in my power to help him?to make his life strong and manly again, and to make your life happy. Now, you must eat. You are exhausted and perhaps overly anxious. Your husband Jonathan would not want to see you looking so pale. Whatever displeases the one he loves is not good for him. So, for his sake, you must eat and smile. You have told me everything about Lucy, so we will not speak of her anymore right now, lest it upset you. I will stay in Exeter tonight because I need to think deeply about what you have told me. Once I have thought it over, I will ask you questions, if I may. And then, you will tell me as much as you can about husband Jonathan's troubles?but not yet. You must eat now; you can tell me everything afterward."

After lunch, when we returned to the drawing-room, he said:?

"And now, tell me everything about him." When it came time to speak to this highly educated and learned man, I suddenly worried he would think I was a weak fool and that Jonathan was a madman?the journal is just so bizarre. I hesitated to continue. But he was so sweet and kind, and he had promised to help. I trusted him, so I said:?

"Dr. Van Helsing, what I have to tell you is so strange that you must not laugh at me or my husband. Since yesterday, I have been consumed by a feverish doubt. You must be kind to me and not think I am foolish for even half-believing such bizarre things." He reassured me with both his tone and his words when he replied:?

"Oh, my dear, if you only knew how strange the matter I am investigating is, you would be the one laughing. I have learned never to dismiss anyone's beliefs, no matter how strange they may seem. I have always tried to keep an open mind. It is not the ordinary things in life that close a mind, but the strange and extraordinary things?the things that make someone doubt their own sanity."

"Thank you, thank you a thousand times over! You have lifted a huge weight from my mind. If you will allow me, I will give you a document to read. It is long, but I have typed it out for you. It will explain my troubles and Jonathan's. It is a copy of the journal he kept while abroad, detailing everything that happened. I don't dare say a word about it; you must read it for yourself and be the judge. And then, when I see you next, perhaps you will be kind enough to tell me what you think."

"I promise," he said as I handed him the papers. "I will come to see you and your husband tomorrow morning as soon as I can, if you will allow me."

"Jonathan will be here at half-past eleven. You must come have lunch with us and meet him then. You can easily catch the fast 3:34 train, which will get you to Paddington before eight." He was surprised that I knew the train schedules off the top of my head, but he didn't know that I memorized all the trains to and from Exeter so I could help Jonathan whenever he was in a hurry.

So, he took the papers and left, and now I am sitting here thinking?thinking, though I hardly know what about.

It may spell disaster for others, but for you and your husband, there is nothing to fear. He is a brave and noble man. Let me tell you, from my vast experience with men, that anyone who could climb down that castle wall and enter that room?and then do it a second time?is not the kind of man to be permanently broken by a shock. His brain and his heart are completely sound; I swear this to you, even before I have met him. So, please rest easy. I will have many things to ask him about later. I am so blessed that I came to see you today, because I have learned so much all at once that my head is spinning?spinning more than ever, and I need time to think.

"Yours most faithfully,

"Abraham Van Helsing."

Letter from Mrs. Harker to Van Helsing.

"September 25, 6:30 p.m.

"My dear Dr. Van Helsing,?

"A thousand thanks for your incredibly kind letter. It has lifted a massive weight from my mind. And yet, if his journal is true, what terrifying things exist in this world! How awful it would be if that man?that monster?is actually here in London! I am terrified even to think of it. Just this moment, as I was writing, I received a telegram from Jonathan saying he is leaving Launceston on the 6:25 train tonight and will be here by 10:18. Knowing he is coming home means I won't have to be afraid tonight. Therefore, instead of joining us for lunch tomorrow, would you please come over for breakfast at eight o'clock, if that isn't too early for you? If you are in a rush, you can easily catch the 10:30 train, which will get you to Paddington around 2:35. You don't need to reply to this; if I don't hear from you, I will assume you are coming for breakfast.

"Believe me,

"Your faithful and grateful friend,

"Mina Harker."

Jonathan Harker's Journal.

September 26.?I never thought I would write in this diary again, but the time has come. When I got home last night, Mina had supper ready. After we ate, she told me about Van Helsing's visit, how she had given him copies of our diaries, and how terribly worried she had been about me. She showed me the doctor's letter confirming that everything I wrote down was true. The news seems to have made me a completely new man. It was the crushing doubt about what was real and what wasn't that had broken me down. I felt powerless, lost in the dark, and paranoid. But now that I know the truth, I am no longer afraid?not even of the Count. So, he finally succeeded in his plan to get to London, and the man I saw really was him. He has grown younger, but how? If Van Helsing is the man Mina claims he is, he is exactly the person we need to unmask the Count and hunt him down. Mina and I stayed up late discussing everything. She is getting dressed now, and I will head over to the hotel in a few minutes to fetch the doctor....

I think he was surprised to see me. When I walked into his room and introduced myself, he grabbed my shoulders, turned my face toward the light, studied me sharply, and said:?

"But Madam Mina told me you were ill, that you had suffered a shock." It was so amusing to hear my wife referred to as "Madam Mina" by this kind, strong-featured old man. I smiled and replied:?

"I was ill, and I did suffer a shock, but you have already cured me."

"And how did I do that?"

"Through the letter you sent Mina last night. I was plagued by doubt, and my entire life took on an air of unreality. I didn't know what to trust?not even the evidence of my own senses. Because I didn't know what to believe, I didn't know what to do. So, I just kept plugging away at work, falling back into the normal routine of my life. But that routine stopped working for me, and I lost all trust in myself. Doctor, you don't know what it's like to doubt everything, even your own mind. No, you definitely don't; you couldn't possibly know that with eyebrows as strong as yours." He seemed pleased by this, chuckled, and said:?

"Aha! So you are a physiognomist (someone who reads character from faces). I learn more here every hour. I am very much looking forward to having breakfast with you. And, sir, please forgive praise from an old man, but you are truly blessed in your wife." I could have happily listened to him praise Mina all day, so I just nodded and stayed silent.

"She is one of God's most perfect creations, fashioned by His own hand to show us men?and other women?that there is a heaven we can enter, and that its light can shine right here on earth. She is so true, so sweet, so noble, and has so little ego?which, let me tell you, is incredibly rare in this skeptical and selfish age. And as for you, sir?I have read all your letters to poor Miss Lucy, and some of her letters mention you, so I've known of you for a few days through the words of others. But last night, I saw your true self. Will you shake my hand? Let us be friends for the rest of our lives."

We shook hands, and he was so earnest and kind that I found myself choking up with emotion.

"And now," he said, "may I ask for a bit more help? I have a massive task ahead of me, and the first step is to gather information. You can help me with that. Can you tell me what happened before you left for Transylvania? I might ask for more help later, and of a different kind, but this is what I need right now."

"Look here, sir," I said, "does your task have anything to do with the Count?"

"It does," he replied solemnly.

"Then I am with you heart and soul. Since you are taking the 10:30 train, you won't have time to read my notes now. But I will get the bundle of papers for you. You can take them with you and read them on the train."

After breakfast, I walked him to the station. As we were saying our goodbyes, he said:?

"Perhaps you will come to London if I send for you, and bring Madam Mina along too."

"We will both come whenever you call," I replied.

I bought him the morning papers as well as the London papers from the night before. While we stood chatting at his carriage window, waiting for the train to depart, he was flipping through them. Suddenly, his eyes seemed to catch something in one of them?"The Westminster Gazette," I could tell by the color of the paper?and he went completely pale. He read the article intensely, groaning to himself: "My God! My God! So soon! So soon!" I don't think he even remembered I was there in that moment. Just then, the train's whistle blew, and it began to pull away. This brought him back to reality; he leaned out the window, waved his hand, and called out: "Give my love to Madam Mina; I will write to you both as soon as I possibly can."

Dr. Seward's Diary.

September 26.?It seems there is truly no such thing as finality. Not even a week ago, I declared "The End" in this diary, and yet here I am starting all over again, or rather, continuing the same terrifying record. Until this afternoon, I had no reason to dwell on the past. Renfield had become, for all intents and purposes, as sane as he had ever been. He was already deeply involved in his fly-catching business again, and he had just started dealing in spiders too, so he hadn't caused me any trouble. I received a letter from Arthur, written on Sunday, and gathered from it that he is holding up remarkably well. Quincey Morris is staying with him, which is a huge help, as Quincey is a bubbling well of good cheer. Quincey dropped me a quick note as well, mentioning that Arthur is starting to recover a bit of his old buoyant spirit. So, my mind is at ease regarding all of them. As for myself, I was settling back into my work with the same enthusiasm I used to have for it. I could honestly say that the wound poor Lucy's death left on me was beginning to heal over. Now, however, everything has been torn open again, and God only knows how it will end. I suspect Van Helsing thinks he knows how it will end too, but he only reveals enough at a time to whet my curiosity. He went down to Exeter yesterday and stayed the night. He returned today, practically bounding into my room around half-past five, and shoved last night's "Westminster Gazette" into my hand.

"What do you make of that?" he asked, stepping back and crossing his arms.

I looked over the newspaper because I honestly didn't know what he was referring to. But he snatched it back and pointed out a paragraph about children being lured away in Hampstead. The story didn't mean much to me until I reached a sentence describing small puncture wounds found on the children's throats. An idea hit me, and I looked up at him. "Well?" he prompted.

"It sounds exactly like poor Lucy's case."

"And what do you conclude from that?"

"Simply that there is a common cause. Whatever it was that injured her has now injured them." I didn't entirely understand his reply:?

"That is true indirectly, but not directly."

"What do you mean, Professor?" I asked. I was inclined to take his seriousness a bit lightly?after all, four days of rest and freedom from burning, agonizing anxiety does wonders for restoring a man's spirits. But when I saw the look on his face, it sobered me instantly. Not once, even in the depths of our despair over poor Lucy, had he looked more severe.

"Tell me!" I said. "I can't even hazard a guess. I don't know what to think, and I have no data on which to base a theory."

"Are you telling me, my friend John, that you have no suspicion as to what killed poor Lucy? Not even after all the hints given, not only by the events themselves but by me?"

"I assume it was nervous exhaustion brought on by a massive loss of blood."

"And how was that blood lost or wasted?" I just shook my head. He walked over, sat down next to me, and continued:?

"You are a clever man, friend John. You reason well, and you have a sharp mind. But you are too prejudiced. You refuse to let your eyes see or your ears hear; anything outside your daily, ordinary life simply does not exist to you. Do you not believe there are things you cannot understand, but which exist nonetheless? That some people can see things that others cannot? But there are things, both ancient and modern, that men refuse to truly examine because they already 'know'?or think they know?what others have told them. Ah, it is the fatal flaw of our science: it wants to explain everything, and if it cannot explain something, it declares there is nothing to explain. Yet, every single day we see new beliefs cropping up around us?beliefs that claim to be new, but are actually ancient ideas masquerading as young ones, much like aging fine ladies at the opera. I suppose you don't believe in the physical teleportation of bodies. No? Or in materialization from spirits? No? Or in astral projection? No? Or in mind reading? No? Or in hypnotism??"

"Yes, I do," I interjected. "Charcot proved that well enough." He smiled and went on: "Ah, so you are satisfied with that one. Yes? And of course, you fully understand how it works, and you can follow the mind of the great Charcot?alas, he is no longer with us!?right into the very soul of the patient he is influencing. No? Then, friend John, am I to assume you simply accept it as fact and are perfectly content to leave a massive blank space between the premise and the conclusion? No? Then tell me?since I am a student of the brain?how you can accept hypnotism but completely reject mind reading? Let me tell you, my friend, that things are being done today in the field of electrical science that the very men who discovered electricity would have condemned as unholy magic?men who, not long before their own discoveries, would have been burned at the stake as wizards. There are always mysteries in life. Why did Methuselah live to be nine hundred years old, and 'Old Parr' to one hundred and sixty-nine, while poor Lucy, even with the blood of four strong men pumping through her veins, couldn't survive one single day longer? Because if she had lived just one more day, we could have saved her! Do you understand all the mysteries of life and death? Do you know everything about comparative anatomy? Can you explain why the traits of savage beasts appear in some men but not in others? Can you tell me why, when normal spiders remain small and die quickly, one massive spider lived for centuries in the tower of an old Spanish church, growing larger and larger until, upon descending, it could drink all the oil from the church lamps? Can you explain why out on the Pampas, and elsewhere, there are bats that come at night, slice open the veins of cattle and horses, and suck them completely dry? Or how, on some islands in the Western seas, there are bats that hang in the trees all day, looking like giant nuts or seed pods to anyone who sees them, but when the sailors sleep out on the deck because it's too hot, those bats flutter down onto them?and the next morning, those sailors are found dead, as white and bloodless as Miss Lucy was?"

"Good God, Professor!" I cried, leaping to my feet. "Are you trying to tell me that Lucy was bitten by one of those bats? And that such a creature exists right here in nineteenth-century London?" He waved his hand, demanding silence, and continued:?

"Can you tell me why a tortoise lives longer than several generations of men? Why an elephant lives on and on, outlasting entire human dynasties? Or why a parrot never dies of old age, only from being bitten by a cat or a dog, or from some illness? Can you tell me why people across all ages and regions believe that there are a chosen few who can live forever, if permitted? That there are men and women who simply cannot die? We all know?because science has finally vouched for it?that toads have been found sealed inside solid rocks for thousands of years, trapped inside a tiny hole since the dawn of the world. Can you tell me how an Indian fakir can induce a death-like state in himself, be buried with his grave sealed, have corn planted on top of it, harvested, cut, planted again, and harvested again?only for men to return, break the unbroken seal, and find the fakir not dead, but rising up to walk among them just as before?" I had to interrupt him here. I was becoming deeply bewildered. He was crowding my mind with such a massive list of nature's eccentricities and seemingly impossible possibilities that my imagination was catching fire. I had a vague sense that he was trying to teach me a lesson, just like he used to do years ago in his study in Amsterdam. But back then, he always told me the core thesis first, so I had a central idea to focus my thoughts on as he spoke. Now, I didn't have that guiding thread, but I desperately wanted to follow his logic. So I said:?

"Professor, treat me like your favorite student again. Tell me your main thesis so I can apply your knowledge as you explain it. Right now, my mind is jumping frantically from point to point, the way a madman?not a sane one?follows an idea. I feel like a clumsy novice stumbling through a foggy bog, leaping blindly from one patch of solid ground to another, without any idea of where I'm actually going."

"That is a perfect image," he replied. "Very well, I will tell you. My thesis is this: I want you to believe."

"To believe what?"

"To believe in things that you currently cannot believe in. Let me give you an example. I once heard of an American who defined faith like this: 'Faith is that faculty which enables us to believe things which we know to be untrue.' I personally follow that man's logic. He meant that we must keep an open mind, and we must not let a tiny sliver of truth block the path of a massive, overwhelming truth?much like a small rock trying to stop a speeding freight train. We discover the small truth first. Good! We keep it, and we value it. But we must never let that small truth trick itself into thinking it is the *only* truth in the entire universe."

"So, you want me to prevent my previous convictions from destroying my mind's receptivity to strange and unbelievable matters. Have I understood your lesson correctly?"

"Ah, you are still my favorite pupil. You are absolutely worth teaching. Now that you are willing to understand, you have taken the very first step toward actually understanding. So, do you believe that those tiny holes in the children's throats were made by the exact same creature that made the holes in Miss Lucy's throat?"

"I suppose so." He stood up and spoke with heavy solemnity:?

"Then you are entirely wrong. Oh, how I wish you were right! But alas, no. The reality is worse. It is far, far worse."

"In God's name, Professor Van Helsing, what on earth do you mean?" I cried.

He threw himself into a chair with a gesture of utter despair, rested his elbows on the table, and buried his face in his hands as he spoke:?

"The wounds on those children... were made by Miss Lucy!"
