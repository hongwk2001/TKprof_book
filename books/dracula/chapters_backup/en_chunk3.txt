Report from Dr. Patrick Hennessey to Dr. John Seward.

"September 20.

"My dear Sir,

"Per your request, I've enclosed a full report on the condition of everything left under my supervision.... Regarding the patient, Renfield, there is more to share. He had another violent outburst that could have ended horribly, though fortunately, it resulted in no major harm. This afternoon, a delivery cart carrying two men pulled up to the empty house next door—the same property the patient has escaped to twice before. The men were lost, so they stopped at our gate to ask the porter for directions. I was smoking after dinner, looking out the study window, and saw one of the men walk up to the building. As he passed Renfield's window, the patient started screaming at him from inside, calling him every filthy name in the book. The delivery man, who seemed like a decent guy, just told him to 'shut up, you foul-mouthed beggar.' This caused Renfield to accuse the man of trying to rob and murder him, shouting that he would stop him even if he had to hang for it. I opened my window and signaled the man to ignore him. After taking a look around and realizing he was at an asylum, the man simply replied, 'Lord bless you, sir, I don't care what someone says to me in a madhouse. I just feel sorry for you and the boss for having to live under the same roof as a wild animal like that.' He then politely asked for directions, and I pointed him to the gate of the empty house next door. He walked away while Renfield continued screaming threats and curses at his back. Because Renfield is usually so well-behaved and has never acted out like this outside of his specific violent fits, I went downstairs to see what had provoked him. To my shock, I found him completely calm and incredibly polite. I tried to get him to talk about the incident, but he just smiled, asked what I meant, and acted like he had absolutely no memory of it happening. Unfortunately, this was just another example of his manipulative cunning, because less than thirty minutes later, I heard from him again. This time, he had smashed through his window and was sprinting down the driveway. Fearing he was going to hurt someone, I yelled for the orderlies to follow me and ran after him. My fear was justified; the same delivery cart we had seen earlier was now driving down the road, loaded with several massive wooden boxes. The delivery men were wiping their foreheads and looking red in the face, clearly exhausted from heavy lifting. Before I could reach him, Renfield launched himself at the men. He dragged one of them right off the cart and started smashing his head into the ground. If I hadn't grabbed him at that exact second, I firmly believe he would have killed the man on the spot. The second delivery man jumped down and cracked Renfield over the head with the heavy handle of his whip. It was a brutal hit, but Renfield didn't even flinch. He grabbed the second man, too, and started wrestling with all three of us, tossing us around like kittens. You know I'm a heavy man, and the orderlies are both big guys, but he was tossing us around like toys. At first, he fought in total silence, but as we finally started overpowering him and the orderlies wrestled him into a straitjacket, he started screaming: 'I'll stop them! They won't rob me! They won't murder me piece by piece! I'll fight for my Lord and Master!' and poured out a stream of similar crazy nonsense. It was incredibly difficult to drag him back inside and lock him in the padded room. One of the orderlies, Hardy, got a broken finger in the scuffle. I set the bone, though, and he's recovering well.

"The two delivery men were furious at first, threatening to sue us for damages and promising to bring the full weight of the law down on the asylum. But their threats were mixed with embarrassed excuses about how they had been beaten up by a weak crazy man. They claimed they would have easily taken him down if they hadn't just spent all their energy carrying and loading those massive boxes. They also blamed their defeat on being dehydrated from their dusty work, complaining that there wasn't a pub anywhere near the job site. I knew exactly what they were hinting at. After giving them each a stiff glass of rum and a gold coin, they laughed off the whole attack. They swore they'd gladly fight a much worse madman any day of the week if it meant meeting a 'great guy' like me. Just in case we need them later, I wrote down their names and addresses: Jack Smollet, of Dudding's Rents, King George's Road, Great Walworth; and Thomas Snelling, Peter Farley's Row, Guide Court, Bethnal Green. They both work for Harris & Sons Moving and Shipment Company in Soho.

"I will keep you updated on anything interesting that happens here, and I'll send a telegram immediately if anything urgent comes up.

"Sincerely,

"PATRICK HENNESSEY."

Letter, Mina Harker to Lucy Westenra.

(Lucy never opened this letter.)

"September 18.

"My dearest Lucy,

"We've just suffered a terrible loss. Mr. Hawkins passed away very suddenly. Some people might not think it's a huge tragedy for us, but Jonathan and I had grown to love him so much that it truly feels like losing a father. I never knew my own parents, so the sweet old man's death has really devastated me. Jonathan is absolutely heartbroken. He's grieving deeply for the wonderful, kind man who supported him his entire life, treated him like a son, and ultimately left him a fortune that—for people from our modest backgrounds—feels like unimaginable wealth. But Jonathan is also struggling with something else. He says the immense responsibility that comes with inheriting the business and the fortune is making him anxious, and he's starting to doubt his own abilities. I try my best to encourage him, and knowing how much I believe in him helps him believe in himself. But this is exactly where the trauma he experienced abroad is doing the most damage. It's so incredibly unfair that such a sweet, genuine, noble, and strong man—a man who worked his way up from a lowly clerk to the owner of the firm in just a few years—has been so mentally scarred that the core of his self-confidence is just gone. I'm so sorry, dear, for dumping all my problems on you while you're supposed to be celebrating your own happiness. But Lucy, I just had to talk to someone. Constantly putting on a brave, cheerful face for Jonathan is wearing me out, and I don't have anyone else here I can confide in. I'm dreading our trip to London the day after tomorrow. Mr. Hawkins left specific instructions in his will to be buried in his father's grave, and since he has no living relatives, Jonathan will have to act as the chief mourner. I'll try to sneak away to see you, my dearest, even if it's only for a few minutes. I'm sorry again for burdening you with this. Sending you all my love and blessings.

"Your loving friend,

"MINA HARKER."

Dr. Seward's Diary.

September 20.—Pure stubbornness and force of habit are the only things forcing me to write this entry tonight. I am so miserable, so depressed, and so utterly sick of the world and everything in it—including life itself—that I wouldn't even care if the Angel of Death came for me right now. And those grim wings have been flapping all too often lately: first Lucy's mother, then Arthur's father, and now... well. Let me just get on with my notes.

I took over the watch from Van Helsing exactly on schedule. We wanted Arthur to get some rest too, but he refused to leave. He only agreed to go when I pointed out that we would need his help during the day, and if we all collapsed from exhaustion, Lucy would be the one to suffer. Van Helsing was incredibly gentle with him. "Come, my boy," he said. "Come with me. You are sick, exhausted, and carrying a heavy burden of sorrow and mental anguish, on top of the physical toll we both know you've paid. You shouldn't be alone right now; being alone only feeds your fears and anxiety. Come down to the living room. There's a roaring fire and two comfortable sofas. You take one, I'll take the other, and just knowing we're together will bring us both comfort, even if we don't speak, or if we just sleep." Arthur agreed and followed him out, casting one last, heartbreaking look at Lucy's face, which looked almost whiter than the pillow beneath it. She was lying completely still. I checked the room to make sure everything was in order. I saw that the Professor had set up his garlic defenses in this room just like the others; the window frames reeked of it, and over the silk scarf Van Helsing made her wear, a rough wreath of those pungent flowers was draped around Lucy's neck. Her breathing was a bit rattling and strained, and her face looked worse than ever. Her mouth hung open, revealing pale gums, and in the dim, flickering light, her teeth looked significantly longer and sharper than they had that morning. The lighting played a strange trick, making her canine teeth look especially long and vicious. I sat down next to her bed. After a while, she shifted uncomfortably in her sleep. Right at that exact moment, I heard a dull flapping and thudding sound against the window glass. I quietly walked over and peeked through a crack in the blinds. The moon was bright, and I could clearly see a massive bat circling outside. It was obviously attracted to the light, even though it was very dim, and every few seconds it would slam its wings against the window. When I walked back to my chair, I saw that Lucy had shifted again and had actually torn the garlic flowers away from her throat. I fixed them the best I could and sat back down to watch her.

She woke up shortly after, and I fed her the small meal Van Helsing had ordered. She ate very little, and moved sluggishly. The desperate, unconscious fight for survival that had defined her illness up until now seemed entirely gone. I found it very strange that the second she regained consciousness, she pulled the garlic flowers tightly against her chest. It was bizarre: whenever she slipped into that deep, rattling sleep, she would push the flowers away; but the moment she woke up, she would clutch them desperately. I know I wasn't imagining this, because over the long hours that followed, she drifted in and out of sleep multiple times, repeating the exact same behavior every single time.

Van Helsing came to relieve me at six o'clock in the morning. Arthur had finally fallen asleep, and the Professor had kindly let him rest. When Van Helsing looked at Lucy's face, I heard him suck in a sharp breath through his teeth. In a harsh, urgent whisper, he ordered, "Open the blinds; I need light!" He leaned over, bringing his face inches from hers, and examined her closely. He pulled the garlic flowers away and gently lifted the silk scarf from her neck. As he did, he recoiled, and I heard a muffled "My God!" escape his lips. I leaned in to look, too, and a cold shiver ran down my spine.

The puncture wounds on her throat had completely vanished.

Van Helsing stood perfectly still for five full minutes, staring at her with the most grim, hardened expression I've ever seen him wear. Then he turned to me and said in a chillingly calm voice:

"She's dying. It won't be long now. Mark my words, it makes a massive difference whether she passes away awake or asleep. Go wake up that poor boy and bring him here to say goodbye. He trusts us, and we promised him."

I went down to the dining room and woke Arthur. He was disoriented for a second, but when he saw the bright morning sunlight coming through the shutters, he panicked, thinking he had slept too long. I calmed him down, telling him Lucy was still asleep, but then I gently explained that both Van Helsing and I believed the end was very near. He buried his face in his hands, slid off the sofa onto his knees, and stayed there for about a minute, crying and praying as his shoulders shook with grief. I grabbed his hand and pulled him to his feet. "Come on, my dear friend," I said. "Gather all your courage. You have to be strong; it will make it easier for her."

When we walked back into Lucy's room, I noticed that Van Helsing, always thoughtful, had tidied everything up to make the room look as peaceful as possible. He had even brushed Lucy's hair so it fanned out over the pillow in its usual beautiful waves. As we stepped to the bed, she opened her eyes. Seeing Arthur, she whispered softly:

"Arthur! Oh, my love, I'm so glad you're here!" He leaned down to kiss her, but Van Helsing suddenly threw his arm out to stop him. "No," he whispered sharply, "not yet! Just hold her hand; it will bring her more comfort."

So Arthur took her hand and kneeled next to the bed. She looked absolutely beautiful; the softness in her face perfectly matched the angelic look in her eyes. Slowly, her eyes fluttered shut, and she drifted off to sleep. For a few moments, her chest rose and fell gently, and she breathed easily, like an exhausted child finally finding rest.

But then, the horrible, subtle change I had seen during the night returned. Her breathing became heavy and rattling, her mouth fell open, and her receding pale gums made her teeth look sharper and longer than ever. In a strange, dazed, half-asleep state, she opened her eyes. The angelic softness was gone; her eyes were now dull and completely hard. In a sultry, seductive voice I had never, ever heard her use before, she purred:

"Arthur! Oh, my love, I'm so glad you're here! Kiss me!" Arthur eagerly leaned in to kiss her, but in a flash, Van Helsing—who had been just as shocked by her voice as I was—pounced on him. He grabbed Arthur by the back of the neck with both hands and yanked him backward with a terrifying, furious strength I didn't know the old man possessed. He literally hurled Arthur across the room.

"Not for your life!" Van Helsing roared. "Not for your living soul, or hers!" He planted himself between Arthur and the bed, standing fiercely like a lion defending its territory.

Arthur was so stunned he stood frozen, totally unsure of what to say or do. But before anger or violence could take over, he remembered where he was and what was happening, and he stood quietly, waiting to see what would happen next.

I couldn't take my eyes off Lucy, and neither could Van Helsing. We watched a sudden spasm of pure rage twist her face into a dark shadow, and her sharp teeth snapped together violently. Then her eyes rolled shut, and she began breathing heavily again.

A few moments later, she opened her eyes again, and the beautiful, gentle softness had returned. She reached out her pale, terribly thin hand and took Van Helsing's large, rough hand, pulling it toward her face and kissing it. "My true friend," she whispered weakly, but with unimaginable heartbreak. "My true friend, and his! Oh, please, protect him, and give me peace!"

"I swear I will!" Van Helsing said solemnly. He dropped to his knees beside the bed and raised his hand in a formal oath. He then looked back at Arthur. "Come here, my boy. Take her hand in yours, and kiss her on the forehead—but only once."

Their eyes met in a final, loving look, replacing the kiss on the lips they couldn't share. And with that, they said goodbye.

Lucy's eyes closed. Van Helsing, who had been watching every movement like a hawk, gently took Arthur by the arm and guided him away from the bed.

Lucy's breathing grew heavy and rattling one last time... and then suddenly stopped entirely.

"It's over," Van Helsing said quietly. "She's gone."

I took Arthur by the arm and walked him down to the living room. He collapsed onto a chair, buried his face in his hands, and broke into a sobbing so devastated it nearly destroyed me just to watch.

I returned to the bedroom and found Van Helsing staring down at poor Lucy's body. His face was harder and more intense than I had ever seen it. A strange physical change was coming over her. Death had somehow restored some of her beauty; the harsh, drawn lines on her forehead and cheeks smoothed out, and even her lips lost that terrifying, dead pallor. It was as if the blood that was no longer needed to pump her heart had flooded her skin to make the ugly reality of death seem a little less cruel.

"We thought her dying whilst she slept, And sleeping when she died."

I stood next to Van Helsing and whispered:

"Well, poor girl, at least she finally has peace. This is the end."

He turned his head to look at me, his expression deadly serious.

"No," he said grimly. "Sadly, no. This is only the beginning."

When I asked him what on earth he meant by that, he just shook his head.

"There is nothing we can do right now," he replied. "Just wait and see."
