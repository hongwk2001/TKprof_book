Chapter 19

Jonathan Harker’s Journal

October 1, 5 a.m. — I went with the search party feeling relatively at ease, as I don’t think I’ve ever seen Mina look so strong and healthy. I’m incredibly glad she agreed to step back and let the men handle this work. Somehow, the thought of her being involved in this terrifying business always filled me with dread. But now that her part is finished, and it is entirely thanks to her energy, intelligence, and foresight that the whole story has been pieced together so flawlessly, she can rightly feel that her work is done and leave the rest to us. I think we were all a bit unsettled by the incident with Mr. Renfield. Walking back from his room to the study, we didn't say a word. Once there, Mr. Morris spoke to Dr. Seward:

"Say, Jack, if that man wasn't bluffing, he is the most rational lunatic I've ever seen. I'm not entirely sure, but I believe he had a serious purpose, and if he did, it was pretty unfair not to give him a chance." Lord Godalming and I remained silent, but Dr. Van Helsing added:

"Friend John, you know more about lunatics than I do, and I'm glad of it. If it had been up to me, I probably would have set him free before that final hysterical outburst. But we live and learn, and in our current mission, we cannot take any chances, as our friend Quincey would say. Things are best left as they are." Dr. Seward seemed to answer them both in a somewhat distant, dreamy tone:

"I think I might agree with you. If he were just an ordinary lunatic, I would have taken a chance and trusted him. But he seems so deeply connected to the Count in an almost indexing way that I'm terrified of making a mistake by indulging his obsessions. I can't forget how he prayed for a cat with almost the exact same fervor, only to turn around and try to tear my throat out with his teeth. Besides, he called the Count 'lord and master,' and he might want to get out to help him in some demonic way. That horrid monster has wolves, rats, and his own kind to assist him, so I doubt he's above using a presentable lunatic. The man certainly seemed sincere, though. I just hope we made the right choice. These things, combined with the wild and dangerous work we have ahead of us, really unnerves a man." The Professor walked over and put his hand on Seward's shoulder, speaking in his solemn, gentle way:

"Friend John, don't be afraid. We are just trying to do our duty in a very sad and terrible situation; we can only do what we think is best. What else can we hope for, other than the mercy of a good God?" Lord Godalming had slipped out for a few minutes, but now he returned, holding up a small silver whistle.

"That old place might be swarming with rats, and if so, I've brought an antidote," he remarked. After climbing over the wall, we made our way to the house, making sure to stay hidden in the shadows of the trees on the lawn whenever the moonlight broke through. When we reached the porch, the Professor opened his bag and laid out an assortment of items on the steps, dividing them into four small piles, clearly one for each of us. Then he spoke:

"My friends, we are walking into terrible danger, and we need many different kinds of weapons. Our enemy is not just a spiritual being. Remember that he has the strength of twenty men. While our necks and windpipes are normal—and therefore can be broken or crushed—his are not vulnerable to mere physical strength. A stronger man, or a group of men stronger than him combined, might be able to hold him down at certain times, but they cannot hurt him the way he can hurt us. Therefore, we must protect ourselves from his touch. Keep this near your heart"—he lifted a small silver crucifix and handed it to me, as I was the closest—"put these flowers around your neck"—he handed me a wreath of withered garlic blossoms—"for more earthly enemies, take this revolver and knife. To help with everything, attach these small electric lamps to your chest. And above all, for the final moment, take this, which we must not desecrate unnecessarily." He placed a piece of the Sacred Wafer into an envelope and handed it to me. Everyone else received the exact same equipment. "Now," he said, "friend John, where are the skeleton keys? If we can unlock the door, we won't need to break in through a window, like we did at Miss Lucy's."

Dr. Seward tried a few skeleton keys, his precise hands as a surgeon proving very useful. Soon, he found one that fit. After jiggling it a bit, the bolt gave way and slid back with a rusty clang. We pushed the door, and the rusty hinges creaked as it slowly opened. It was startlingly similar to the description in Dr. Seward’s diary of Miss Westenra’s tomb being opened. I suspect everyone else felt the same way, as we all instinctively stepped back. The Professor was the first to move forward, stepping through the open doorway.

"In manus tuas, Domine!" he said, crossing himself as he crossed the threshold. We shut the door behind us, afraid that our electric lamps might attract attention from the road. The Professor carefully checked the lock to ensure we wouldn't be trapped inside if we had to make a quick escape. Then, we all turned on our lamps and began the search.

The light from our small lamps created bizarre shapes as the beams intersected or our bodies cast massive shadows. I couldn't shake the eerie feeling that someone else was in there with us. I suppose the grim setting brought back powerful memories of my terrifying experience in Transylvania. I think we all felt it, because I noticed the others constantly glancing over their shoulders at every sound and new shadow, exactly as I was doing.

The entire place was buried in dust. It seemed to be inches thick on the floor, except where someone had recently walked. When I pointed my lamp down, I could see hobnail boot prints where the crust of dust had been broken. The walls were thick and heavy with dust, and the corners were filled with massive spider webs that had gathered so much dust they hung down like torn, old rags under the weight. On a table in the hall sat a large bunch of keys, each with a yellowed tag. They had obviously been used recently, as lifting them revealed clean outlines in the thick blanket of dust on the table. The Professor turned to me and said:

"You know this place, Jonathan. You copied maps of it, so you know it better than we do. Which way to the chapel?" I had a general idea of the direction, even though I hadn't been able to get inside during my previous visit. I took the lead, and after a few wrong turns, found myself facing a low, arched oak door reinforced with iron bands. "This is the spot," the Professor said, shining his lamp on a small map of the house that he had copied from my original purchase documents. With a little effort, we found the right key on the ring and unlocked the door. We were bracing ourselves for something unpleasant, as a faint, disgusting smell seemed to leak through the cracks as we opened the door, but none of us were prepared for the stench that hit us. The others had never encountered the Count up close, and when I saw him, he was either fasting in his room or bloated with fresh blood in an open, ruined building. But this place was small and confined, and years of disuse had made the air stagnant and foul. There was an earthy scent, like a dry, toxic mist, cutting through the polluted air. But how can I even describe the odor itself? It wasn't just a mixture of every fatal disease and the sharp, acidic smell of blood; it smelled as if rot itself had rotted. Ugh! It makes me sick just thinking about it. It felt like every breath that monster had ever exhaled had clung to the room, multiplying its repulsiveness.

Normally, such a stench would have stopped us in our tracks. But this wasn't a normal situation, and our solemn, terrifying mission gave us a strength that overpowered mere physical discomfort. After the involuntary flinching from that first nauseating wave, we all went to work as if that disgusting place was a rose garden.

We thoroughly examined the place, and as we began, the Professor said:

"The first priority is to count how many boxes are left. Then we need to check every nook and cranny to see if we can find any clues about what happened to the rest." A single glance was enough to tell how many remained, as the large earth-filled crates were massive and impossible to miss.

There were only twenty-nine left out of the fifty! I had a moment of sheer panic when I saw Lord Godalming suddenly turn and stare down the dark hallway beyond the vaulted door. I followed his gaze, and for a second, my heart skipped a beat. Hiding in the shadows, I thought I caught a glimpse of the Count's evil face—the bridge of his nose, those glowing red eyes, red lips, and that terrifyingly pale skin. It only lasted a moment. As Lord Godalming muttered, "I thought I saw a face, but it was just the shadows," and went back to the search, I pointed my lamp that way and stepped into the passage. There was no one there. Since the hallway was just solid walls with no corners, doors, or openings of any kind, there was absolutely nowhere for him to hide. I figured my fear had just played tricks on my imagination, so I didn't say anything.

A few minutes later, I saw Morris abruptly step back from a corner he was inspecting. Our nerves were definitely getting the better of us, so we all turned to look. We saw a massive cluster of glowing lights twinkling like stars in the dark. Instinctively, we all backed away. The entire room was suddenly crawling with rats.

For a few moments, we stood there horrified—everyone except Lord Godalming, who apparently had come prepared for this exact situation. He rushed over to the heavy, iron-reinforced oak door that Dr. Seward had described seeing from the outside, and which I had also seen. He unlocked it, pulled back the massive bolts, and swung it wide open. Then, pulling a small silver whistle from his pocket, he blew a sharp, low note. Almost immediately, a chorus of barking answered from behind Dr. Seward’s house, and about a minute later, three terriers came sprinting around the corner. We had all unconsciously backed toward the door, and as we moved, I noticed the dust on the floor had been heavily disturbed. The missing boxes must have been dragged out this way. But even in that short minute, the number of rats had multiplied terrifyingly. They swarmed the room until the lamplight reflecting off their moving black bodies and gleaming, wicked eyes made the floor look like a mound of dirt covered in fireflies. The dogs charged forward but abruptly stopped at the threshold, snarling. Then, all at once, they pointed their noses up and started howling in the most mournful way. The rats were now swarming in the thousands, so we stepped outside.

Lord Godalming picked up one of the dogs, carried him inside, and set him on the floor. The moment the dog's paws hit the ground, he seemed to snap out of his fear and immediately attacked his natural prey. The rats scattered so quickly that before the first dog could even kill a dozen of them, the other dogs—who were brought inside the same way—barely had anything left to hunt before the entire swarm vanished.

When the rats disappeared, it felt as though some dark presence had lifted from the room. The dogs happily bounded around, barking cheerfully as they pounced on their dead prey, flipping them over and violently shaking them in the air. Everyone's mood seemed to improve. I don't know if it was because opening the chapel door cleared out the toxic air or if we just felt relieved to be out in the open, but the heavy shadow of fear definitely slipped away from us. The grim reality of why we were there felt a little less overwhelming, though our determination hadn't faded one bit. We closed, barred, and locked the outer door, then took the dogs with us to search the rest of the house. We found absolutely nothing except an unbelievable amount of dust, completely untouched except for my own footprints from my first visit. The dogs never showed any signs of fear again, and even when we went back to the chapel, they played around as if they were hunting rabbits in a sunny forest.

Dawn was starting to break in the east when we finally came out the front door. Dr. Van Helsing took the front door key from the ring, properly locked up the house, and put the key in his pocket.

"So far," he said, "tonight has been a huge success. We haven't suffered any of the harm I was worried about, and we figured out exactly how many boxes are missing. But more than anything, I am thrilled that we managed to get through this first—and probably most dangerous—step without involving our dear Madam Mina. We didn't have to burden her waking or sleeping mind with sights, sounds, and smells of horror that she might never be able to forget. We also learned an important lesson, if we can draw conclusions from specific events: the animals the Count commands are not actually under his spiritual control. Just look—those rats came when he called them, just like he summoned the wolves from his castle when you left and when that poor mother was crying outside. But even though they came to him, they scattered in a panic the moment my friend Arthur's small dogs showed up. We still face other issues, dangers, and fears, and this monster will definitely use his control over animals again. So, he went somewhere else. Good! That gives us a chance to put him in 'check' in this chess game we are playing for human souls. Now, let's head home. Dawn is almost here, and we have every reason to be happy with our first night's work. We might have many more perilous days and nights ahead of us, but we must keep going and never back down from any danger."

The asylum was quiet when we returned, except for some poor patient screaming in one of the far wards and a low moaning coming from Renfield’s room. The poor guy was probably tormenting himself with unnecessary, painful thoughts, as insane people often do.

I tiptoed into our room and found Mina asleep, breathing so softly I had to put my ear close just to hear it. She looked paler than usual. I really hope tonight's meeting didn't upset her. I am incredibly thankful she will be left out of our future work and even our planning sessions. It’s just too much stress for a woman to handle. I didn't think so at first, but I know better now. That's why I'm glad it’s finally settled. There might be things that would terrify her, but keeping them a secret could be even worse if she ever suspected we were hiding something. From now on, our work is completely off-limits to her, at least until we can tell her it's all over and the world is finally free of that demonic monster. I imagine it will be hard to start keeping secrets after we've shared everything so openly, but I need to stay strong. Tomorrow, I will keep quiet about everything we did tonight and refuse to discuss what happened. I'll sleep on the sofa so I don't wake her.

*       *       *       *       *

October 1, later. — It makes sense that we all overslept, considering how busy the day was and that we got zero rest during the night. Even Mina must have been exhausted. Even though I slept well past sunrise, I woke up before her and had to call her name two or three times before she stirred. She was sleeping so deeply that for a few seconds, she didn't even recognize me. She just stared at me in sheer terror, like someone jolted awake from a nightmare. She mentioned feeling a bit tired, so I let her rest until later in the day. We now know that twenty-one boxes were moved. If multiple boxes were taken during any of those trips, we might be able to track them all down at once. That would obviously make our job much easier, and the sooner we handle it, the better. I am going to track down Thomas Snelling today.

Dr. Seward’s Diary.

October 1. — It was around noon when the Professor walked into my room and woke me up. He seemed much more cheerful and upbeat than usual, and it was obvious that last night's progress had lifted a lot of the heavy burden off his mind. After recapping our nighttime adventure, he suddenly asked:

"Your patient really interests me. Would it be possible for me to visit him with you this morning? Or, if you're too busy, I can just go by myself. It’s a completely new experience for me to meet a lunatic who can discuss philosophy and reason so logically." I had some urgent work to finish, so I told him I’d appreciate it if he went alone so I wouldn't keep him waiting. I called an orderly and gave him the necessary instructions. Before the Professor left the room, I warned him not to let my patient give him any false impressions. "But," he replied, "I want him to talk about himself and his delusion about eating living things. I read in your diary from yesterday that he told Madam Mina he used to believe in that. Why are you smiling, friend John?"

"Excuse me," I said, "but the answer is right here." I tapped my hand on the typewritten pages. "When our supposedly sane and educated lunatic told Mrs. Harker that story about consuming life, his breath literally smelled like the flies and spiders he had eaten just before she walked into the room." Van Helsing smiled back. "Good!" he said. "You have a sharp memory, friend John. I should have remembered that. And yet, this twisted way of thinking and remembering is exactly what makes mental illness so fascinating to study. Who knows? I might learn more from the foolishness of this madman than I would from the teachings of the wisest scholars." I went back to my work and soon finished everything I needed to do. It felt like barely any time had passed, but Van Helsing was already back in the study. "Am I interrupting?" he asked politely from the doorway.

"Not at all," I answered. "Come in. I’m finished with my work and I’m free. I can go with you now, if you'd like."

"There's no need; I already saw him!"

"Well?"

"I'm afraid he doesn't think much of me. Our meeting was brief. When I walked into his room, he was sitting on a stool in the middle of the floor, elbows on his knees, looking absolutely miserable. I spoke to him as cheerfully and respectfully as I could. He didn't answer at all. 'Don't you know me?' I asked. His response wasn't very encouraging: 'I know you well enough; you're the old fool Van Helsing. Take your idiotic brain theories and get out of here. Damn all you thick-headed Dutchmen!' He refused to say another word and just sat there in stubborn silence, completely ignoring me as if I wasn't even in the room. So, there goes my chance to learn something profound from this 'clever' lunatic. I think I'll go have a nice chat with sweet Madam Mina to cheer myself up. Friend John, words can't express how glad I am that she won't be troubled or stressed by our horrifying work anymore. We'll definitely miss her help, but this is for the best."

"I agree with you completely," I said firmly, not wanting him to second-guess the decision. "Mrs. Harker is better off out of this. Things are bad enough for us men, who have seen the worst the world has to offer and survived our fair share of tight spots. But this is no place for a woman. If she stayed involved in this mess, it would eventually destroy her."

So Van Helsing went to talk with the Harkers, while Quincey and Art are out tracking down clues about the earth-filled boxes. I’m going to finish my rounds, and then we’ll all meet up tonight.

Mina Harker’s Journal.

October 1. — It feels so strange to be kept in the dark today. After sharing everything with Jonathan for so many years, it hurts to see him so obviously avoiding certain subjects—especially the most important ones. I slept in late this morning after yesterday's exhaustion, and though Jonathan woke up late too, he was up before me. He spoke to me so sweetly and tenderly before he left, but he didn't say a single word about what happened at the Count's house. And he must have known how incredibly anxious I was! My poor, sweet husband. I imagine keeping it from me upset him even more than it upset me. They all decided it was best for me not to be dragged any deeper into this nightmare, and I agreed. But the thought of him hiding things from me! And now I’m crying like an idiot, even though I know it’s because of my husband's deep love for me and the genuine kindness of those brave men.

That actually made me feel a bit better. Well, Jonathan will tell me everything someday. And just in case he ever suspects I kept anything from him, I’m going to keep writing in my journal as usual. If he ever doubts my trust, I’ll show him this journal so he can read every thought in my heart. I feel strangely sad and depressed today. I guess it’s just the aftermath of all that terrifying excitement.

Last night, I went to bed right after the men left, simply because they told me to. I wasn't sleepy; I was consumed by anxiety. I kept replaying everything that's happened since Jonathan came to see me in London, and it all feels like a horrific tragedy pushing us relentlessly toward some inevitable doom. It seems like no matter what you do, even if it's the right thing, it just causes the very disaster you were trying to prevent. If I hadn't gone to Whitby, maybe poor, sweet Lucy would still be alive. She didn't start going to the churchyard until I arrived. If I hadn't taken her there during the day, she wouldn't have sleepwalked there at night. And if she hadn't gone there asleep at night, that monster wouldn't have been able to destroy her. Oh, why did I ever go to Whitby? Great, I'm crying again! I don't know what's wrong with me today. I have to hide this from Jonathan. If he knew I'd cried twice in one morning—I, who never cry over my own problems, and whom he has never made cry—it would break his heart. I have to put on a brave face, and if I feel like crying, he must never see it. I suppose this is just one of the harsh lessons we women have to learn....

I can't exactly recall how I fell asleep last night. I remember hearing dogs suddenly barking and a bunch of weird noises—like loud, frantic praying—coming from Mr. Renfield's room, which is somewhere below mine. And then, total silence. It was so quiet it actually scared me, so I got up and looked out the window. Everything was dark and still. The black shadows cast by the moonlight seemed to hold a quiet, eerie mystery of their own. Nothing moved; everything felt as cold and permanent as death. Because of that, a thin trail of white mist creeping across the grass toward the house at an almost invisible pace looked as if it were alive and thinking. Distracting myself must have helped, because when I got back into bed, I felt incredibly heavy and tired. I lay there for a while but couldn't quite drift off, so I got up to check the window again. The mist had spread and was now right up against the house, thick against the wall, like it was trying to sneak into the windows. The poor man downstairs was yelling louder than ever. I couldn't make out his words, but his tone sounded like he was desperately begging for something. Then I heard a struggle, and I realized the orderlies were restraining him. I was so terrified that I crawled under the covers, pulled them over my head, and plugged my ears. I didn't think I was sleepy at all, but I must have passed out, because other than my dreams, I don't remember a thing until Jonathan woke me up this morning. I think it took me a minute to realize where I was and that it was Jonathan leaning over me. My dream was very bizarre, a perfect example of how the thoughts you have right before sleep can twist into a dream.

In my dream, I thought I was asleep, waiting for Jonathan to return. I was terribly worried about him, but I couldn't move. My hands, feet, and brain felt weighed down with lead, making everything slow and sluggish. I tossed and turned in my sleep, trapped in my thoughts. Then I slowly realized the air had become heavy, damp, and cold. I pulled the blankets off my face and was shocked to see that the room was completely foggy. The gaslight I had left on low for Jonathan was just a tiny red dot glowing through the thick mist that had poured into the room. I suddenly remembered that I had closed the window before going to bed. I tried to get up to check, but a heavy, paralyzing exhaustion chained my body and my mind. I just lay there and endured it; that was all I could do. I closed my eyes, but I could still see straight through my eyelids. (It’s amazing how our minds play tricks on us in dreams and how easily we can imagine impossible things.) The mist kept getting thicker, and I could finally see how it was getting inside. It wasn't coming through the window; it was seeping through the cracks around the door like smoke, or like the intense white steam from boiling water. It poured in until it gathered into a thick pillar of fog in the middle of the room. At the top of the pillar, the gaslight glowed like a single red eye. My thoughts started spinning, matching the swirling fog in the room, and a Bible verse echoed in my head: "a pillar of cloud by day and of fire by night." Was God sending me some kind of spiritual sign in my sleep? But this pillar had both the day and night guides—the fire was in that red eye, and that thought suddenly terrified and fascinated me. As I stared, the flame split in two. Now it looked like two glowing red eyes staring at me through the fog, exactly like Lucy had described when she was delirious on the cliff, talking about the sunset hitting the windows of St. Mary's Church. Suddenly, I was hit with the horrifying realization that this was exactly how Jonathan had described those terrifying women materializing out of the swirling mist and moonlight. I must have fainted in my dream, because everything went pitch black. The last thing my imagination conjured up was a pale, bruised face leaning out of the fog to look at me. I need to be careful with these dreams; if I have too many of them, I’ll lose my mind. I’d ask Dr. Van Helsing or Dr. Seward for sleeping pills, but I don't want to worry them. If I told them about a dream like this right now, it would just add to their fears for my safety. Tonight, I will try my hardest to fall asleep naturally. If I can't, I'll ask for a dose of chloral tomorrow night. Taking it once won't hurt, and it will finally give me a good night's rest. Last night exhausted me more than if I hadn't slept at all.

*       *       *       *       *

October 2, 10 p.m. — I slept last night, but I didn't dream. I must have slept deeply because I didn't even wake up when Jonathan came to bed. But the sleep didn't refresh me; today, I feel incredibly weak and lifeless. I spent all of yesterday trying to read or just dozing on the couch. In the afternoon, Mr. Renfield asked if he could see me. Poor man, he was so gentle. When I left, he kissed my hand and told God to bless me. For some reason, it really got to me; I'm crying just thinking about it. This emotional fragility is new, and I need to be careful. Jonathan would be miserable if he knew I'd been crying. He and the others were out until dinner, and they all came back exhausted. I did my best to cheer them up, and the effort seemed to help me, too, because I completely forgot how tired I was. After dinner, they sent me off to bed so they could go smoke together—or so they said. I know they really just wanted to share everything they discovered today. I could tell from Jonathan's behavior that he had something important to tell them. I wasn't as sleepy as I should have been, so before they left, I asked Dr. Seward for a mild sedative, explaining that I hadn't slept well the night before. He kindly mixed me a sleeping draft and promised it wouldn't hurt me because it was very mild.... I took it and am now waiting to fall asleep, but I'm still wide awake. I hope I didn't make a mistake. As I start to drift off, a new fear is creeping in: I worry that I was foolish to take away my ability to wake up easily. I might need to. Ah, here comes sleep. Good night.
