Chapter 23

Cease forbidding philosophers to possess money. No one has condemned wisdom to poverty. The philosopher may own ample wealth. But they will not own wealth that someone tore from another. They will not own wealth stained with another's blood. They must obtain their wealth without wronging any person. They must obtain it without winning it by base means. They must honorably come by it and honorably spend it. It must be such that spite alone could shake its head at it. Raise it to whatever figure you please. It will still remain an honorable possession. It includes much which every person would like to call their own. Yet nothing exists which anyone can say belongs to them. Such a person will not forfeit their right to the favor of Fortune. They will neither boast of their inheritance nor blush for it if they honorably acquired it. Yet they will have something to boast of if they throw their house open. They will let all their countrymen come among their property. They will say that if anyone recognizes anything belonging to them here, let them take it. What a great person they will be. How excellently rich they will be if after this speech they possess as much as they had before. I say that if they can safely and confidently submit their accounts to the scrutiny of the people, such a person may boldly enjoy their riches. No one can find in them any item upon which they can lay hands. The wise person will not allow a single ill-won penny to cross their threshold. Yet they will not refuse or close their door against great riches. These represent the gift of Fortune and the product of Virtue. What reason do they have for grudging them good quarters? Let them come and be their guests. They will neither brag of them nor hide them away. The one marks the part of a silly spirit. The other marks a cowardly and paltry spirit. It muffles up a good thing in its lap. Neither will they turn them out of their house. What will they say? Will they say they remain useless, or that they do not know how to use riches? They can perform a journey upon their own feet. Yet they would prefer to ride in a carriage. So they will be capable of remaining poor, yet will wish to be rich. They will own wealth. But they will view it as an uncertain possession which will someday fly away from them. They will not allow it to burden themselves or anyone else. They will give it. Why do you prick up your ears? Why do you open your pockets? They will give it either to good people or to those whom it may make into good people. They will give it after taking the utmost pains to choose the fittest people to receive it. This becomes one who bears in mind that they ought to give an account of their spending as well as their receiving. They will give for good and commendable reasons. A gift ill bestowed counts as a shameful loss. They will have an easily opened pocket, but not one with a hole in it. Someone may take much out of it, yet nothing may fall out of it.
