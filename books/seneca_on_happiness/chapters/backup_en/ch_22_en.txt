Chapter 22

Who can doubt that the wise person has a wider field for the development of their powers if they remain rich than if they remain poor? In the latter case, they can only display the Virtue of neither allowing poverty to pervert them nor crush them. If they have riches, they will have a wide field for the exhibition of temperance, generosity, hard work, orderly arrangement, and greatness. The wise person will not despise themselves, however short of stature they may be. Nevertheless, they will wish to be tall. Even though they remain feeble and one-eyed they may remain in good health. Yet they would prefer to have bodily strength. They know all the while that they have something which remains even more powerful. They will endure illness and will hope for good health. Some things may represent trifles compared with the sum total. We may take them away without destroying the Highest Good. Yet they add somewhat to that constant cheerfulness which arises from Virtue. Riches encourage and brighten up such a person. A favorable wind that bears a sailor on their way delights them. People feel pleasure at a fine day or at a sunny spot in the cold weather. What wise person of our school can deny that even these matters hold a certain value? We call them neither good nor bad. We prefer some of them to others. We show a certain amount of respect to some of them. We show a great deal of respect to some. Do not make any mistake. Riches belong to the class of desirable things. You may ask why I laugh at you, since I place them in the same position that you do. Do you wish to know how different the position is in which we place them? If my riches leave me, they will carry away with them nothing except themselves. You will feel bewildered and will seem left without yourself if they pass away from you. With me riches occupy a certain place. With you they occupy the highest place of all. In short, my riches belong to me. You belong to your riches.
