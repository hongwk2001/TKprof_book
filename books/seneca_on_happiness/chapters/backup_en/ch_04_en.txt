Chapter 4

We may also define our Highest Good in other ways. We can express the same idea using different language. Think of an army. It might spread out wider at one moment. It might contract into a smaller formation at another time. It might curve at its flanks or align in a straight line. Yet, it keeps its strength and loyalty unchanged in any formation. In the same way, we can express our definition of the Highest Good either at length or in a short form.

It means the exact same thing if I say that the Highest Good is a mind that ignores the accidents of Fortune and finds pleasure in Virtue. Or I could say that it is an unconquerable strength of mind. This mind knows the world well, acts gently, and shows great courtesy and consideration to everyone. We may also define a happy person as one who recognizes good and bad only in the form of good or bad minds. Such a person honors honesty. They rest satisfied with their own Virtue. Good fortune does not puff them up, and bad luck does not cast them down. They know no other good than the good they give themselves. They find their real pleasure when they reject fleeting pleasures.

If you wish to pursue this further, you can state this same core idea in many other ways. You can do this without weakening its meaning. Nothing prevents us from saying that a happy life consists of a free, upright, fearless, and steadfast mind. Such a mind stays beyond the reach of fear or desire. It thinks nothing is good except honor and nothing is bad except shame. It regards everything else as trivial details. These details can neither add to nor subtract from the happiness of life. They come and go without increasing or diminishing the Highest Good.

Continuous cheerfulness and deep happiness inevitably accompany a person who lives by these principles. This happiness comes from within. They delight in their possessions. They desire no greater pleasures than the pleasures they find within themselves. They are right to let these lasting values outweigh the petty, ridiculous, and short-lived cravings of the body. On the day a person becomes immune to Pleasure, they also become immune to pain.

Consider the miserable and dangerous slavery a person serves when pleasure and pain rule them. These masters are the most untrustworthy and passionate. We must therefore escape from them into freedom. Only a healthy disregard for Fortune will grant us this freedom. If we achieve this, priceless blessings will dawn upon us. We will gain the serenity of a mind resting in a safe haven. We will have lofty insights. We will find great and steady delight when we overcome error and know the truth. We will also have courtesy and cheerfulness. We will delight in all of these. We will recognize them not as external goods, but as things flowing from the true, proper good of human nature.
