Chapter 19

They say that Diodorus did not act according to the rules of Epicurus when he cut his throat. This Epicurean philosopher within these last few days put an end to his life with his own hand. Some choose to regard this act as the result of madness. Others see it as the result of recklessness. He, meanwhile, remained happy and filled with the consciousness of his own goodness. He bore testimony to himself by his manner of departing from life. He commended the repose of a life spent at anchor in a safe harbor. He said what you do not like to hear because you too ought to do it.

He said he had lived and had run the race which Fortune set for him.

You argue about the life and death of another. You yelp at the names of men whom some peculiarly noble quality rendered great. You do this just as tiny curs do when strangers approach. It interests you that no one should appear good. You act as if Virtue in another reproached all your crimes. You enviously compare the glories of others with your own dirty actions. You do not understand how greatly this harms you. If the people who follow after Virtue remain greedy, lustful, and fond of power, what must you be? You hate the very name of Virtue. You say that no one acts up to their professions or lives according to the standard which they set up in their talks. What wonder is this? The words which they speak remain brave and gigantic. They can weather all the storms which wreck mankind. Yet they themselves struggle to tear themselves away from crosses. Each one of you drives your own nail into these crosses. People hang from one single pole when executioners crucify them. These people who punish themselves divide themselves between as many crosses as they have lusts. But yet they like to speak evil. They act so magnificent in their contempt of the vices of others that I should suppose they had none of their own. I would believe this if some criminals did not spit upon the spectators when they hung on the gibbet.
