Chapter 18

Our opponent objects that you talk one way and live another. You most spiteful of creatures, you who always show the bitterest hatred to the best of men, people flung this reproach at Plato, at Epicurus, and at Zeno. All these declared how they ought to live, not how they did live. I speak of Virtue, not of myself. When I blame vices, I blame my own first of all. When I have the power, I shall live as I ought to live. Spite, however deeply steeped in venom, shall not keep me back from what is best. That poison itself with which you cover others and with which you choke yourselves shall not hinder me. It will not keep me from continuing to praise that life which I do not indeed lead, but which I know I ought to lead. It shall not hinder me from loving Virtue and from following after her, even if I follow a long way behind her and with a limping step. Am I to expect that evil speaking will respect anything? It respected neither Rutilius nor Cato. Will anyone care about men thinking him too rich, when Diogenes the Cynic was not poor enough for those men? That most energetic philosopher fought against all the desires of the body. He remained poorer even than the other Cynics, because besides giving up possessing anything, he had also given up asking for anything. Yet they reproached him because he did not want enough things. They acted as though he professed knowledge of poverty, not Virtue.
