Chapter 15


The strange result of this incident was that when they met for dinner that evening, both Mrs. Fisher and Lady Caroline felt a strange bond of secret understanding with Mr. Wilkins. He couldn't be like other men to them. He couldn't be the same as if they had met him fully dressed. The ice had been broken. They felt close to him and very forgiving, almost like nurses who have helped patients or young children with their baths. They were, after all, familiar with Mr. Wilkins's legs.

What Mrs. Fisher said to him that morning in her initial shock remains a secret, but Mr. Wilkins's reply—once he realized his state—was so polite in its apology and so proper in its embarrassment that she ended up feeling sorry for him and was completely calmed. It was an accident, after all, and no one could prevent accidents. When she saw him at dinner, fully dressed, polished, with spotless clothes and neat hair, she felt that strange bond of a secret understanding. Along with that, she felt a sort sort of personal pride in his appearance now that he was dressed, which soon extended in a subtle way to a personal pride in everything he said.

There was absolutely no doubt in Mrs. Fisher's mind that a man was a far better companion than a woman. Mr. Wilkins's presence and conversation immediately elevated the dinner table from a chaotic mess—yes, a chaotic mess—to a civilized social gathering. He talked about interesting topics the way men do, and though he was very polite to Lady Caroline, he didn't turn into a giggling idiot when speaking to her. He was just as polite to Mrs. Fisher herself. When politics were brought up for the first time at the table, he listened seriously when she wanted to speak and treated her opinions with the attention they deserved. He seemed to agree with her about politician Lloyd George, and his literary tastes were just as solid. It was a real conversation, and he liked nuts too. How he had ended up marrying Mrs. Wilkins was a complete mystery.

Lotty, meanwhile, watched with wide eyes. She had expected Mellersh to need at least two days to relax like this, but the magic of San Salvatore had worked immediately. It wasn't just that he was nice at dinner, because he was always polite at dinner parties with others. He had been pleasant all day in private too—so nice that he had complimented her looks while she was brushing her hair and kissed her. Kissed her! And it wasn't even morning or bedtime.

Since things were going so well, she decided to wait until the next day to tell him the truth about her savings and that Rose wasn't actually hosting them. It would be a shame to ruin the mood. She had planned to tell him as soon as he rested, but it seemed wrong to disturb Mellersh's wonderful mood on his very first day. Let him get more comfortable in paradise first. Once he was fully settled, he wouldn't mind anything.

Her face lit up with joy at the immediate effect of San Salvatore. Even the bath disaster, which she heard about when she came in from the garden, hadn't upset him. Clearly, all he had needed was a vacation. She felt she had been terrible to him when he wanted to take her to Italy himself. But this setup was much better, even if it wasn't her doing. She talked and laughed happily, with no fear of him left. When she noted how clean he looked and joked that you could eat dinner off him, Scrap laughed and Mellersh laughed too. He would have been offended by that back home, assuming she would have even had the courage to say it there.

It was a very successful evening. Whenever Scrap looked at Mr. Wilkins, she pictured him in his towel, dripping wet, and felt amused. Mrs. Fisher was thrilled with him. In Mr. Wilkins's eyes, Rose was a dignified hostess, quiet and proper, and he admired the way she let Mrs. Fisher sit at the head of the table as a polite gesture to her age. Mr. Wilkins thought Mrs. Arbuthnot was naturally quiet. She was the most reserved of the three ladies. He had met her briefly in the living room before dinner and thanked her politely for inviting him, and she had been very quiet. Was she shy? Probably. She had blushed, mumbled something in embarrassment, and then the others had walked in. She spoke the least at dinner. He looked forward to getting to know her better over the next few days, sure it would be pleasant.

Meanwhile, Lady Caroline was everything Mr. Wilkins had hoped for, accepting the compliments he slipped into the conversation between courses very graciously. Mrs. Fisher was exactly the kind of wealthy older lady he had been hoping to find throughout his career. And Lotty had not only improved her manners, but was clearly on excellent terms with Lady Caroline. He had been very embarrassed during the day about talking to Lady Caroline while undressed, and had written her a note apologizing deeply and asking her to forgive his lapse. She had replied in pencil on the back of the envelope: “Don't worry.” He had taken her advice and stopped worrying. As a result, he was now very content. Before going to sleep that night, he affectionately pinched his wife's ear. She was shocked by these displays of affection.

Even better, the next morning brought no change in Mr. Wilkins. He remained just as pleasant all day, even though it was the first day of their second week, which meant it was pay day.

The fact that it was pay day forced Lotty to confess, which she had wanted to delay. She wasn't afraid, but Mellersh was in such a good mood that she didn't want to ruin it. However, right after breakfast, Costanza showed up with a stack of dirty little papers covered in pencil math. She had knocked on Mrs. Fisher's door and been sent away, knocked on Lady Caroline's door and been sent away, and got no answer at Rose's door because Rose was out. Finally, she cornered Lotty, who was showing Mellersh around the house. Costanza pointed at the papers, talking fast and loud, shrugging her shoulders, and gesturing constantly. Lotty realized a week had passed without anyone paying for anything, and it was time to settle the bills.

“Does this lady want something?” Mr. Wilkins asked smoothly.

“Money,” Lotty said.

“Money?”

“It's the housekeeping bills.”

“Well, you don't have to worry about those,” Mr. Wilkins said calmly.

“Oh, yes I do—”

And so she had to confess.

It was amazing how Mellersh reacted. You would think he had always wanted her savings to be spent on this trip. He didn't cross-examine her the way he would have at home. He accepted everything as she explained her lies, and when she finished and said, “I think you have every right to be angry, but I hope you'll forgive me instead,” he simply asked, “What could be better for you than a holiday like this?”

She linked her arm through his, holding it tight, and said, “Oh, Mellersh, you really are too sweet!”—her face red with pride.

The fact that he had adapted to the atmosphere so quickly, becoming completely kind, showed he really appreciated good and beautiful things. He fitted naturally into this peaceful place. She had completely misjudged him; he was naturally a good person. Imagine him not caring about the lies she told before leaving home; he didn't even comment on them. It was wonderful, but maybe not surprising since he was in paradise now. In paradise, no one cared about past mistakes; they were too happy to hold grudges. She squeezed his arm in gratitude, and though he didn't pull away, he didn't squeeze back either. Mr. Wilkins was not a very physical man.

Meanwhile, Costanza, realizing she wasn't getting anywhere with the Wilkinses, went back to Mrs. Fisher. Mrs. Fisher understood Italian and, in the servants' eyes, was clearly the one who should pay the bills because of her age and appearance. While Mrs. Fisher got dressed—putting on a hat, veil, feather boa, and gloves to go for her first walk in the lower garden since she arrived—Costanza explained that if they didn't pay last week's bills, the shops in Castagneto would refuse to give them food for this week. They wouldn't even give credit, said Costanza, who had spent a lot of money and wanted to pay her relatives what was owed. She also wanted to see how her bosses would react. Soon it would be time for lunch, and how could they have lunch without meat, fish, eggs, or anything else?

Mrs. Fisher took the bills and looked at the total. She was so shocked by how high it was and the obvious waste that she sat down at her desk to examine them closely.

Costanza had a terrible half-hour. She hadn't expected English people to be so focused on money. And the 'old lady,' as the servants called her, knew a lot of Italian. With a persistence that made Costanza feel embarrassed for her, since they expected the English to be generous, she went through every single item, demanding explanations until she got them.

There were no real explanations, other than that Costanza had spent a week cooking exactly what she wanted with no limits, and this was the cost.

With no excuse, Costanza began to cry. It was awful to think she would have to cook while being watched and doubted. And what would her relatives say when they realized the orders were being cut back? They would think she had no power and would look down on her.

Costanza cried, but Mrs. Fisher didn't care. In slow, formal Italian that sounded like poetry, she told her she wouldn't pay any bills until next week. Meanwhile, the food had to be just as good, but at a quarter of the price.

Costanza threw her hands up in the air.

Next week, Mrs. Fisher continued, if she saw they had saved money, she would pay the bill. Otherwise—she paused, not actually knowing what she would do. But she looked so stern, majestic, and threatening that Costanza was intimidated.

Then Mrs. Fisher dismissed her and went to find Lady Caroline to complain. She had assumed Lady Caroline was managing the meals and was responsible for the costs, but now she realized the cook had been left to do whatever she wanted since they arrived, which was simply disgraceful.

Scrap wasn't in her room, but when Mrs. Fisher opened the door—suspecting she was hiding inside—the room still smelled sweet from her presence.

“Perfume,” Mrs. Fisher sniffed, closing the door. She wished the famous writer Carlyle could have given this young woman a good talking-to. And yet, maybe even he would have been charmed.

She went downstairs to look for her in the garden and ran into Mr. Wilkins in the hall. He was wearing his hat and lighting a cigar.

Even though Mrs. Fisher liked Mr. Wilkins and felt a strange connection with him after yesterday, she still hated cigar smoke inside the house. She could tolerate it outside, but with so much space outdoors, there was no reason to smoke inside. Even Mr. Fisher, who had been a stubborn man, had given up that habit soon after they married.

However, seeing her, Mr. Wilkins quickly took off his hat and threw his cigar away. He tossed it into a large jar of lilies, and Mrs. Fisher, knowing how much men valued a fresh cigar, was impressed by his quick and polite gesture.

But the cigar missed the water. It got stuck in the flowers, smoking on its own, looking very out of place.

“Where are you going, my pret—” Mr. Wilkins started, walking toward her, but he stopped himself just in time.

Was his good mood making him quote a nursery rhyme to Mrs. Fisher? He didn't even realize he knew it. It was very strange. What had made him think of that? He respected Mrs. Fisher and would never want to offend her by calling her a pretty maid. He wanted to make a good impression. She was intelligent and, he suspected, wealthy. They had gotten along great at breakfast, and he was impressed by the famous people she had known. They were Victorians, of course, but it was nice to talk about them after his brother-in-law's exhausting modern parties in Hampstead. He felt they were becoming good friends. She seemed like she might even hire him as her lawyer soon. He absolutely didn't want to offend her, and he shivered thinking about how close he had come to making a fool of himself.

She hadn't noticed, though.

“You are going out,” he said politely, ready to join her if she said yes.

“I need to find Lady Caroline,” Mrs. Fisher said, walking toward the glass door to the upper garden.

“A pleasant task,” Mr. Wilkins said. “May I help you look? Allow me—” he added, opening the door.

“She usually sits in that corner behind the bushes,” Mrs. Fisher said. “And I don't know about it being pleasant. She has allowed the bills to get incredibly high, and she needs to be scolded.”

“Lady Caroline?” Mr. Wilkins asked, confused. “What does Lady Caroline have to do with the bills here?”

“She was supposed to manage the housekeeping, and since we share costs, she should have made sure things were fair—”

“But Lady Caroline doing the housekeeping? For a group that includes my wife? My dear lady, I am shocked. Don't you know she is the daughter of the Droitwich family?”

“Oh, is that who she is,” Mrs. Fisher said, walking over the gravel toward the corner. “Well, that explains it. The mess her father made in the government during the war was a scandal. He practically stole public funds.”

“But surely you can't expect the daughter of the Droitwiches—” Mr. Wilkins started.

“The Droitwiches don't matter,” Mrs. Fisher interrupted. “If you agree to a job, you should do it. I don't intend to waste my money for any Droitwiches.”

She was a stubborn old woman. Maybe she wouldn't be as easy to manage as he had hoped. But she must be very rich. Only someone with a lot of money would dismiss the Droitwich family so easily. Lotty had been vague about Mrs. Fisher's wealth, describing her house as a tomb with goldfish, but now he was sure she was wealthy. Still, he wished he hadn't joined her right now, as he had no desire to watch her scold Lady Caroline Dester.

But Scrap surprised him again. Whatever she felt when she saw Mr. Wilkins show up in her private corner, she looked completely angelic. She moved her feet off the wall when Mrs. Fisher sat down. As Mrs. Fisher began complaining about not having money to waste on housekeeping, Scrap interrupted by offering her a cushion from behind her head.

“Sit on this,” Scrap said, holding it out. “You'll be more comfortable.”

Mr. Wilkins quickly took it to help.

“Oh, thank you,” Mrs. Fisher said, her train of thought broken.

It was hard to start complaining again. Mr. Wilkins placed the cushion carefully behind her, and she had to thank him again. Her momentum was gone. Besides, Lady Caroline didn't defend herself; she just looked at her with the face of an attentive angel.

Mr. Wilkins thought it must be hard to scold a Dester who looked so beautiful and said nothing. He was glad to see that Mrs. Fisher found it hard too. Her anger faded, and she finally said weakly, “You should have told me you weren't doing the shopping.”

“I didn't know you thought I was,” the lovely voice replied.

“I would like to know,” Mrs. Fisher said, “what you plan to do for the rest of our time here.”

“Nothing,” Scrap said, smiling.

“Nothing? Do you mean to say—”

“If I may make a suggestion, ladies,” Mr. Wilkins interrupted in his smoothest professional voice. They both looked at him, and remembering the towel incident, they felt forgiving. “I suggest you don't ruin a wonderful vacation worrying about housekeeping.”

“Exactly,” Mrs. Fisher said. “That is what I want to avoid.”

“Very sensible,” Mr. Wilkins said. “Why not give the cook—who is excellent, by the way—a set amount of money per person each day? Tell her she must feed you for that amount, and keep the food just as good. You could easily calculate it. You could use the rates of a cheap hotel as a starting point, and cut them in half or even a quarter.”

“And what about last week?” Mrs. Fisher asked. “The high bills from the first week?”

“They will be my gift to the house,” Scrap said, not wanting Lotty's savings to be drained more than she had planned.

There was silence. Mrs. Fisher had nothing left to say.

“Of course, if you want to throw your money away—” she said finally, disapproving but very relieved. Mr. Wilkins was in awe of her high-class generosity. This willingness to spend money without worrying was exactly what he admired most, and it was very useful for professionals like him. He felt it should be welcomed warmly. Mrs. Fisher, however, was not warm. She accepted the offer—which made him think she was stingy with her wealth—but she did so grudgingly.

Gifts were gifts, and you shouldn't criticize them, he felt. If Lady Caroline wanted to pay for everyone's food for the week, they should accept it gracefully. You shouldn't discourage generosity.

On behalf of his wife, Mr. Wilkins thanked her. Joking lightly to keep from embarrassing her, he said she had technically been his wife's host since they arrived. He turned happily to Mrs. Fisher and suggested that she and his wife should write a thank-you note to Lady Caroline. He called it a 'Collins'—a literary term for a thank-you letter after a stay.

Scrap smiled and offered her cigarettes. Mrs. Fisher couldn't help but feel better. They had found a way to avoid waste thanks to Mr. Wilkins, and she hated waste as much as paying for it. She also didn't have to manage the house now. For a moment she had feared that if the others forced her to do the housekeeping because of their indifference or lack of Italian, she would have to invite Kate Lumley to do it. Kate and she had learned Italian together, and she would only let Kate join them if she agreed to manage the house.

But Mr. Wilkins's idea was much better. He really was an excellent man. There was nothing like an intelligent, mature man for good company. When the business was settled and she stood up to take a walk before lunch, Mr. Wilkins didn't stay with Lady Caroline, as most men would have. Instead, he asked to walk with her, showing he preferred good conversation to a pretty face. He was a sensible, companionable, smart, and worldly man. She was very glad she hadn't written to Kate. Why would she want Kate when she had found a better companion?

But Mr. Wilkins didn't walk with Mrs. Fisher for her conversation. He had stood up to walk her out, but then Lady Caroline put her feet back on the wall, adjusted her head in the cushions, and closed her eyes.

She wanted to go to sleep.

It wouldn't be polite for him to stay and disturb her.
