Chapter 22

That night was a full moon. The garden became an enchanted place where all the flowers looked white. You could see the lilies, daphnes, orange blossoms, white stocks, carnations, and white roses as clearly as in the daytime. The colored flowers, however, could only be sensed by their sweet fragrance.

After dinner, the three younger women sat on the low wall at the edge of the upper garden, with Rose sitting slightly apart from the others. They watched the huge moon rise slowly over the bay where the poet Shelley had lived his final months nearly a hundred years earlier. The sea shimmered under the moonlight. Stars twinkled in the sky. The mountains were soft blue outlines dotted with clusters of lights from small villages. The plants in the garden stood perfectly still, without a breath of wind to stir them. Through the glass doors, the dining room—with its candlelit table and bright nasturtiums and marigolds—glowed like a colorful cave. The three men sitting around it, smoking, looked lively and animated compared to the quiet, cool calm outside.

Mrs. Fisher had gone inside to the fireplace in the drawing room. Scrap and Lotty looked up at the sky, speaking only in whispers. Rose remained silent, also looking up at the sky. She was staring at the pine tree, which looked glorious silhouetted against the stars. Every now and then, Scrap and Lotty would look at Rose. She was truly beautiful tonight. Even among the most famous beauties, she would have stood out. No one could have overshadowed her; she was practically glowing.

Lotty leaned close to Scrap's ear. "Love," she whispered.

Scrap nodded. "Yes," she whispered back.

She had to admit it. You only had to look at Rose to know she was in love.

"There's nothing like it," Lotty whispered.

Scrap was silent.

"It's a wonderful thing," Lotty whispered after a moment, as they both watched Rose, "to just let yourself love. Can you think of anything else in the world that works such miracles?"

Scrap couldn't answer her, and even if she could, this was no night to start an argument. This was a night for...

She stopped herself. Love again. It was everywhere, and there was no escaping it. She had come to San Salvatore to escape it, and yet here was everyone experiencing it in different ways. Even Mrs. Fisher seemed to have been touched by it; at dinner, she was different, showing concern because Mr. Briggs wasn't eating, her face soft with motherly affection.

Scrap looked up at the pine tree standing still against the stars. Beauty made you love, and love made you beautiful...

She pulled her shawl closer around her, trying to guard herself. She didn't want to get sentimental. But it was hard not to here; the beautiful night crept in through every gap, bringing overwhelming emotions—feelings about death, time, and wasted years. They were glorious and terrifying feelings of deep longing. She felt small and incredibly alone, exposed and defenseless. She pulled her chiffon shawl tighter, trying to shield herself from the vastness of the universe.

"I suppose," Lotty whispered, "Rose's husband looks to you like just an ordinary, friendly, middle-aged man."

Scrap looked down from the stars, focusing her attention on Lotty.

"Just a rather stout, red-faced man," Lotty whispered.

Scrap nodded.

"He isn't," Lotty whispered. "Rose sees past all that. That's just the surface. She sees what we can't see, because she loves him."

Always love.

Scrap stood up, wrapping her shawl tightly around herself. She walked to her daytime corner, sat alone on the wall, and looked out across the sea where the sun had set, toward the faint shadow of France in the distance.

Yes, love worked wonders, and Mr. Arundel (she still struggled to think of him as Frederick) was Rose's whole world. But love could also do the opposite; it didn't always turn people into angels. Sometimes it made them worse. She had experienced far too much of it in her life. If it had left her alone, or at least been less intense, she might have turned out to be a kind, generous person. And what was she now, thanks to all this love Lotty talked about? Scrap looked at herself honestly: she was a spoiled, bitter, suspicious, and selfish woman.

The glass doors of the dining room opened, and the three men walked out into the garden, with Mr. Wilkins's voice leading the way. He seemed to be doing all the talking, while the other two walked in silence.

She thought she should go back to Lotty and Rose; it would be annoying to be cornered by Mr. Briggs in her dead-end spot.

She stood up reluctantly, annoyed that Mr. Briggs's presence forced her to keep moving from her favorite spots. She walked out from the bushes wishing she looked as stern and cold as she felt, which might make Mr. Briggs lose interest and leave her alone. But she knew she looked nothing like that. At dinner, his hand had shaken when he drank, and he couldn't speak to her without blushing and turning pale. Mrs. Fisher had looked at her with pleading eyes, silently begging her not to hurt the young man.

How could a man behave this way? Scrap thought, frowning. He was young, attractive, and intelligent—surely meant for better things. He had brains; she had watched him at dinner and was sure of it. He had a noble, kind forehead that suggested character. It was a pity he allowed himself to be so infatuated by a pretty face, wasting his energy and peace of mind on her. If only he could see past her looks, he would be cured, and she could sit by herself in peace.

Just past the bushes, she ran into Frederick, who was in a hurry.

"I wanted to find you before I went to Rose," he said. He added quickly, "I want to kiss your shoes."

"Do you?" Scrap smiled. "Then I must go change into my new ones. These aren't nice enough."

She felt very friendly toward Frederick now. He, at least, would not try to pursue her. His chasing days were over. He was a nice, pleasant man, and she liked him. He had obviously been in some sort of mess, and she was grateful Lotty had stopped her from making it worse at dinner. Whatever the problem had been, he was out of it now; his face and Rose's had the same happy glow.

"I will adore you forever now," Frederick said.

Scrap smiled. "Will you?" she said.

"I admired you before for your beauty. Now I admire you because you're not just beautiful, but you're as loyal as a true friend."

Scrap laughed, amused. "Am I?"

"When that impulsive woman—thank goodness she was impulsive—blurted out that I was Rose's husband, you acted just like a loyal friend would," Frederick continued.

"Did I?" Scrap said, her dimple showing.

"It's the rarest combination," Frederick said, "to be a beautiful woman with the loyalty of a brother."

"Is it?" Scrap smiled wistfully. These were very nice compliments. If only she were really like that...

"And I still want to kiss your shoes."

"Won't this do instead?" she asked, holding out her hand.

He took her hand, kissed it quickly, and hurried off. "God bless you," he said as he walked away.

"Where is your luggage?" Scrap called after him.

"Oh, right," Frederick said, pausing. "It's at the station."

"I'll send for it."

He disappeared through the bushes. She went inside to arrange it, which was why Domenico found himself walking to Mezzago for the second time that night, wondering what was going on.

Having arranged for the couple's happiness, she walked back out to the garden, deep in thought. Love brought happiness to everyone but her. Everyone here had been touched by it except her. Poor Mr. Briggs had been hit by the most awkward kind of infatuation. He was a difficult problem, and she was afraid his departure tomorrow wouldn't solve it.

When she reached the others, Frederick was already walking off with Rose, his arm through hers, heading toward the privacy of the lower garden. They had a lot to talk about; their relationship had gone wrong but was suddenly fixed. Lotty would say it was the magic of San Salvatore. She could believe in that magic now. Even she was happier here than she had been in years. The only one left out was Mr. Briggs.

Poor Mr. Briggs. When she saw him, he looked too nice and youthful to be so unhappy. It didn't seem right that the owner of this beautiful place, the one who made their stay possible, should be the only one to leave empty-handed.

She felt a wave of regret. She had spent such peaceful days in his house, enjoying his garden, flowers, views, and furniture. It was the most relaxing and thoughtful time of her life, and it was all thanks to him. She knew she paid a small rent, but that was nothing compared to what she got in return. And it was thanks to renting this place that she met Lotty. Otherwise, they never would have met.

Feeling guilty and suddenly very grateful, Scrap walked straight up to Briggs.

"I owe you so much," she said, realizing how much she had to thank him for, and feeling ashamed of how cold she had been to him earlier. Of course, he hadn't realized she was being cold, since her beautiful face hid her bad mood, but she knew it. She had been cold to everyone for years. If anyone could see through her looks, they would see she was just a spoiled, bitter, suspicious, and selfish woman.

"I owe you so much," Scrap said earnestly, walking right up to Briggs, humbled by her own thoughts.

He stared at her in wonder. "You owe me?" he stammered. "But it is I who... I who..." To see her standing in his garden—no white flower was more beautiful than she was.

"Please," Scrap said earnestly, "let's just be honest. You don't owe me anything. Why would you?"

"I don't owe you anything?" Briggs repeated. "I owe you the most beautiful sight of my life—"

"Oh, for goodness' sake," Scrap pleaded, "please just talk to me normally. Don't be so humble. Why should you be? It's silly. You're worth fifty of me."

"Unwise," thought Mr. Wilkins, who was standing nearby while Lotty sat on the wall. He was shocked that Lady Caroline was encouraging Briggs. "Very unwise," he thought, shaking his head.

Briggs was already in such a bad state that the only option was to reject him completely, Mr. Wilkins believed. Kindness would only be misunderstood by the poor young man. Lady Caroline couldn't possibly want to encourage him; Briggs was fine, but his name alone showed they were from different social classes. Perhaps Lady Caroline didn't realize how encouraging her voice and face made her words sound. She needed a wise advisor like him. There she was, practically holding her hands out to Briggs. Briggs deserved thanks for the holiday, but not this way—alone with Lady Caroline in the moonlight.

Trying to rescue Lady Caroline with some quick tact, Mr. Wilkins said heartily, "It is very proper that you are thanked, Briggs. Let me add my thanks, and my wife's, to Lady Caroline's. We should have toasted you at dinner. There should have been some—"

But Briggs ignored him completely, continuing to look at Lady Caroline as if she were the only woman in the world. Lady Caroline ignored him too, looking at Briggs with a pleading expression. Most unwise, Mr. Wilkins thought.

Lotty, however, stepped in. She got up from the wall, put her arm through Mr. Wilkins's, and pulled him away.

"I want to tell you something, Mellersh," Lotty said, pulling him.

"Later," Mr. Wilkins said, waving her off.

"No, now," Lotty insisted, dragging him away.

He went very reluctantly. Briggs shouldn't be given any encouragement at all.

"Well, what is it?" he asked impatiently as they walked toward the house. "Lady Caroline shouldn't be left alone like that."

"Oh, she isn't alone," Lotty said, as if answering his unspoken thoughts. "Caroline is perfectly fine."

"She is not fine. That young Briggs is—"

"Of course he is. What did you expect? Let's go inside to the fire and Mrs. Fisher. She's all alone."

"I cannot leave Lady Caroline alone in the garden," Mr. Wilkins said, trying to go back.

"Don't be silly, Mellersh—she isn't alone. Besides, I want to tell you something."

"Well, tell me then."

"Inside."

Very reluctantly, Mr. Wilkins let himself be led farther from Lady Caroline. He trusted his wife now, but he was sure she was making a huge mistake. Inside, Mrs. Fisher was sitting by the fire. Mr. Wilkins preferred warm rooms to dark gardens anyway, so he was glad to be inside, but he wished Lady Caroline were safe too.

Mrs. Fisher sat with her hands folded, staring into the fire. A reading lamp was set up, but she wasn't reading. Her old literary heroes didn't seem interesting tonight. They always said the same things, and there was nothing new to learn from them. They were great writers, but they had one major flaw: they were dead. Nothing new could come from them, while the living still offered endless possibilities. She wanted to connect with living, growing people now. She wished she had a son—a son like Mr. Briggs, who was alive, affectionate, and could care for her.

The look on her face touched Lotty's heart. "Poor old dear," she thought, realizing how lonely old age could be—especially for a childless woman who had failed to make friends. It seemed people were happiest in pairs—whether as lovers, friends, siblings, or parents and children. Where was Mrs. Fisher's other half?

Lotty decided to kiss her again. The kiss earlier had worked wonders, and she had felt Mrs. Fisher's warm response. She walked over, leaned down, kissed her, and said cheerfully, "We've come inside."

This time, Mrs. Fisher actually reached up and held Lotty's cheek against hers. She felt safe with this warm, affectionate woman who did unusual things so naturally. She knew Lotty wouldn't make her feel embarrassed.

Lotty was delighted. "I think I'm the other half of her pair," she thought. "I'm going to be close friends with Mrs. Fisher!"

She looked up, laughing. The changes brought by San Salvatore were amazing. Her and Mrs. Fisher... but she could see them becoming very close.

"Where are the others?" Mrs. Fisher asked. "Thank you... dear," she added as Lotty slipped a footstool under her short legs.

"I can see myself bringing footstools to Mrs. Fisher for years to come," Lotty thought, her eyes dancing.

"The Roses," Lotty said, standing up, "have gone down to the lower garden—I think to make love."

"The Roses?"

"The Fredericks, then. They are completely inseparable now."

"Why not just say the Arbuthnots, my dear?" Mr. Wilkins suggested.

"Very well, Mellersh—the Arbuthnots. And the Carolines—"

Both Mr. Wilkins and Mrs. Fisher gasped. Mr. Wilkins, normally so controlled, was shocked and felt angry with his wife for the first time since arriving.

"Really—" he began indignantly.

"Alright, Mellersh—the Briggses, then."

"The Briggses!" Mr. Wilkins cried, genuinely angry. The implication was an insult to Lady Caroline's noble family. "Really—"

"I'm sorry, Mellersh," Lotty said, pretending to be meek, "if you don't like it."

"Like it? You've lost your mind! They only met today!"

"True. But that's why they can start now."

"Start now!" Mr. Wilkins could only echo her words in disbelief.

"I'm sorry, Mellersh," Lotty repeated, "but—"

Her gray eyes sparkled with that same bright conviction that had surprised Rose on their first meeting.

"There's no use fighting it," she said. "I wouldn't resist if I were you. Because—"

She stopped, looking at their worried, serious faces, and laughed.

"I see them becoming the Briggses," Lotty concluded.

During the final week, the mock orange bushes and acacias bloomed at San Salvatore. No one had realized how many acacia trees there were until one day the garden was filled with a new scent, and the delicate trees, succeeding the wisteria, were covered in blossoms among their trembling leaves. Lying under an acacia tree that last week, looking up through the branches at the white flowers against the blue sky as a gentle breeze shook down their sweet scent, was pure happiness. Gradually, the entire garden turned white and filled with fragrance. The lilies were as strong as ever, surrounded by white stocks, carnations, climbing white roses, mock orange, jasmine, and finally, the scent of the acacias. When they left on the first of May and passed through the gates into the village, they could still smell the sweet acacias.
