Chapter 13


The quiet days—quiet only on the outside—passed in floods of warm sunshine, and the servants, watching the four ladies, concluded that they didn't have much energy.

To the staff, San Salvatore seemed completely asleep. Nobody came over for tea, and the ladies never went out for tea either. Guests in past springs had been much more active. They had been energetic and adventurous, taking the boat out, making excursions, ordering Beppo's carriage, and hosting visitors from Mezzago who spent the day. The house used to echo with voices, and sometimes they even drank champagne. Life had been diverse and interesting. But this? What was this? The servants weren't even scolded. They were left entirely to themselves. They yawned.

It was also puzzling that there were no gentlemen around. How could men stay away from so much beauty? After all, even if you subtracted the old lady, the three younger ones combined made a very impressive group of the kind of women men usually chased.

The servants were also confused by how much each lady wanted to spend long hours away from the others. Except at mealtimes, the house was dead silent. It might as well have been as empty as it was all winter, for all the noise they made. The old lady sat in her room alone. The dark-eyed lady wandered off by herself, lingering—as Domenico told the others when he crossed paths with her—for no clear reason among the rocks. The stunningly beautiful blonde lady lay in her lounge chair in the upper garden alone. The less striking, but still pretty, reddish-haired lady walked up into the hills and stayed there for hours alone. Every day, the sun crossed the sky over the house and sank into the sea in the evening, and absolutely nothing happened.

The servants yawned.

Yet the four guests, while their bodies sat (like Mrs. Fisher), lay down (like Lady Caroline), wandered (like Mrs. Arbuthnot), or climbed the hills in solitude (like Mrs. Wilkins), were actually far from lazy. Their minds were incredibly active. Even night, their brains were working, producing clear, light, fast dreams that were completely different from the heavy nightmares of home. There was something in the air at San Salvatore that stimulated the mind, affecting everyone except the locals. The servants, no matter how beautiful the scenery or how lush the seasons, remained completely unchanged in their thoughts. They had seen the amazing show of April in the gardens year after year, and familiarity had made them blind to it. They were as unconscious of it as Domenico's dog sleeping in the sun.

The guests couldn't ignore it—it was too striking after a terribly wet and gloomy March in London. To be suddenly moved to a place where the air was completely still, where the golden light made even the most ordinary things look magical—to be placed in that gentle warmth and sweet fragrance, with the old gray castle as their home and the calm, clear hills in the distance—was an incredible contrast. Even Lady Caroline, who was used to beautiful homes and had traveled everywhere, was surprised by it. It was a particularly wonderful spring that year, and of all the months at San Salvatore, April was the best if the weather was nice. May would burn and dry everything up; March was unstable and could be harsh and cold; but April arrived gently like a blessing. When April was sunny, it was so beautiful that you couldn't help but feel changed, stirred, and deeply touched.

Mrs. Wilkins, as we've seen, responded immediately. She practically threw off all her worries and dived straight into the joy of the place, without hesitation, with a cry of pure delight.

Mrs. Arbuthnot was also moved, but in a different way. She had strange feelings, which we will describe soon.

Mrs. Fisher, being older and more set in her ways, resisted it more, but even she experienced strange feelings that will also be described.

Lady Caroline, who was already very familiar with beautiful estates and warm climates, wasn't as easily surprised, yet she reacted almost as quickly as Mrs. Wilkins. The place had an immediate effect on her too, and she was aware of at least one part of it: starting on the very first night, it made her want to think, acting on her like a conscience. And what this conscience kept pointing out to her with a shocking persistence—a word she didn't want to accept, but couldn't get out of her head—was that she was cheap and superficial.

Superficial. Her. Imagine that.

She had to think that over.

The morning after their first dinner together, she woke up regretting that she had been so talkative with Mrs. Wilkins the night before. Why had she done it? Now, of course, Mrs. Wilkins would try to latch onto her and be inseparable. The thought of someone invading her space for four weeks made Scrap feel exhausted. No doubt the encouraged Mrs. Wilkins would be waiting in the upper garden to corner her when she went out, greeting her with bright morning cheerfulness. She absolutely hated being greeted with morning cheerfulness—or being bothered at all. She shouldn't have encouraged Mrs. Wilkins the night before. Encouraging people was always a mistake. It was bad enough when she didn't encourage them, since just sitting silently usually drew people in anyway, but actively encouraging someone was social suicide. What had she been thinking? Now she would have to waste her precious, lovely time—which she wanted for thinking and sorting out her life—trying to get rid of Mrs. Wilkins.

With great care, walking on her tiptoes to keep the gravel from crunching, she stole out to her corner after getting dressed. But the garden was empty. She didn't need to avoid anyone. Neither Mrs. Wilkins nor anyone else was in sight. She had the place completely to herself. Except for Domenico, who soon appeared to water his plants—especially the ones right next to her—no one came out. She spent a long time chasing thoughts that seemed to slip away just as she grasped them, falling asleep out of exhaustion between these attempts. When she finally felt hungry and checked her watch, she saw it was past three in the afternoon. She realized no one had even bothered to call her for lunch. So, Scrap noted, if anyone was being avoided, it was actually her.

Well, how wonderful, and how new. Now she could finally think without any interruptions. It was lovely to be forgotten.

Still, she was hungry. Mrs. Wilkins, after being so friendly the night before, could have at least let her know lunch was ready. Scrap had really gone out of her way to be nice—like agreeing to Mellersh's sleeping arrangements and letting him have the spare room. She usually didn't care about details, so she felt she had been exceptionally kind to Mrs. Wilkins. In return, Mrs. Wilkins didn't even care if she ate lunch or not.

Fortunately, even though she was hungry, she didn't mind skipping a meal. Life was full of meals anyway. They took up too much time, and she feared Mrs. Fisher was the type of person who lingered at the table. She had dined with Mrs. Fisher twice now, and both times the old lady had been hard to get away from, slowly cracking endless nuts and sipping a single glass of wine that seemed to last forever. It might be a good idea to make a habit of skipping lunch. Since she could easily have tea brought outside and she ate breakfast in her room, she would only have to sit at the dining table and deal with the nuts once a day.

Scrap snuggled her head into the cushions, crossed her feet on the low stone wall, and tried to think some more. She kept telling herself, as she had all morning: Now, I am going to think. But since she had never actually thought deeply about anything in her life, it was hard. It was amazing how her attention kept drifting and her mind kept wandering. She tried to review her past to prepare for her future, searching for any reason why she might be called 'superficial.' But before she knew it, she was thinking about Mr. Wilkins instead.

Well, Mr. Wilkins was easy to think about, though not pleasant. She was dreading his arrival. Not only was it a massive bore to have a man join their group—especially the kind of man she was sure Mr. Wilkins was—but she was also afraid, based on her exhausting past experiences, that he would try to hang around her.

Mrs. Wilkins obviously hadn't thought of this possibility, and Scrap couldn't exactly point it out to her without sounding incredibly vain. She tried to hope that Mr. Wilkins would be the exception to the rule. If he left her alone, she would be so grateful that she might actually like him.

But she had her doubts. What if he followed her around and ruined her peace in the upper garden? What if he made Mrs. Wilkins lose her happy, expressive look? Scrap really didn't want to ruin Mrs. Wilkins's holiday. Yet she had never met a wife who could understand that Scrap had zero interest in her husband. She had met plenty of wives who didn't even like their own husbands, but they still got angry if they thought another woman did, and they were always convinced Scrap was trying to steal them. Steal them! Just thinking about those past situations bored her so much that she fell right back asleep.

When she woke up, her mind returned to Mr. Wilkins.

If Mr. Wilkins behaved like every other man, would Mrs. Wilkins understand, or would it ruin her trip? She seemed smart, but would she see the truth about this? She seemed to read people well, but would she be able to read Scrap's feelings when it came to her own husband?

Having dealt with this so many times, Scrap was full of doubts. She shifted her feet on the stone wall and adjusted a cushion. Maybe she should try to explain her feelings to Mrs. Wilkins in a general, vague way before he arrived. She could explain how much she disliked other people's husbands and how desperately she wanted to be left alone for this one month.

But Scrap was doubtful about that plan too. Having that kind of conversation required a level of closeness that meant becoming real friends with Mrs. Wilkins. And if they became close, and then Mr. Wilkins turned out to be sneaky—as determined men often were—and managed to get into the upper garden anyway, Mrs. Wilkins might think Scrap had lied to her. Deceitful! And over Mr. Wilkins, of all people. Wives were really pathetic.

At four-thirty, she heard the sound of teacups on the other side of the bushes. Was someone bringing her tea?

No, the sounds stayed near the house. They were having tea in the garden—her garden. Scrap thought they could have at least asked if she minded the intrusion. They all knew she sat there.

Maybe someone would bring hers to her corner.

No, nobody brought a thing.

Well, she was too hungry to skip it today, but she would give Francesca strict orders to bring her tea directly to her from now on.

She stood up and walked with her usual effortless grace toward the noise of the tea party. She was hungry, and she also realized she wanted to talk to Mrs. Wilkins again. Mrs. Wilkins hadn't tried to cling to her; she had left her alone all day despite their friendly conversation the night before. Sure, she was a bit eccentric and wore silk sweaters to dinner, but she wasn't clingy. That was a huge relief. Scrap walked toward the table actually looking forward to seeing her, but when she arrived, only Mrs. Fisher and Mrs. Arbuthnot were there.

Mrs. Fisher was pouring the tea, and Mrs. Arbuthnot was offering Mrs. Fisher cookies. Every time Mrs. Fisher offered Mrs. Arbuthnot something—her cup, milk, or sugar—Mrs. Arbuthnot immediately offered her cookies back, insisting with a strange persistence. Was this a game? Scrap wondered, sitting down and taking a cookie.

<Scrap>“Where is Mrs. Wilkins?”</Scrap> Scrap asked.

They didn't know. At least, Mrs. Arbuthnot didn't, and Mrs. Fisher's face became completely blank at the mention of the name.

Apparently, Mrs. Wilkins hadn't been seen since breakfast. Mrs. Arbuthnot thought she had probably gone for a walk with some food. Scrap actually missed her. She ate the giant cookies, which were the best she had ever tasted, in silence. Tea without Mrs. Wilkins was boring. Mrs. Arbuthnot had that overly motherly way of trying to pamper her, make her comfortable, and urge her to eat—even though she was already eating plenty. Scrap had dealt with people like that her whole life. Why couldn't they just leave her alone? She could feed herself. She tried to stop Mrs. Arbuthnot's mothering by being blunt, but it didn't work. Her irritation was completely hidden behind her beautiful face.

Mrs. Fisher sat like a statue, ignoring both of them. She had had a strange day and was a bit concerned. She had been completely alone, since none of the other three had shown up for lunch, and none of them had bothered to tell her they would be out. Then Mrs. Arbuthnot had wandered in for tea and acted weirdly until Lady Caroline joined them and distracted her.

Mrs. Fisher was willing to like Mrs. Arbuthnot, who looked very gentle and proper, but she had some annoying habits. Her way of repeating any offer of food or drink back to the person offering it was strange. “Would you like more tea?” should be answered with a simple yes or no, but Mrs. Arbuthnot kept repeating her trick from breakfast the day before, adding, “Would you?” to her answer. She had done it again this morning and now at tea—the two meals Mrs. Fisher served. Why did she do it? Mrs. Fisher couldn't understand.

But that wasn't what was really bothering her; that was just a minor annoyance. What actually worried her was that she hadn't been able to focus on anything all day, doing nothing but wander restlessly from her study to the stone walls and back. It had been a wasted day, and she hated waste. She had tried to read and write to Kate Lumley, but she couldn't. After reading a few words or writing a few lines, she would get up, walk outside to the terrace, and stare at the sea.

It didn't matter that the letter to Kate hadn't been written. There was plenty of time for that. Let the others think Kate's arrival was set in stone. That was actually better, as it would force Mr. Wilkins to share his wife's room. Kate could wait. She was useful as a threat. An imaginary Kate was just as effective as a real one, and had some advantages. For instance, if Mrs. Fisher was going to be this restless, she preferred not to have Kate watch her pace around. Wandering back and forth looked undignified. But it did matter that she couldn't read a single sentence written by her famous late friends—not even Browning, who had lived in Italy, or Ruskin, whose book about Venice she had brought to read. She couldn't even focus on an interesting book she found in the study about the German Emperor—written in the nineties, before he became so misunderstood, and full of details about his birth and his doctors—without having to put it down to go look at the ocean.

Reading was important; keeping one's mind active was a key duty. How could she read if she was constantly pacing? This restlessness was strange. Was she getting sick? No, she felt healthy—in fact, unusually well. She was moving in and out quite quickly, almost running, and without her cane. It was very strange that she couldn't sit still, she thought, looking past some purple flowers at the sparkling Gulf of Spezia. It was odd that she, who usually walked so slowly with a cane, was suddenly moving so fast.

She felt like talking to someone about it. Not Kate, but someone else. Kate would just offer her tea. Kate always solved things with tea, and she had a boring face. That Mrs. Wilkins, on the other hand—as annoying, talkative, and rude as she was—would probably understand and know what was causing this. But she couldn't tell Mrs. Wilkins. She was the last person Mrs. Fisher would share her feelings with. Her pride wouldn't allow it. Confiding in Mrs. Wilkins? Never.

Mrs. Arbuthnot, while mothering the quiet Scrap at tea, also felt she had had a strange day. Like Mrs. Fisher's day, it had been busy, but only in her head. Her body had been still, but her mind had been racing. For years, she had made sure she stayed too busy to think. Her packed schedule at the church had kept old memories and wishes away. Today, they had all come rushing back. She felt depressed at tea, and feeling sad in such a beautiful place made her feel even worse. How could anyone enjoy this beauty alone? How could you truly appreciate it without sharing it? Except for Lotty, who seemed perfectly capable of it. She had gone down the hill right after breakfast, singing and happy, without even asking Rose to join her.

Rose had spent the day alone, sitting with her arms around her knees, staring ahead. She looked at the spiky leaves of the plants and the pale irises growing in a secluded spot she had found, with the sea visible beyond the blue flowers. It was a hidden corner covered in wild thyme, where no one was likely to disturb her. It was out of sight and sound of the house, far from the path, near the edge of the cliff. She sat so still that lizards ran over her feet, and tiny birds that had flown away at first returned to hop around the bushes as if she weren't there. It was gorgeous. But what was the point of all this beauty when you were alone, with no one who loved you to share it with? If someone were here, she could say, <Rose>“Look, darling.”</Rose> Just saying that sweet word to someone who cared would make her so happy.

She sat still, staring ahead. It was strange that she didn't want to pray here. Back home, she prayed constantly, but here she couldn't do it at all. On her first morning, she had just muttered a quick thank-you to God when she got out of bed, then immediately went to the window to look at the view—tossing the prayer off casually and forgetting it. This morning, feeling guilty, she had forced herself to kneel down, but forcing it didn't help. She couldn't think of anything to say. And she hadn't said her nightly prayers either night. She had simply forgotten them, so absorbed in her own thoughts that she fell asleep and started dreaming the moment she got into bed.

What was happening to her? Why had she stopped praying? She also found it hard to think about her charity work, or even remember that poor people existed. Holidays were good, of course, but should they completely erase reality like this?

Maybe it was fine to forget about her charity work for a while; she would return to it with more energy later. But forgetting to pray couldn't be good, and it was even worse that she didn't care.

Rose didn't care. She knew she didn't, and worse, she was okay with not caring. In this place, she felt indifferent to the two things that had filled her life and kept her going for years. If only she could enjoy this beautiful place to make up for it, but she couldn't. She had no work, she didn't pray, and she felt empty.

Lotty had ruined her day today, just as she had yesterday. It was Lotty's fault for inviting Mellersh and suggesting Rose invite her husband too. After putting Frederick in her head the day before, Lotty had left her alone with her thoughts all afternoon. Since then, she had thought of nothing but Frederick. In Hampstead, she only saw him in her dreams, but here he stayed out of her dreams and haunted her thoughts during the day instead. And this morning, as she tried not to think of him, Lotty had asked if she had written to him yet before running off, forcing him back into her mind again.

How could she invite him? They had been distant for so many years that she wouldn't even know what to say. Besides, he wouldn't come. Why would he? He didn't care about spending time with her. What would they even talk about? They were divided by his writing career and her religious beliefs. She couldn't support his books, which she found immoral, or live off the money he made from them. And he had resented her beliefs at first, then simply got bored by them. He had let her drift away, not caring anymore. In the clear light of San Salvatore, Rose finally saw the truth: both she and her religion bored him.

Naturally, realizing this for the first time made her miserable. For a moment, the beauty of Italy completely vanished. What could she do? She couldn't stop believing in right and wrong, and she felt it was wrong to live off the money he made writing scandalous histories of famous affairs. Even if she abandoned her values and her past ten years of work, would he find her any less boring? Rose knew deep down that once you bore someone, it is almost impossible to change their mind. Once a bore, always a bore—especially to the person who got bored in the first place.

So, looking out at the sea through blurry eyes, she decided she might as well cling to her religion. It was better than nothing. But she wanted something real to hold onto, to love someone living whom she could touch and care for. If only her baby hadn't died. Babies didn't get bored of you; it took them a long time to grow up and judge you. And maybe your child never would. No matter how old they got, you would always be their mother—someone unique and irreplaceable.

Sitting there with tears in her eyes, looking at the water, she felt a powerful urge to hold someone of her own close. Rose was thin and reserved, yet she felt this strange, motherly warmth in her chest. San Salvatore made her feel like a mother who wanted to protect and comfort. She wanted to hold someone, stroking their head and whispering loving words. She imagined Frederick or his child coming to her for comfort because they were hurt or sad. They would need her then, and they would let her love them.

Well, the child was gone and would never return, but maybe Frederick would—one day, when he was old and tired.

Those were Rose's thoughts during her first day alone at San Salvatore. She went to tea feeling more depressed than she had in years. The place had stripped away the fake happiness she had carefully built up at home, leaving her with nothing. Actually, it left her with this painful longing and motherly ache, which was worse than nothing. And even though she was usually calm and kind, today she couldn't stand Mrs. Fisher acting like she owned the place at tea.

You would think such a small thing wouldn't bother her, but it did. Was she changing? Was she now going to fight over petty details on top of longing for her husband? After tea, when Mrs. Fisher and Lady Caroline vanished again—making it clear no one wanted her around—she felt even more depressed. The contrast between the beautiful, warm nature outside and the empty feeling in her heart was too much to bear.

Then Lotty returned for dinner, covered in new freckles and beaming with energy from her day in the sun. She talked, laughed, and spoke without thinking, bringing life back to the table. Lady Caroline became lively, Mrs. Fisher quieted down, and Rose began to feel better. Lotty's good mood was contagious as she described her day—which for anyone else would have just been a long, hot walk with sandwiches.
