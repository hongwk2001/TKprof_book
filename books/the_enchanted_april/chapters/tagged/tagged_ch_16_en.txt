Chapter 16

And so the second week began in total harmony. Three of the women had feared that Mr. Wilkins's arrival would disrupt the peace, while the fourth (Lotty) only avoided this fear because of her strong faith in San Salvatore's magic. Instead of ruining the harmony, he actually increased it. He fitted in perfectly. He was determined to make a good impression, and he succeeded. He was very sweet to his wife—not just in public, which she was used to, but also in private, which he wouldn't have bothered to do unless he really wanted to. And he did want to. He felt so grateful and pleased with her for introducing him to Lady Caroline that he felt genuinely fond of her. He was also proud; he figured there must be much more to Lotty than he had thought, since Lady Caroline had become so close and affectionate with her. The nicer he was to her, the more Lotty blossomed and became truly wonderful, which in turn made him even nicer. It was a wonderful, positive cycle.

For Mellersh, he was practically pampering her. He was naturally a cold, reserved man, not given to showy affection. Yet San Salvatore’s influence was so strong that during this second week, he sometimes playfully pinched both of her ears, one after the other, instead of just one. Lotty, amazed by this rapid increase in affection, wondered what he would do in the third week if he kept this up, since she only had two ears.

He was very polite about sharing the washstand and tried hard not to take up too much space in their small bedroom. Quick to respond, Lotty was even more careful not to get in his way. The room became the setting for many sweet contests of generosity, leaving them both more pleased with each other than ever. He didn't try to use the indoor bathtub again, even though it was fixed and ready. Instead, he got up early every morning and walked down to the sea. Despite the chilly nights making the water cold, he took his daily swim like a proper gentleman, returning for breakfast rubbing his hands and telling Mrs. Fisher he felt ready for anything.

Since Lotty's belief in the magic of San Salvatore was so clearly proven right, and Mr. Wilkins—whom Rose had feared and Scrap had imagined as cold and cruel—was so obviously a changed man, both Rose and Scrap began to think Lotty might be onto something. Maybe San Salvatore really did have a purifying effect on people's characters.

They were especially open to this idea because they felt changes happening inside themselves, too. Both of them felt a sense of clarity during that second week. Scrap found her thoughts becoming much kinder; she was thinking warmly of her parents and relatives, and starting to appreciate the incredible blessings she had received from fate, or providence, or whatever it was. She realized she had wasted those blessings by failing to be happy. As for Rose, her heart still yearned, but now it was with a purpose. She realized that just longing for her husband silently was useless. She had to either find a way to stop it, or give it a chance to be resolved by writing to Frederick and inviting him to join her.

If Mr. Wilkins could change, Rose thought, why not Frederick? How wonderful—how incredibly wonderful—it would be if the place worked its magic on him too, helping them understand each other a little better and become friends. Her rigid shell was softening, and she began to think her stubborn disapproval of his books and her own obsession with charity work had been foolish, maybe even wrong. He was her husband, and she had driven him away. She had scared off precious love, and that couldn't be right. Wasn't Lotty right when she said nothing mattered except love? Nothing really seemed worth doing unless it was built on love. But once love was scared off, could it ever return? Yes, it might, in a beautiful place like this, under the influence of the happiness that Lotty and San Salvatore seemed to spread around like a wonderful, healthy infection.

She had to get him there first, though, and he certainly wouldn't come if she didn't write to tell him where she was.

She would write. She had to write. If she did, there was a chance he might come; if she didn't, there was absolutely no chance. Once he was here in this lovely place, surrounded by such softness and sweetness, it would be easier to talk to him, to try to explain, and to ask for a change. They could try to build a different life together, instead of living with the cold, empty separation, and the bleakness of her religious duties and charity work. Having just one person in the world who was truly yours, to talk to, care for, love, and care about, was worth more than all the public speeches and compliments from committee chairmen in the world. It was even worth more—Rose couldn't help but think it—than all her prayers.

These weren't intellectual thoughts like Scrap's, who didn't experience such longings, but emotional, heartfelt ones. They stayed deep inside Rose's chest, where she felt a physical ache of loneliness. Whenever her courage failed her—which happened almost every day—and writing to Frederick felt impossible, she would look at Mr. Wilkins and feel hopeful again.

There he was, a completely changed man. He went into that tiny, cramped bedroom every night—the very room Lotty had worried about—and came out in the morning with Lotty, both of them happy and sweet to each other. And hadn't he, who was usually so critical of any minor inconvenience at home, survived the bathroom flood with his good mood completely intact? Miracles were happening here. If they could happen to Mr. Wilkins, why not to Frederick?

She got up quickly. Yes, she would write. She would go write to him right now.

But what if...

She paused. What if he didn't reply? What if he didn't even acknowledge her letter?

She sat down again to think about it a little longer.

Rose spent most of the second week in this state of hesitation.

Then there was Mrs. Fisher. Her restlessness grew during that second week. It got to the point where she might as well not have had a private sitting room, because she couldn't sit still anymore. She couldn't sit for even ten minutes at a time. Along with this restlessness, she felt a strange, worrying sensation of new life, like rising sap in a tree. She recognized the feeling from her childhood during sudden springs, when the flowers seemed to bloom overnight, but it was bizarre to feel it again after more than fifty years. She wanted to mention it to someone, but she was too embarrassed. It was a ridiculous feeling for a woman of her age. Yet, day by day, Mrs. Fisher felt more and more like she was about to blossom.

She tried sternly to suppress this inappropriate feeling. Blossoming indeed! She had heard of dry walking sticks or dead wood suddenly sprouting green leaves, but only in myths. She wasn't in a myth. She knew how she ought to behave. Dignity demanded that she have nothing to do with new growth at her age, and yet the feeling persisted—at any moment, she felt like she might sprout fresh green leaves.

Mrs. Fisher was deeply annoyed. One of her pet peeves was when older people imagined they felt young and acted like it. Of course, they were only kidding themselves, but the results were always embarrassing. She had aged the proper way—steadily and firmly, without any sudden phases or silly attempts to feel young again. If, after all these years, she was going to be tricked into some inappropriate emotional outburst, it would be humiliating.

She was actually thankful that her old friend Kate Lumley wasn't there. It would be highly unpleasant if Kate witnessed any change in her behavior. Kate had known her for a lifetime. Mrs. Fisher felt she could let herself go—she frowned at the book she was trying to read, wondering where that phrase "let herself go" had come from—much more easily in front of strangers than an old friend. Old friends, she reflected, constantly compare you to how you used to be. They always do that if you change. They are surprised by any growth, expecting you to remain completely unchanged after fifty until the end of your life.

That is foolish of friends, Mrs. Fisher thought, as her eyes scanned the lines of the page without a single word registering in her mind. It was like sentencing someone to an early death. A person should continue to grow, with dignity of course, no matter how old they were. She had nothing against growing or maturing further; as long as you were alive, you weren't dead—obviously—and growth, change, and maturity were what made life. What she did not want was to grow backwards, to become immature or green again. She would hate that, and yet that was exactly what she felt she was about to do.

Naturally, this made her very uneasy, and she could only distract herself by keeping busy. Growing restless and no longer content to stay on her balcony, she wandered more and more aimlessly in and out of the upper garden. Scrap began to notice this with surprise, especially when she saw that all Mrs. Fisher did was stare at the view for a few minutes, pluck a few dead leaves off the rose bushes, and walk away.

Talking to Mr. Wilkins gave her temporary relief, but although he joined her whenever he could, he wasn't always available. He divided his attention carefully among the three other ladies, and when he was busy, she had to deal with her own thoughts. Perhaps it was the intense light and color of San Salvatore that made everywhere else seem so dark. Her home at Prince of Wales Terrace seemed like a very gloomy place to return to—a dark, narrow street, with a house just as dark and narrow, and nothing truly alive or young in it. The goldfish were barely active, certainly not young, and other than them, there were only the maids, who were dusty old things.

"Dusty old things." Mrs. Fisher stopped mid-thought, shocked by the phrase. Where did that come from? How could she think such a thing? It sounded like something Mrs. Wilkins would say in her casual, almost slangy way. Maybe she had heard Lotty say it and had unconsciously picked it up.

If so, this was both serious and disgusting. It was deeply disturbing that this silly woman had crept into Mrs. Fisher's mind and influenced her thoughts. Lotty's personality—despite the apparent harmony between her and her clever husband—was still completely foreign to Mrs. Fisher, far from what she liked or understood. To be infected by her phrases was awful. Mrs. Fisher had never had such a thought in her life. She had never thought of her maids, or anyone else, as "dusty old things." Her maids were very respectable, neat women who were allowed to use the bath every Saturday night. They were older, of course, but so was she, so was her house, her furniture, and her goldfish. They were all older together, as they should be. But there was a huge difference between being older and being a dusty old thing.

How true it was, as Ruskin said, that bad company ruins good character. But did Ruskin actually say that? (It is actually a biblical proverb.) On second thought, she wasn't sure, but it sounded like something he would write, and it was true anyway. Just hearing Mrs. Wilkins's chatter at meals—even though Mrs. Fisher tried not to listen—was spoiling her own thoughts. Lotty's talk was often vulgar, inappropriate, and irreverent, and Lady Caroline always laughed at it. If she kept hearing it, she might soon start speaking that way herself. How terrible! If her personal awakening was going to express itself in bad language, Mrs. Fisher didn't think she could bear it.

At this point, Mrs. Fisher wished more than ever that she could talk about her strange feelings with someone who would understand. But there was no one who would get it except Mrs. Wilkins herself. She would understand instantly, Mrs. Fisher was sure. But that was out of the question. It would be as humiliating as begging the very germ that infected you to cure your illness.

So she continued to suffer in silence, which kept driving her to wander aimlessly in the upper garden until even Scrap began to notice.

Scrap had noticed and wondered about it for a while before Mr. Wilkins asked her about it one morning. He was helping arrange her cushions—a daily chore he had claimed as his special privilege—when he asked if there was something wrong with Mrs. Fisher.

At that moment, Mrs. Fisher was standing by the eastern wall, shading her eyes and looking closely at the distant white houses of Mezzago. They could see her through the branches of the daphne bushes.

It was amazing how many different ways Scrap found to send Mr. Wilkins away from her corner. Every morning, she came up with a new excuse that sent him off happy after he set up her cushions. She let him adjust her cushions because she had realized, within the first five minutes of his arrival, that her fear of him staring at her with unwanted admiration was completely baseless. Mr. Wilkins did not look at her that way. She felt that it wasn't in his nature, and even if it were, he wouldn't dare try it with her. He was extremely respectful. She could guide his actions with a simple glance, and his only goal was to please. She had been willing to like him if he just avoided hit-on-her behavior, and she did like him. She hadn't forgotten how funny and helpless he looked in his towel that first morning, he amused her, and he was nice to Lotty. True, she liked him best when he wasn't around, but she usually felt that way about everyone. He was one of those rare men who never looked at a woman in a predatory way. This made everything so much simpler and more comfortable for the group. In that sense, Mr. Wilkins was ideal—unique and precious. Whenever she found him a bit boring, she would remind herself of this and think, "What a treasure."

In fact, Mr. Wilkins's main goal during his stay was to be a treasure. He wanted the three ladies who weren't his wife to like and trust him. That way, when problems arose in their lives—as they always did—they would remember how reliable and understanding he was and ask for his professional advice. Clients with problems were exactly what he wanted. He guessed Lady Caroline didn't have any worries at the moment, but someone so beautiful must have had troubles in the past, and would surely have more in the future. He hadn't been around to help before, but he hoped to be in the future. Meanwhile, Mrs. Fisher's behavior looked promising from a business perspective. He had been watching her closely and was almost sure she had something on her mind.

He had made the least progress with the third lady, Mrs. Arbuthnot, because she was so quiet and reserved. But perhaps her habit of avoiding everyone and spending time alone meant she was troubled too? If so, he was ready to help. He would make an effort to talk to her, sit with her, and get her to open up. He knew from Lotty that her husband worked at the British Museum—nothing major yet, but Mr. Wilkins liked to build connections with everyone. Plus, there was always the chance of promotion, which could make Mr. Arbuthnot a very valuable contact later.

As for Lotty, she was charming. She really did have all the good qualities he had noticed when they were dating, which had apparently just been hidden since their marriage. His early impressions of her were now confirmed by the affection and admiration Lady Caroline showed her. He was sure Lady Caroline, with her high social standing and experience, was never wrong about people. Lotty was clearly as valuable as he had once believed. She had already been very helpful by introducing him to Lady Caroline and Mrs. Fisher. A man in his profession could benefit greatly from a clever, attractive wife. Why hadn't she been this appealing before? What caused this sudden blossoming?

Mr. Wilkins began to think there really was something special about the atmosphere of San Salvatore, just as Lotty had told him. It encouraged people to open up and brought out hidden qualities. Feeling increasingly pleased and even charmed by his wife, happy with his progress with the other ladies, and hopeful about Mrs. Arbuthnot, Mr. Wilkins couldn't remember ever having such a pleasant vacation. The only minor complaint he had was that they kept calling him "Mr. Wilkins." Nobody used "Mr. Mellersh-Wilkins," even though he had introduced himself that way to Lady Caroline—a memory that still made him wince a little.

Still, it was a minor detail, nothing to worry about. It would be foolish to stress over anything in such a beautiful place with such good company. He wasn't even worrying about the cost of the trip. He had decided to pay for both his and his wife's expenses, planning to surprise her at the end by returning her savings completely untouched. Just knowing he was planning this nice surprise made him feel warmer than ever toward her.

In fact, Mr. Wilkins, who had started out deliberately trying to be on his best behavior, was now acting that way naturally and without any effort.

Meanwhile, the beautiful golden days of the second week passed one by one, just as lovely as the first. The scent of blooming beanfields on the hills behind the village drifted across to San Salvatore whenever a breeze blew. In the garden, the white narcissus flowers in the long grass along the winding path faded, replaced by slender, rose-colored wild gladiolas. White carnations bloomed along the borders, filling the air with their sweet, spicy scent, and a previously unnoticed bush burst into fragrant purple lilac blooms. Such a mixture of spring and summer flowers seemed unbelievable to anyone who didn't live there. Everything bloomed at once—all the flowers that would normally take six months to appear in England were crowded into a single month. Lotty even found primroses one day in a shady spot up in the hills, and when she brought them down to place near the bright geraniums and heliotrope of San Salvatore, they looked sweet and modest.
