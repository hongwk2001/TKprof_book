Chapter 3

The owner of the medieval castle was an Englishman named Mr. Briggs, who was currently in London. He wrote to them explaining that the castle had enough beds for eight people (not counting servants), three sitting rooms, battlements, dungeons, and electric lighting. The rent was sixty pounds for the month, with the servants' wages extra. He also requested references—assurances that the second half of the rent would be paid (since the first half was due in advance), and a character reference from a lawyer, a doctor, or a clergyman. He was very polite in his letter, explaining that asking for references was standard practice and merely a formality.

Mrs. Arbuthnot and Mrs. Wilkins hadn't thought about references, and they had never imagined the rent would be so high. They had pictured something like three guineas a week, or even less, since the place was described as small and old.

Sixty pounds for a single month!

The price staggered them.

Mrs. Arbuthnot immediately visualized boots: endless rows of sturdy boots that sixty pounds could buy for the parish children. In addition to the rent, they would have to pay the servants' wages, food, and train tickets there and back. As for references, they seemed like an impossible obstacle. It felt impossible to provide them without making their secret vacation plans public.

Both of them had wanted to keep things quiet. Even Mrs. Arbuthnot, tempted for once to be less than completely honest because it saved so much trouble, agreed that they should tell their respective social circles (which luckily didn't overlap) that they were going to stay with a friend who owned a house in Italy. It was true as far as it went—Mrs. Wilkins insisted it was perfectly true, though Mrs. Arbuthnot had her doubts. Mrs. Wilkins said it was the only way to keep Mellersh quiet. Spending her own money just to travel to Italy would make him furious; what he would say if he knew she was renting a medieval castle, she preferred not to think. It would take him days to say it all—even though it was her own money, and not a single penny of it had ever belonged to him.

Mrs. Arbuthnot said nothing, because her reason for hiding the trip from Frederick was the exact opposite. Frederick would be only too happy to see her go. He wouldn't mind at all. In fact, he would find her sudden self-indulgence and worldly behavior amusing. He would mockingly tell her to have a great time and not to rush back, with a crushing lack of interest. To her, it was far better to be missed and complained about by a husband like Mellersh than to be pushed out the door with Frederick's detachment. Being missed and needed, for whatever reason, was better than the complete loneliness of not being missed or needed at all.

So she remained silent, letting Mrs. Wilkins jump to her own conclusions. Still, for a whole day, they both felt their only choice was to give up the medieval castle. It was in making this bitter decision that they realized just how desperately they wanted to go.

Then, Mrs. Arbuthnot—who was skilled at finding ways out of difficulties—came up with a solution to the reference problem. At the same time, Mrs. Wilkins had a vision that showed them how to reduce the rent.

Mrs. Arbuthnot's plan was simple and highly successful. She took the entire rent in person to the owner, drawing it directly out of her Savings Bank. Once again, she felt secretive and apologetic, as if the bank clerk knew she was spending the money on personal pleasure. Carrying the sixty pounds in ten-pound notes in her handbag, she went to the owner's address near the Brompton Oratory and paid the full amount upfront, waiving her right to pay only half in advance. When the owner saw her soft dark eyes, her parted hair, her plain clothes, and heard her serious voice, he told her not to worry about references.

Rose Arbuthnot. Her letter making the appointment lay on the table. It was a pretty name.

So that difficulty was solved. But there was still the other problem: the heavy toll the rent took on their savings. This was especially true for Mrs. Wilkins, whose savings compared to Mrs. Arbuthnot's were like a tiny quail egg compared to a large duck egg. This problem was solved by a sudden idea of Mrs. Wilkins's: they would advertise in the personal column of The Times for two more ladies to join them and share the expenses.

This would immediately cut their rent share from a half to a quarter. Of course, they realized they could reduce the rent even more by inviting more people. They could invite six more ladies instead of two, since there were eight beds in total. Besides, they felt that too many people would ruin the peace. They were going to San Salvatore for quiet, rest, and joy, and six other women—especially in their bedrooms—might disrupt that.
