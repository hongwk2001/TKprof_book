Chapter 11


The sweet scents floating everywhere around San Salvatore were enough on their own to bring peace. They drifted into the living room from the flowers on the stone walls, blending with the fragrance of the flowers inside. Mrs. Wilkins felt as if she could almost see the scents greeting each other with a holy kiss. Who could stay angry surrounded by such gentleness? Who could be greedy or selfish, in that tense old London way, when faced with such generous beauty?

Yet Mrs. Fisher seemed to be all three of those things.

There was so much beauty, far more than enough for everyone, that trying to claim a private corner of it seemed like a pointless waste of time.

Yet Mrs. Fisher was trying to do exactly that, blocking off a section of it for her own private use.

Well, she would get over that soon. Mrs. Wilkins was sure that after a day or two in this incredibly peaceful atmosphere, she would inevitably change her mind.

For now, though, she clearly hadn't even begun to relax. She stood looking at her and Rose with an angry expression. Anger! Just imagine. What a silly, stressful London feeling, thought Mrs. Wilkins. In her own mind, the room was full of imaginary kisses, and everyone in it was being kissed—including Mrs. Fisher, just as much as she and Rose.

<Lotty>“You don't like us being in here,”</Lotty> Mrs. Wilkins said, standing up and immediately speaking the truth, as she always did. <Lotty>“Why?”</Lotty>

<Fisher>“I would have thought,”</Fisher> Mrs. Fisher said, leaning on her cane, <Fisher>“that you could see this is my room.”</Fisher>

<Lotty>“You mean because of the photos,”</Lotty> Mrs. Wilkins said.

Mrs. Arbuthnot, whose face was a little red with surprise, stood up as well.

<Fisher>“And the writing paper,”</Fisher> Mrs. Fisher said. <Fisher>“Stationery with my London address on it. And that pen—”</Fisher>

She pointed. The pen was still in Mrs. Wilkins's hand.

<Lotty>“Is yours. I'm very sorry,”</Lotty> Mrs. Wilkins said, putting it down on the table. She added with a smile that she had just used it to write some very nice things.

<Rose>“But why,”</Rose> asked Mrs. Arbuthnot, who couldn't bring herself to agree to Mrs. Fisher's demands without a quiet fight, <Rose>“shouldn't we be in here? It's a living room.”</Rose>

<Fisher>“There is another living room,”</Fisher> Mrs. Fisher said. <Fisher>“You and your friend can't sit in two rooms at once. Since I have no desire to disturb you in yours, I don't see why you should want to disturb me in mine.”</Fisher>

<Rose>“But why—”</Rose> Mrs. Arbuthnot started again.

<Lotty>“It's completely natural,”</Lotty> Mrs. Wilkins interrupted, noticing that Rose was looking stubborn. Turning to Mrs. Fisher, she said that while sharing things with friends was nice, she understood that Mrs. Fisher was still stuck in her old London habits and didn't want to share yet. But she assured her that she would lose that attitude soon and feel completely different. <Lotty>“Soon you'll want us to share,”</Lotty> Mrs. Wilkins said reassuringly. <Lotty>“Why, you might even end up asking me to use your pen if you knew I didn't have one.”</Lotty>

Mrs. Fisher was almost too angry to speak. To have this messy young woman from Hampstead patting her on the back, so to speak, and cheerfully predicting that she would improve, offended her more deeply than anything had since she first discovered her late husband wasn't the man she thought he was. Mrs. Wilkins definitely needed to be put in her place. But how? The woman seemed completely bulletproof. Right now, for instance, she was smiling as pleasantly and calmly as if she hadn't said anything rude at all. Would she even realize she was being disciplined? If she didn't, if she was too thick-skinned to feel it, then what could she do? Nothing but avoid her—which meant, exactly, having her own private room.

<Fisher>“I am an old woman,”</Fisher> Mrs. Fisher said, <Fisher>“and I need a room to myself. I can't get around easily because of my cane. Since I can't move around, I have to sit. Why shouldn't I sit quietly and undisturbed, just as I told you in London I planned to? If people are going to come in and out all day, talking and leaving doors open, you will have broken our agreement, which was that I was to have peace and quiet.”</Fisher>

<Lotty>“But we have no desire to—”</Lotty> Mrs. Arbuthnot began, but she was cut off by Mrs. Wilkins again.

<Lotty>“We are more than happy for you to have this room if it makes you comfortable,”</Lotty> Mrs. Wilkins said. <Lotty>“We just didn't know, that's all. We wouldn't have come in if we had—at least, not until you invited us. I'm sure you will invite us soon,”</Lotty> she finished, looking down at Mrs. Fisher with a cheerful smile. She picked up her letter, took Mrs. Arbuthnot's hand, and pulled her toward the door.

Mrs. Arbuthnot didn't want to leave. Normally the gentlest of women, she was filled with a strange and rather unchristian urge to stay and argue. Not aggressively, of course. She just wanted to reason with Mrs. Fisher patiently. She felt that something had to be said, and that she shouldn't let herself be scolded and kicked out like a schoolgirl caught misbehaving by a teacher.

But Mrs. Wilkins pulled her firmly out of the room. Once again, Rose was amazed by Lotty's poise, her sweet and even temper—especially since she had been so moody in England. Ever since they arrived in Italy, Lotty seemed like the more mature one. She was incredibly happy, absolutely blissful. Did happiness make a person that strong, that wise? Rose was happy too, but not nearly as happy. Clearly not, because not only did she want to fight with Mrs. Fisher, but she also wanted something else—something more than this beautiful place to make it complete. She wanted Frederick. For the first time in her life, she was surrounded by perfect beauty, and her only thought was to show it to him and share it with him. She wanted Frederick. She yearned for him. If only Frederick were here...

<Lotty>“Poor old lady,”</Lotty> Mrs. Wilkins said, gently closing the door on Mrs. Fisher and her small victory. <Lotty>“Imagine being like that on a day like this.”</Lotty>

<Rose>“She is a very rude old woman,”</Rose> Mrs. Arbuthnot said.

<Lotty>“She'll get over it. I'm just sorry we chose her room to sit in.”</Lotty>

<Rose>“It's the nicest room by far,”</Rose> Mrs. Arbuthnot said. <Rose>“And it doesn't belong to her.”</Rose>

<Lotty>“Oh, but there are plenty of other places, and she's just a lonely old lady. Let her have it. What does it matter?”</Lotty>

Mrs. Wilkins then said she was heading down to the village to find the post office and mail her letter to Mellersh, and she asked Rose to join her.

<Lotty>“I've been thinking about Mellersh,”</Lotty> Mrs. Wilkins said as they walked single file down the narrow, winding path they had climbed in the rain the night before.

She walked in front, and Mrs. Arbuthnot followed naturally. In England, it had been the exact opposite—Lotty had been shy and hesitant, except for her awkward outbursts, always hiding behind the calm, sensible Rose.

<Lotty>“I've been thinking about Mellersh,”</Lotty> Mrs. Wilkins repeated over her shoulder, since Rose hadn't replied.

<Rose>“Have you?”</Rose> Rose said with a hint of dislike. Her interactions with Mellersh hadn't been pleasant, so she didn't enjoy thinking about him. She had lied to him, which made her dislike him. She didn't realize that was the reason, and simply thought he lacked any spiritual goodness. Yet she scolded herself for thinking that, realizing it was judgmental. No doubt Lotty's husband was much closer to God than she would ever be. Still, she just didn't like him.

<Lotty>“I've been a terrible person,”</Lotty> Mrs. Wilkins said.

<Rose>“A what?”</Rose> Mrs. Arbuthnot asked, not sure she had heard correctly.

<Lotty>“Leaving him behind in that gloomy place while I enjoy myself in paradise. He had actually planned to take me to Italy for Easter himself. Did I mention that?”</Lotty>

<Rose>“No,”</Rose> Rose said. She had actively avoided talking about husbands. Whenever Lotty started to share personal details, Rose had quickly changed the subject. She felt that talking about one husband always led to talking about another, and she absolutely refused to talk about Frederick. Aside from the simple fact that he existed, he hadn't been mentioned. Mellersh had only been discussed because he stood in the way of their trip, but Rose had made sure not to let the conversation linger on him.

<Lotty>“Well, he did,”</Lotty> Mrs. Wilkins said. <Lotty>“He had never suggested anything like that in his life, and I was shocked. Imagine—just as I had planned to come here on my own.”</Lotty>

She stopped on the path and looked back up at Rose.

<Rose>“I see,”</Rose> Rose said, trying to think of a new topic.

<Lotty>“So you see why I feel so guilty. He wanted to take me on a trip to Italy, and I planned to come to Italy and leave him at home. Honestly,”</Lotty> she went on, looking closely at Rose, <Lotty>“Mellersh has every right to be angry and hurt.”</Lotty>

Mrs. Arbuthnot was amazed. She was unsettled by how quickly Lotty was becoming completely selfless right before her eyes. She was turning into a saint. Here she was feeling affectionate toward Mellersh—the same Mellersh who, only that morning as they dipped their feet in the sea, had seemed like a distant illusion, a fading vapor, as Lotty had called him. That was just this morning, and by lunchtime Lotty had brought him back to reality enough to write him a long letter. Now, a few minutes later, she was saying he had every right to be hurt and angry, and calling herself a mean dog—a strange way to put it, but it showed she was genuinely sorry.

Rose stared at her in wonder. If she kept going like this, she would have a halo around her head soon—in fact, she almost did, if you didn't realize it was just the sunlight filtering through the trees and catching her reddish hair.

Lotty seemed filled with a massive urge to love and be friends with everyone, a desire for pure goodness. In Rose's experience, being good was hard work. It took a long time and a lot of struggle; in fact, you never really arrived, or if you did, it was only for a brief moment. You had to fight constantly to stay on the right path, which was full of doubt. Lotty, however, was flying. Rose noted that Lotty hadn't lost her impulsive nature; it had just found a new outlet. She was impulsively becoming a saint. Could someone really achieve goodness so quickly and intensely? Wouldn't there be an equally strong backlash?

<Rose>“I wouldn't,”</Rose> Rose said cautiously, looking down into Lotty's bright eyes—since the path was steep, Lotty was standing much lower than she was—<Rose>“I wouldn't be too sure of that just yet.”</Rose>

<Lotty>“But I am sure of it, and I've already written to tell him.”</Lotty>

Rose stared at her. <Rose>“But just this morning—”</Rose> she started.

<Lotty>“It's all in here,”</Lotty> Lotty interrupted, tapping the letter and looking pleased.

<Rose>“What—everything?”</Rose>

<Lotty>“You mean about the ad and spending my savings? Oh, no—not yet. I'll explain all that when he gets here.”</Lotty>

<Rose>“When he gets here?”</Rose> Rose repeated.

<Lotty>“I invited him to come and stay with us.”</Lotty>

Rose could only stare at her in shock.

<Rose>“It's the least I could do. Besides, look at this place!”</Rose> Lotty waved her hand toward the scenery. <Rose>“It would be awful not to share it. I was wrong to leave him behind, but it would be even worse if I didn't try to get Mellersh to come out and enjoy this too. It's only fair that he gets some pleasure from my savings. After all, he has supported me for years. I shouldn't be selfish.”</Rose>

<Lotty>“But do you think he'll actually come?”</Lotty>

<Lotty>“Oh, I hope so,”</Lotty> Lotty said with deep sincerity, adding, <Lotty>“Poor guy.”</Lotty>

Hearing that, Rose wanted to sit down. Mellersh, a poor guy? The same Mellersh who had been just a fading illusion a few hours ago? There was a bench at the curve of the path, so Rose walked over and sat down. She needed to catch her breath and think. Maybe she could slow Lotty down before she made a mistake she would regret. Mellersh at San Salvatore? The husband Lotty had worked so hard to escape just a short time ago?

<Lotty>“I see him here,”</Lotty> Lotty said, as if reading her mind.

Rose looked at her with genuine worry. Every time Lotty confidently said, <Lotty>“I see,”</Lotty> it actually happened. That meant Mr. Wilkins would probably be arriving soon.

<Rose>“I wish I understood you,”</Rose> Rose said anxiously.

<Lotty>“Don't try,”</Lotty> Lotty said, smiling.

<Rose>“But I have to, because I care about you.”</Rose>

<Lotty>“Dear Rose,”</Lotty> Lotty said, quickly leaning down to kiss her.

<Rose>“You change so fast,”</Rose> Rose said. <Rose>“I can't keep up with you. That's what happened with Freder—”</Rose>

She stopped mid-word, looking frightened.

<Lotty>“The whole point of coming here,”</Lotty> she continued quickly, since Lotty didn't seem to notice the slip, <Lotty>“was to get away, wasn't it? Well, we did. And now, after only one day, you want to write to the exact people—”</Lotty>

She paused.

<Lotty>“The very people we were escaping,”</Lotty> Lotty finished for her. <Lotty>“It's true. It seems totally illogical. But I'm so happy and healthy, and I feel so good. This place—it just floods me with love.”</Lotty>

She looked down at Rose, looking wonderfully surprised by her own feelings.

Rose was silent for a moment. Then she asked, <Rose>“And do you think it will have the same effect on Mr. Wilkins?”</Rose>

Lotty laughed. <Lotty>“I don't know,”</Lotty> she said. <Lotty>“But even if it doesn't, there is enough love here to flood fifty Mr. Wilkinses, as you call him. The important thing is to have plenty of love around. Out here, I realize it doesn't matter who is doing the loving, as long as someone is. At home I was selfish, always keeping score. I was obsessed with fairness. As if fairness mattered. As if you can really tell the difference between justice and revenge. Only love actually helps. At home, I refused to love Mellersh unless he loved me back just as much—total equality. Can you believe it? Since he didn't, I didn't either, and our home was so empty and dry! Just completely lifeless...”</Lotty>

Rose didn't say anything. She was confused by Lotty. One strange effect of San Salvatore on her friend was that she had started using much stronger words than she ever did in Hampstead. Words like 'beast' and 'dog' were not the sort of language she used back home. Even in her speech, Lotty had been set free.

But Rose wished with all her heart that she could write to her husband too and tell him to come. The Wilkinses' marriage, even if Mellersh was pompous—and he had seemed very pompous to Rose—was healthier and more normal than her own. Lotty could write to Mellersh and get a reply. Rose couldn't write to Frederick, because she knew he wouldn't write back. Or if he did, it would be a rushed note showing how bored he was, with a polite thank-you. That would be worse than silence. Seeing his handwriting on an envelope addressed to her would break her heart. It would remind her too painfully of the letters from the beginning of their relationship, when he was miserable without her, full of love and longing. To receive a letter that looked just like those old ones, only to open it and read:

“Dear Rose—Thanks for the letter. Glad you're having a good time. Don't rush back. Let me know if you need money. Everything is going great here.—Yours, Frederick.”

—no, she couldn't bear that.

<Lotty>“I don't think I'll go to the village with you today,”</Lotty> she said, looking up at Lotty with tears in her eyes. <Lotty>“I need to think.”</Lotty>

<Lotty>“All right,”</Lotty> Lotty said, immediately walking briskly down the path. <Lotty>“But don't think too long,”</Lotty> she called back. <Lotty>“Write and invite him now.”</Lotty>

<Rose>“Invite who?”</Rose> Rose asked, startled.

<Lotty>“Your husband.”</Lotty>
