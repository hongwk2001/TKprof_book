Chapter 10


There was no way in or out of the upper garden at San Salvatore except through the two glass doors of the dining room and the hall, which unfortunately sat side by side. Anyone in the garden who wanted to escape unseen couldn't, because they would run straight into the person they were trying to avoid. The garden was small and rectangular, making it impossible to hide. The few trees—the Judas tree, the tamarisk, and the pine—grew close to the low walls. The rose bushes offered no real cover; if you took one step to the side, you were easily seen. The only private spot was in the northwest corner, where a small stone balcony jutted out from the main wall. It was probably used as a lookout point in the old days, and you could sit there completely hidden because of a thick clump of daphne bushes between it and the house.

After checking to make sure no one was looking, Scrap picked up her chair and carried it into this hidden spot, tiptoeing as quietly as if she were committing a crime. There was a similar balcony on the northeast corner of the wall, and though it had an even more beautiful view of the bay and the mountains behind Mezzago, it was completely open. It had no bushes or shade. So she decided to sit in the northwest corner, settling into her chair, resting her head on a cushion, and putting her feet up on the wall. To the villagers in the square below, her white slippers looked like two white doves resting on the wall. She thought she was finally safe.

Mrs. Fisher found her anyway, guided by the smell of her cigarette. Scrap hadn't thought about that. Mrs. Fisher didn't smoke, which made her very sensitive to the smell of tobacco. The strong scent hit her as soon as she walked into the garden after lunch to have her coffee. She had told Francesca to serve the coffee in the shade just outside the glass door. When Mrs. Wilkins saw the table being set up and tactlessly reminded her that Lady Caroline wanted to be alone, Mrs. Fisher sharply—and correctly—replied that the garden belonged to everyone.

She walked into the garden and immediately noticed that Lady Caroline was smoking. She muttered to herself, 'These modern young women,' and went to find her. Now that lunch was over and her meal was finished, her walking stick didn't seem to slow her down at all.

No one entertained her anymore, Mrs. Fisher thought, heading straight for the daphne bushes. The world had grown dull and lost its sense of humor. People still made jokes, of course, but they were different now. The writer Thackeray would have shredded this generation. Young people had no idea how much they needed a sharp critic like him, and she had heard they didn't even respect his work anymore. Well, she couldn't force them to understand, but she could at least give Lady Caroline a good dose of advice.

'I hear you're not feeling well,' she said, standing at the entrance of the balcony and looking down at the still, seemingly asleep Scrap. Mrs. Fisher spoke in a deep, masculine voice—a trait that sometimes happens to women in their later years.

Scrap tried to pretend she was asleep, but if she had been, the cigarette would have fallen from her fingers. She forgot about this detail, but Mrs. Fisher didn't. She walked onto the balcony and sat down on a narrow stone bench built into the wall, knowing she could only sit there for a little while before the cold stone chilled her.

She looked at the girl. She was definitely beautiful, the kind of person who would have been popular at the poet Tennyson's home. It was strange how easily even the greatest men were influenced by looks. She had seen Tennyson turn his back on a crowd of important people just to talk at the window with an unknown young woman whose only quality was her beauty. Beauty faded so quickly, yet while it lasted, it seemed to control men. Even husbands weren't immune; there had been moments in Mr. Fisher's life too...

'I expect the travel has upset your stomach,' she said in her deep voice. 'What you need is a dose of simple medicine. I'll ask Domenico if he can find castor oil in the village.' Scrap opened her eyes and looked right at Mrs. Fisher. 'Ah,' Mrs. Fisher said, 'I knew you weren't asleep. If you had been, you would have dropped your cigarette.' Scrap threw the cigarette over the wall. 'That's a waste,' Mrs. Fisher said. 'I don't like women smoking, but I hate waste even more.'

'What do you do with people like this?' Scrap thought, staring at Mrs. Fisher in annoyance. But to Mrs. Fisher, the look seemed like sweet politeness. 'Now, take my advice,' Mrs. Fisher said, feeling touched, 'and don't ignore this. We're in Italy, and you have to be careful. To start with, you should go to bed.' 'I never go to bed,' Scrap snapped, and her voice sounded so sad and lonely that it reminded Mrs. Fisher of a play she saw as a girl, where a character named Poor Jo cried, 'I'm always moving on.' Mrs. Fisher had wept during that scene.

Scrap's voice was beautiful. For the past ten years, it had made everything she said sound important and memorable. She should have been a singer, but she couldn't sing; her voice was only musical when she spoke, and it had a magical effect. Because she was so beautiful, every man who saw her looked at her with intense interest, and once they heard her voice, they were completely hooked. It was the same with every man—educated or not, old or young, bus conductors, soldiers, or generals during the war. Even priests and bishops were affected. It didn't matter who they were, or how happily married they were; every man fell under her spell the moment they saw and heard her.

Scrap was tired of that look. It only caused problems. At first she had enjoyed it, feeling excited and successful. It was pleasant to be adored, listened to, and praised wherever she went, and to have proud parents at home. And it was so easy—she didn't have to work hard or learn anything. She just had to show up and speak. But over time, things changed. She found she had to defend herself. That look in men's eyes meant they wanted to possess her. Some were more polite than others, but they all tried to win her over. She had started out confident and trusting of older people, but she quickly began to distrust and dislike them, shrinking away in anger. She felt like she didn't even own herself, but was treated as a public attraction. Men were exhausting, and she often found herself in strange arguments and being disliked by other women. When the war started and she joined the effort, it made things even worse.

The war had devastated Scrap. It killed the only man she had ever felt safe with, the one she would have married, and it made her hate the idea of love. Since then, she had been bitter. She felt trapped in her glamorous life, like a wasp stuck in honey, trying desperately to free herself. She didn't care about competing with other women or taking their men. What would she even do with a man? None of them would talk to her about anything other than romance, which got boring very quickly. It was like feeding someone nothing but sugar when they wanted real food. Love made her want to slap someone. 'Why should I love you?' she would ask when men tried to propose to her. But she never got a straight answer.

Scrap became deeply cynical. She felt old and disillusioned inside, even though she still looked beautiful on the outside. She worried about her future. She felt useless, having wasted her life just being pretty. Eventually she would lose her looks, and then what? The thought terrified her. Even though she was tired of the attention, she was used to it. Becoming ordinary and fading away would be painful. And once she started aging, she would have decades of it. Imagine spending most of your life being old instead of young. It was stupid. Everything was stupid. There was nothing she wanted to do, and a thousand things she didn't want to do. She just wanted to be left alone in silence and privacy. Yet here she was, unable to get a minute of peace because this ridiculous woman wanted to control her by making her go to bed and drink castor oil.

'I'm sure you will be reasonable,' Mrs. Fisher said, feeling the cold bench and knowing she couldn't sit there much longer. 'Your mother would want—do you have a mother?' Scrap looked at her in surprise. Of course she had a mother; her mother was a prominent duchess who had held high positions at Court. Her father had also been a very famous figure, though he was older now and had made some mistakes during the war. It was incredibly refreshing to find someone who didn't know her family at all, or hadn't connected her to them yet.

She began to like Mrs. Fisher. Perhaps the other two ladies didn't know who she was either. When she first wrote to them and signed her name, Dester—a family name famous in English history—she assumed they would recognize it. At their interview in London, she thought they didn't ask for references because they knew who she was. Scrap started to feel better. If no one here knew her, she could spend a month forgetting her hectic life and the endless attention. Maybe she could actually use this time to think, clear her mind, and make some decisions.

'What I want to do here is make some decisions,' she said, leaning forward and looking up at Mrs. Fisher. She was so pleased that Mrs. Fisher didn't know her that she felt a spark of energy. 'That's all. It's not too much to ask, is it?' She looked at Mrs. Fisher, thinking that any decision would do, as long as she stopped drifting through life.

Mrs. Fisher looked at her. 'I would say that what a young woman like you needs is a husband and children.' 'Well, that's one of the things I'm going to think about,' Scrap said politely. 'But I don't think that's a final answer.'

'In the meantime,' Mrs. Fisher said, getting up as the cold bench chilled her, 'I wouldn't worry about decisions and deep thoughts. Women's brains weren't made for thinking, I assure you. Go to bed and rest.' 'I am perfectly fine,' Scrap said.

'Then why did you send a message saying you were sick?' 'I didn't.' 'Then I walked all the way out here for nothing.' 'But wouldn't you rather find me healthy than sick?' Scrap smiled. Even Mrs. Fisher couldn't resist the smile. 'Well, you are a lovely girl,' she said forgivingly. 'It's a pity you weren't born fifty years ago. My friends would have loved to look at you.'

'I'm glad I wasn't,' Scrap said. 'I hate being stared at.' 'Ridiculous,' Mrs. Fisher said, turning strict again. 'That is what young women like you are made for. What else, indeed? And if my friends had looked at you, you would have been admired by very important people.' 'I don't like important people,' Scrap said, frowning as she remembered a recent unpleasant event.

'What I dislike is the attitude of modern young women,' Mrs. Fisher said coldly. 'It seems absolutely pitiful in its foolishness.' She walked away, her stick crunching on the gravel path.

'That's fine,' Scrap thought, relaxing back into her chair with her feet up. As long as people left her alone, she didn't care why they went.

'Don't you think Scrap is acting a little strange?' her mother had asked her father recently, noticed her quiet behavior and how she avoided everyone except very young men. 'What? Strange? Let her act however she wants. A girl that beautiful can do whatever she pleases,' her father replied, completely infatuated. 'I do let her,' her mother said quietly; after all, she couldn't stop her anyway.

Mrs. Fisher regretted worrying about Lady Caroline. She walked down the hall toward her private sitting room, her stick clacking sharply on the stone floor. She had no patience for the dramatic attitudes of young people. Unable to accomplish anything themselves, they tried to look smart by criticizing everything good and praising everything bad just to be different. 'Idiots,' Mrs. Fisher thought. 'Idiots.' When she entered her sitting room, she found more intrusions. Mrs. Arbuthnot was calmly drinking coffee, while Mrs. Wilkins was sitting at the desk—the desk she considered her own—writing a letter using Mrs. Fisher's personal pen. 'Isn't this a lovely room?' Mrs. Arbuthnot said warmly. 'We just found it.' 'I'm writing to Mellersh,' Mrs. Wilkins said cheerfully, turning around. Mrs. Fisher didn't care at all who Mellersh was. 'He will want to know that I arrived safely,' Mrs. Wilkins added, feeling optimistic in this beautiful place.
