Chapter 7


Their eyes followed her admiringly. They had no idea they had just been snubbed. Of course, it was a disappointment to find she had beaten them to it, depriving them of the joy of preparing her room and seeing her reaction when she first arrived. But at least they still had Mrs. Fisher. They would focus on Mrs. Fisher and watch her face instead—though, like everyone else, they would have preferred to watch Lady Caroline's.

Since Lady Caroline had mentioned breakfast, they figured they should go eat too. There was far too much to do that day to spend any more time looking at the views—they had to meet the servants, inspect the house, and finally get Mrs. Fisher's room ready and decorated.

They waved cheerfully at Lady Caroline, who seemed too absorbed in the view to notice. Turning away, they saw that the maid from the night before had walked up silently behind them in cloth slippers with rope soles.

She was Francesca, the elderly maid who had worked for the owner for years, making a property inventory unnecessary. After wishing them good morning and hoping they had slept well, she told them that breakfast was ready in the dining room downstairs and asked them to follow her.

They didn't understand a single word of Francesca's lengthy Italian explanation, but they followed her anyway because it was obvious they were supposed to. Walking downstairs and through a wide hallway—which looked just like the one upstairs, except it had glass doors opening to the garden instead of a window—they were shown into the dining room. There, sitting at the head of the table and eating breakfast, was Mrs. Fisher.

This time, they both gasped. Even Mrs. Arbuthnot let out an surprised <Rose>"Oh."</Rose>

Mrs. Wilkins had more to say. <Lotty>"Why, it's like having the bread snatched right out of our mouths!"</Lotty> she exclaimed.

<Other>"How do you do,"</Other> Mrs. Fisher said. <Other>"I can't get up because of my walking stick."</Other> She reached out her hand across the table.

<Other>"Yes,"</Other> Mrs. Fisher replied, returning to her breakfast. <Other>"Yes, I am here."</Other> She calmly cracked the top off her soft-boiled egg.

<Lotty>"It's a big disappointment,"</Lotty> Mrs. Wilkins said. <Lotty>"We were planning to give you such a warm welcome."</Lotty>

Mrs. Fisher glanced at her and remembered that this was the woman who claimed to have seen Keats during their meeting in London. She decided she had to be careful with her and set boundaries right from the start.

<Lotty>"It's really too bad,"</Lotty> Mrs. Wilkins said, completely ignoring the snub. <Lotty>"Now there's no one left for us to prepare for. I feel disappointed, like someone took away my food just as I was about to enjoy eating it."</Lotty>

<Fisher>"Where would you like to sit?"</Fisher> Mrs. Fisher asked Mrs. Arbuthnot, pointedly ignoring Mrs. Wilkins. The comparison to food and mouths seemed very vulgar to her.

<Rose>"Oh, thank you—"</Rose> Mrs. Arbuthnot said, sitting down quickly next to her. There were only two empty seats, one on either side of Mrs. Fisher. She took one, and Mrs. Wilkins sat down opposite her in the other.

Mrs. Fisher sat at the head of the table, surrounded by the coffee and tea pots. Even though they were all sharing the expenses of San Salvatore equally, Mrs. Arbuthnot couldn't help reflecting that she and Lotty were the ones who had found the place, done the work of renting it, and agreed to let Mrs. Fisher join them. Without them, Mrs. Fisher wouldn't even be here. In a way, Mrs. Fisher was their guest. There was no official host, but if there were, it should have been either herself or Lotty, not Mrs. Fisher or Lady Caroline. Mrs. Arbuthnot felt this quite clearly as she sat down. Mrs. Fisher hovered her hand over the pots—the same hand that the famous writer Ruskin had once shaken—and asked, <Fisher>"Tea or coffee?"</Fisher> The feeling grew stronger when Mrs. Fisher rang a small gong on the table as if she owned the place, and when Francesca appeared, ordered her in formal Italian to bring more milk. Mrs. Fisher had a strange way of acting like she was in charge, and if Mrs. Arbuthnot hadn't been so happy, she might have been annoyed by it.

Mrs. Wilkins noticed it too, but it only made her random thoughts turn to cuckoos—birds that take over other birds' nests. If she had been as nervous and shy as during their first meeting, she probably would have blurted out something incoherent and embarrassing about cuckoos. But her happiness had cured her shyness. She felt very calm and in control of what she said, without the fear of saying something foolish. She was completely at ease and natural. Her disappointment about not welcoming Mrs. Fisher vanished immediately, since it was impossible to feel down in such a paradise. She didn't even mind Mrs. Fisher playing the host. What did it matter? You didn't care about things like that here. So she and Mrs. Arbuthnot sat down happily on either side of Mrs. Fisher. The sun flooded the room through two east-facing windows, and an open door led to a garden filled with beautiful flowers, especially freesias.

The sweet, delicate scent of the freesias drifted through the door. Back in London, freesias were far too expensive for her. Sometimes she would go into a flower shop just to ask the price so she could smell them, knowing they cost a ridiculous amount for just a few stems. Here, they grew everywhere—sprouting from every corner and covering the rose beds. Imagine being able to pick armfuls of freesias whenever you wanted, in the warm sunshine, wearing a summer dress, and all on the first of April!

<Lotty>"I suppose you realize that we've arrived in heaven, don't you?"</Lotty> she said, smiling at Mrs. Fisher like a fellow angel.

"They are much younger than I expected," Mrs. Fisher thought, "and not nearly as plain." She ignored Mrs. Wilkins's excitement and thought back to how quickly and nervously they had refused to exchange references when they met at her house in London.

Of course, nothing anyone did could affect Mrs. Fisher's solid respectability. She had the backing of the three famous names she had offered as references, and many others she could call on. Even if these young women turned out to be unreliable eccentrics (and she had no proof that the girl in the garden was actually Lady Caroline Dester; she had only been told she was), why should she care? Let them do what they wanted. Being sixty-five had its advantages. Beside, it was only for four weeks, after which she would never see them again. In the meantime, there were plenty of quiet places where she could sit away from them and reminisce. She also had her own private sitting room, a charming space with honey-colored furniture, paintings, windows looking toward Genoa, and a door leading to the castle walls. The house had two sitting rooms, and she had explained to Lady Caroline (who was indeed very pretty, and whom the poet Tennyson would have loved to take for walks on the hills) that she needed the honey-colored room all to herself because of her walking stick.

<Fisher>"No one wants to see an old woman hobbling around everywhere,"</Fisher> she had said. <Fisher>"I'll be perfectly happy spending most of my time alone in here or sitting out on the terrace."</Fisher>

She had a very nice bedroom too. It had windows facing both the morning sun over the bay and the garden. She and Lady Caroline had discovered that these were the only two bedrooms with double views, and they were by far the most spacious. Each had two beds, so they immediately had the extra beds moved into the other rooms to make more space. Lady Caroline had turned her room into a bed-sitting room by moving in a sofa, a desk, and a comfortable chair. Mrs. Fisher didn't need to do that since she had her own sitting room. Lady Caroline had initially thought of taking the main living room for herself, suggesting that the other two women could use the dining room between meals since it was very pleasant. But she hadn't liked the shape of the main living room—it was a dark, round room in the tower with narrow windows in the thick walls and a ceiling shaped like an open umbrella. Lady Caroline had clearly wanted the honey-colored sitting room instead, and would have taken it if Mrs. Fisher hadn't stood her ground. That would have been ridiculous.

Beds, too, were discussed with caution, and proper manners prevented anyone from speaking of husbands and beds in the same sentence.

<Rose>"No, thank you. But won't you have some?"</Rose>

<Fisher>"No, thank you. I never drink more than two cups. Would you like an orange?"</Fisher>

<Rose>"No, thank you. Would you?"</Rose>

<Rose>"Yes, thank you. Have you?"</Rose>

Mrs. Fisher paused before replying. Was this a habit—this annoying trick of repeating a question back? If so, she would have to stop it, because no one could live comfortably for a month with someone who had such an irritating habit.

She looked at Mrs. Arbuthnot's gentle face and felt reassured. No, it was probably just an accident, not a habit. She couldn't imagine Mrs. Arbuthnot having annoying habits any more than a gentle dove. Looking at her, she thought she would make a wonderful wife for the writer Thomas Carlyle, much better than his actual, sharp-tongued wife Jane. She would have calmed him down.
