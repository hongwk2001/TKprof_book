Chapter 19

And then when she spoke... what chance did poor Briggs stand? He was completely infatuated. All Scrap said was "How do you do" when Mr. Wilkins introduced him, but it was enough to completely undo Briggs.

From a cheerful, talkative, happy young man full of life and friendliness, he became quiet and serious, with sweat dripping from his forehead. He also became clumsy, dropping the teaspoon as he handed her a cup and spilling the cookies so that one rolled on the floor. He couldn't take his eyes off her beautiful face for a second. Meanwhile, Mr. Wilkins explained the situation to Lady Caroline since Briggs was too tongue-tied to do so himself. He told her that Mr. Briggs owned San Salvatore, was traveling to Rome but had stopped at Mezzago, and that the other three ladies had invited him to stay the night in his own house instead of a hotel. He added that Briggs was just waiting for her approval as the fourth hostess. While Mr. Wilkins delivered this explanation in his clear, polished voice, Briggs just sat there without saying a single word.

A deep sadness washed over Scrap. The signs of an admirer who would try to cling to her were all too familiar, and she knew that if Briggs stayed, her peaceful vacation would be over.

Then she thought of Kate Lumley. She clung to the thought of Kate like a lifesaver.

"It would be lovely," she said, giving Briggs a faint smile. She had to be polite and smile at least a little, but even that small smile showed her dimple, making Briggs stare even harder. "I'm just wondering if there is enough room."

"Yes, there is," Lotty said. "There's Kate Lumley's room."

"I thought your friend was arriving immediately," Scrap said to Mrs. Fisher. To Briggs, her voice sounded like the most beautiful music he had ever heard.

"Oh, no," Mrs. Fisher replied, with a surprising calmness, Scrap noticed.

"Miss Lumley," Mr. Wilkins said, then turned to Mrs. Fisher, "or should I say Mrs.?"

"No one has ever married Kate," Mrs. Fisher said smugly.

"Quite so. Miss Lumley isn't arriving today anyway, Lady Caroline, and Mr. Briggs has to continue his trip tomorrow, so his stay won't interfere with Miss Lumley's plans."

"Then of course I join in the invitation," Scrap said, with what Briggs felt was the most divine warmth.

He stammered a reply, blushing bright red. Scrap thought, "Oh, boy," and turned her head away. But that just gave Briggs a view of her profile, and if anything was more beautiful than her face from the front, it was her profile.

Well, it was only for this one afternoon and evening. He would surely leave first thing in the morning. It took hours to get to Rome. It would be awful if he stayed until the night train. She had a feeling the main express to Rome passed through at night. Why hadn't that Kate Lumley woman arrived yet? She had forgotten all about her, but now she recalled she was supposed to have been invited two weeks ago. What had happened to her? If this man got too close, he would look for her in London and follow her around. Her experienced eye told her he had the potential to be a very persistent admirer.

"If," thought Mr. Wilkins, watching Briggs's face and sudden silence, "there was any connection between this young man and Mrs. Arbuthnot, there is going to be trouble now. It will be different from what I feared with Mr. Arbuthnot, but still the kind of problem that needs legal advice, even if it's not a public scandal. Briggs, infatuated by Lady Caroline's beauty, will try to win her over. She will naturally reject him. Mrs. Arbuthnot, ignored, will be upset. Her husband, when he arrives, will find his wife crying. If he asks why, she will be cold and distant. More trouble will follow, and they will turn to me for advice. When Lotty said Mrs. Arbuthnot wanted her husband, she was wrong. Mrs. Arbuthnot wants Briggs, and it looks like she won't get him. Well, I'm their man."

"Where are your things, Mr. Briggs?" Mrs. Fisher asked maternal-like. "Shouldn't they be brought here?" The sun was setting into the sea, and the damp, sweet smell of the April evening was starting to fill the garden.

Briggs snapped out of his trance. "My things?" he repeated. "Oh yes—I have to get them. They're at the station in Mezzago. I'll send Domenico. My carriage is waiting in the village. He can use it to go back. I'll go tell him."

He stood up. He was supposedly talking to Mrs. Fisher, but his eyes were glued to Scrap, who remained silent and looked down.

Then, catching himself, he stammered, "I'm so sorry—I keep forgetting—I'll go down and get them myself."

"We can easily send Domenico," Rose said. At her gentle voice, he turned to look at her.

Yes, there was his friend, the lady with the lovely name—but how much she seemed to have changed in just a few minutes! Was it the fading light that made her look so pale, dim, and ghostly? A kind ghost, of course, with a pretty name, but still just a shadow compared to Lady Caroline.

He turned back to Scrap and completely forgot Rose existed. How could he focus on anyone else when he was standing right in front of his dream come true?

Briggs had never believed anyone as beautiful as his ideal could actually exist. He had never even met anyone close to it. He had known plenty of pretty, charming women and appreciated them, but never anyone this breathtaking. He used to think he would die if he ever saw a perfectly beautiful woman. Although he didn't die upon meeting her, he became just as helpless as if he had.

The others had to arrange everything for him. By asking questions, they found out his luggage was at the station in Mezzago. They sent for Domenico, and with everyone's help except Scrap's—she sat silently, looking at no one—Briggs managed to give him instructions to go fetch his things.

It was a sad sight to see how quickly Briggs had fallen. Everyone noticed, even Rose.

"Honestly," Mrs. Fisher thought, "the way a pretty face can turn an intelligent man into a fool is beyond belief."

Feeling the air grow cold and finding Briggs's behavior painful to watch, she went inside to order his room prepared, now regretting that she had invited him to stay. She had briefly forgotten how Lady Caroline's beauty affected men, especially since Mr. Wilkins seemed immune to it. Poor boy. He was so charming on his own. It was true that Lady Caroline was ignoring him completely, but that didn't help. Men were like foolish moths, fluttering around the flame of a pretty face. She had seen it happen far too often. She almost laid a motherly hand on Briggs's blond head as she walked past him. Poor boy.

Then Scrap finished her cigarette, got up, and went inside too. She saw no reason to sit there just so Mr. Briggs could stare at her. She would have liked to stay out longer in her corner behind the bushes to watch the sunset, see the lights twinkle on in the village below, and enjoy the cool evening air, but she knew Mr. Briggs would follow her if she did.

The familiar nuisance had started again. Her peaceful holiday was disrupted—maybe even ruined, for who knew if he would actually leave tomorrow? He might leave the castle to make room for Kate Lumley, but he could easily rent a room in the village and come up every day. She hated how people tried to claim others this way. And she was so cursed with her looks that she couldn't even scowl at him without it being taken as a flirtation.

Scrap, who loved this time of evening in her private corner, felt annoyed with Mr. Briggs for ruining it. She turned her back on him and the garden and walked toward the house without a word or a look. But the moment Briggs realized she was leaving, he jumped up, moved chairs out of her way, kicked a footstool aside, rushed to hold the open door for her, and followed her down the hall.

What could she do with him? Well, it was his hallway; she couldn't stop him from walking in it.

"I hope," he said, unable to look away from her as they walked, causing him to bump into a bookcase, an old cupboard, and the hall table, spilling water from the flower vase, "that you are comfortable here? If you aren't, I'll... I'll fire everyone."

His voice shook. What was she going to do with him? She could hide in her room, pretend to be sick, and skip dinner, but that felt like letting him control her actions.

"I'm very comfortable indeed," Scrap said.

"If I had only known you were coming—" he began.

"It's a wonderful old place," Scrap said, trying to sound as cold and distant as possible, though she doubted it would work.

The kitchen was on the same floor, and as they passed the cracked door, the servants watched them. Their knowing looks said it all: they saw what was coming, understood it completely, and approved.

"Are you going upstairs?" Briggs asked as she stopped at the bottom of the steps.

"Yes."

"Which room do you spend time in? The drawing room or the small yellow room?"

"In my bedroom."

That meant he couldn't follow her up. All he could do was wait until she came back down.

He wanted to ask which room was hers—it thrilled him to hear her call a room in his house "her own"—so he could picture her in it. He wondered if it might be his former bedroom, but he didn't dare ask. He would find out later from Francesca or someone else.

"So I won't see you again until dinner?"

"Dinner is at eight," Scrap replied evasively as she walked up the stairs.

He watched her go.

She passed the Madonna painting, and the portrait that resembled Rose Arbuthnot seemed to fade into insignificance as she walked by.

As she turned the corner of the staircase, the setting sun caught her face through the window, making her look radiant.

Then she disappeared, the sun went down, and the staircase became dark and empty.

He listened until her footsteps faded, trying to guess which door closed. Then he wandered back through the hall and out to the upper garden.

Looking out her window, Scrap saw him there. She saw Lotty and Rose sitting on the far wall, where she wished she could be, and she saw Mr. Wilkins cornering Briggs, clearly telling him the story of the oleander tree in the middle of the garden.

Briggs listened patiently, which she thought was nice of him since it was his family's oleander and his father's story. She recognized the story from Mr. Wilkins's gestures. Domenico had told her shortly after she arrived, and he had told Mrs. Fisher, who passed it to Mr. Wilkins. Mrs. Fisher loved this story. It was about a cherrywood walking stick that Briggs's father had stuck into the ground, telling Domenico's father (the gardener then) to plant an oleander there. He left the stick as a reminder, and eventually, the stick itself sprouted into the oleander tree.

There was poor Mr. Briggs, patiently listening to a story he must have known since childhood.

He was probably thinking of something else. She feared he was. It was so unfortunate how people felt the need to possess and cling to others. If only they could learn to stand on their own. Why couldn't Mr. Briggs be more like Lotty, who never demanded anything from anyone, but was complete on her own and respected others? She loved being around Lotty, where she felt both free and supported. Mr. Briggs seemed like a nice guy, too; she thought she might actually like him if he didn't like her so much.

Scrap felt gloomy. Here she was, stuck in her hot bedroom because of Mr. Briggs, instead of enjoying the cool garden.

It was an unbearable restriction, she thought, getting annoyed. She wouldn't put up with it. She would go out anyway. She would slip downstairs while Mr. Wilkins—who was proving to be a real lifesaver—kept Mr. Briggs busy with the oleander story. She would slip out the front door and hide in the shadows of the winding path. No one could see her there, and no one would think to look for her.

She grabbed a shawl, planning to stay out for a long time—maybe even skipping dinner, and it would be Mr. Briggs's fault if she went hungry. Checking out the window to make sure the coast was clear, she slipped outside to the winding path and sat on one of the benches placed at the turns for tired walkers.

Ah, this was wonderful, Scrap thought with a sigh of relief. It was so cool and smelled so fresh. She could see the calm water of the little harbor through the pine trees, and the lights turning on in the houses across the bay. The green dusk was dotted with pink gladiolas in the grass and white daisies.

It was so peaceful. Nothing moved—not a single leaf. The only sound was a dog barking far off in the hills, or the occasional burst of voices from the restaurant below when its door opened and shut quickly.

She took a deep breath of pleasure. This was—

She stopped mid-breath. What was that?

She leaned forward, listening intently.

Footsteps on the path. Was it Briggs finding her?

Should she run?

No, the footsteps were coming up, not down. It must be someone from the village, maybe Angelo bringing groceries.

She relaxed. But the footsteps didn't sound like Angelo's quick pace. They were slow, heavy, and paused frequently.

"Someone who isn't used to walking uphill," Scrap thought.

She didn't think of running back to the house. She wasn't afraid of anything except unwanted affection. Criminals held no terror for her; she would only fear them if they stopped trying to rob her and tried to flirt instead.

A moment later, the person turned the corner of the path and stopped.

"Catching his breath," Scrap thought, not looking back.

When the man didn't move, she turned around and was astonished to see Ferdinand Arundel, a well-known memoir writer she had seen frequently in London.

She stared. Being followed didn't surprise her anymore, but she was shocked he had found her. Her mother had promised not to tell anyone where she was.

"You?" she said, feeling betrayed. "Here?"

He walked up to her and took off his hat. His forehead was sweaty from the unaccustomed climb. He looked embarrassed and pleading, like a guilty but loyal dog.

"You must forgive me," he said. "Lady Droitwich told me where you were. Since I was passing through on my way to Rome, I decided to stop at Mezzago and drop by to see how you were."

"But didn't my mother tell you I was on a rest cure?"

"Yes, she did. That's why I didn't disturb you earlier. I thought you would probably sleep all day and wake up around now to eat."

"But—"

"I know. I have no excuse. I couldn't help myself."

"This is what happens when mother insists on inviting authors to lunch, and when I look much friendlier than I actually am," Scrap thought.

She had been nice to Ferdinand Arundel; she liked him, or at least didn't dislike him. He seemed like a jolly, simple man with the eyes of a friendly dog. In London, he had admired her but hadn't tried to force himself on her. There, he had just been a harmless, pleasant conversationalist who made lunch parties fun. Now it turned out he was a clinger too. How dare he follow her all the way here! No one else had. Maybe her mother gave him the address because she thought he was completely harmless and could escort her home.

Still, whatever he was, he wouldn't cause as much trouble as Mr. Briggs. Briggs was young, reckless, and could easily make a scene. She could picture Briggs using rope ladders or singing under her window, which would be very awkward. Mr. Arundel was not built for that kind of adventure. He had lived too long and eaten too many good dinners. He was at least forty. If a man spent forty years writing books instead of exercising, he would naturally have Mr. Arundel's stout build—suited for conversation rather than action.

Scrap, who had felt gloomy when Briggs arrived, became practical upon seeing Arundel. Since he was here, she couldn't send him away until he had eaten dinner.

So she might as well make the best of it. Besides, he could serve as a shield against Mr. Briggs. Since she already knew Ferdinand, they could talk about mutual friends and her mother, which would keep Briggs at bay during dinner. It was only for one evening, and he wasn't going to eat her.

So she decided to be friendly. "I'm having dinner at eight," she said, ignoring his last comment, "and you must come up and join us. Sit down, cool off, and tell me how everyone is doing."

"Can I really dine with you? In these travel clothes?" he asked, wiping his brow before sitting next to her.

She was too beautiful to be real, he thought. Just looking at her and hearing her voice made the whole trip worth it.

"Of course. I assume you left your carriage in the village and will take the night train from Mezzago."

"Or stay at a hotel in Mezzago and leave tomorrow. But tell me about yourself," he said, staring at her profile. "London has been so boring and empty. Lady Droitwich said you were staying with people she didn't know. I hope they've been nice to you? You look wonderful, like your rest cure worked perfectly."

"They've been very kind," Scrap said. "I found them through an advertisement."

"An advertisement?"

"I find it's a great way to make friends. I'm actually closer to one of them than I've been to anyone in years."

"Really? Who is it?"

"You can guess who it is when you meet them. Tell me about my mother. When did you see her last? We agreed not to write to each other unless it was important. I wanted a completely quiet month."

"And now I've come and interrupted you. I'm so ashamed of doing this, but I couldn't help myself."

"Oh, don't worry," Scrap said quickly. He couldn't have arrived on a better day, since the infatuated Briggs was waiting for her up at the house. "I'm actually very glad to see you. Tell me about mother."
