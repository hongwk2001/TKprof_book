Chapter 1

It all started at a women’s club in London on a gloomy February afternoon. The club was uncomfortable, and the weather was miserable. Mrs. Wilkins, who had traveled from her home in Hampstead to do some shopping, had just finished lunch at the club. She picked up The Times from the table in the smoking room. As she listlessly scanned the personal ads in the Agony Column, her eyes caught this:

To Those Who Appreciate Wistaria and Sunshine: A small medieval Italian castle on the Mediterranean coast is available to rent, fully furnished, for the month of April. Essential staff will stay on. Contact Z, Box 1000, The Times.

That was the seed of the idea. But like many life-changing moments, the person who conceived it had no idea what was happening at the time.

In fact, Mrs. Wilkins was so completely unaware that her April plans for the year had just been decided that she dropped the newspaper with a sigh of irritation and resignation. She walked over to the window and stared drearily out at the rain-soaked street.

A medieval castle was not for someone like her, even a small one. The Mediterranean coast in April, the wistaria, the sunshine—none of that was in her reach. Such luxuries were only for the wealthy. And yet, the advertisement was addressed to "those who appreciate" these things. That technically meant her, because she certainly appreciated them—far more than anyone knew, and more than she had ever admitted. 

But she was poor. In the entire world, her only personal possession was ninety pounds. She had saved it carefully, pound by pound, over several years out of her clothing allowance. She had scraped this nest egg together at her husband's suggestion, to serve as a shield against a "rainy day." Her clothing allowance, given to her by her father, was one hundred pounds a year. Because of this, Mrs. Wilkins's clothes were what her husband (who urged her to save) called "modest and sensible," while her acquaintances (on the rare occasions they spoke of her at all, since she was easily ignored) called her "a complete sight."

Mr. Wilkins, a lawyer, was a big supporter of saving money—except when it came to his food. He didn't call saving on food "thrift"; he called it bad housekeeping. But he had nothing but praise for the kind of thrift that crept into Mrs. Wilkins’s wardrobe and ruined her style. "You never know," he often said, "when a rainy day will come. You might be very glad to find you have a nest egg. Indeed, we both might."

Staring out the club window onto Shaftesbury Avenue—her club was cheap but convenient for traveling back to Hampstead where she lived, and close to Shoolbred’s department store where she shopped—Mrs. Wilkins stood there for a long time. In her mind's eye, she saw the Mediterranean in April, the wistaria, and the wonderful opportunities enjoyed by the rich. Meanwhile, her physical eyes watched the depressing, soot-colored rain falling steadily on the crowd of umbrellas and splashing buses. 

Suddenly, she wondered if this cold, wet afternoon was exactly the "rainy day" Mellersh—Mellersh was Mr. Wilkins—had warned her to prepare for. And wasn't escaping this terrible weather to go to a small medieval castle exactly what Providence had intended for her savings? Only a portion of her savings, of course—perhaps a very small part. Since the castle was medieval, it might be run-down, and surely run-down places were cheap to rent. She wouldn't mind a bit of wear and tear, since you didn't have to pay to repair damage that was already there. In fact, by lowering the rent, the dilapidations practically paid you back! But what nonsense to even think about it...

She turned away from the window with the same mix of irritation and resignation with which she had laid down the paper. She walked across the room toward the door, intending to get her raincoat and umbrella, fight her way onto a crowded bus, and head to Shoolbred’s to buy some fish for Mellersh’s dinner. Mellersh was very picky about fish and would eat only sole, or occasionally salmon. 

Just then, she noticed Mrs. Arbuthnot sitting at the table in the middle of the room. Mrs. Arbuthnot was a woman she knew by sight; she also lived in Hampstead and belonged to the club. She was currently sitting at the magazine table, completely absorbed in the front page of The Times.

Mrs. Wilkins had never spoken to Mrs. Arbuthnot before. Mrs. Arbuthnot belonged to one of the church groups that analyzed, classified, and helped the local poor. In contrast, when Mrs. Wilkins and Mellersh did go out, they attended parties hosted by impressionist painters, who were common in Hampstead. Mellersh had a sister who was married to one of these artists living near the Heath. Because of this connection, Mrs. Wilkins was pulled into a social circle that felt completely unnatural to her, and she had grown to dread discussing art. She was expected to comment on paintings but never knew what to say. She usually just muttered, "Marvelous," and felt like a fraud. 

But nobody minded. Nobody listened, and nobody paid attention to Mrs. Wilkins anyway. She was the kind of person who went unnoticed at parties. Her thrifty, outdated clothes made her practically invisible. Her face was plain, her conversation was quiet, and she was terribly shy. If your clothes, face, and conversation are all forgettable, thought Mrs. Wilkins, who knew her own limitations, what is actually left of you at a party?

She was also always overshadowed by Wilkins. He was a clean-shaven, handsome man who brought an air of respectability to any gathering just by showing up. Wilkins was highly respected, well-liked by his senior partners, and admired by his sister's artistic circle. He made sensible judgments on art, spoke prudently, and never said too much or too little. He gave off the impression of keeping a carbon copy of everything he said. He was so visibly reliable that people meeting him at parties often became unhappy with their own lawyers and eventually switched to Wilkins.

Naturally, Mrs. Wilkins was completely ignored. "She," his sister had once declared in a highly authoritative tone, "should stay at home." But Wilkins couldn't leave his wife at home. As a family lawyer, it was important for him to have a wife and show her off. During the week he took her to parties, and on Sundays he took her to church. Being relatively young—he was thirty-nine—and wanting to attract wealthy elderly clients, he couldn't afford to skip church. It was there that Mrs. Wilkins had become familiar with Mrs. Arbuthnot, though they had never exchanged a word.

Mrs. Wilkins had watched her lead the Sunday school children into the pews. Mrs. Arbuthnot would arrive at the head of the procession exactly five minutes before the choir, organizing the boys and girls neatly into their seats. She would have them kneel for a quick prayer and stand them back up just as the organ swelled, the vestry door opened, and the choir and clergy marched out. 

Mrs. Arbuthnot had a sad face, yet she was highly efficient. This combination puzzled Mrs. Wilkins. Mellersh had always told her—especially on days when she could find only cheap fish for dinner—that efficient people were never depressed, and that doing one's job well automatically made a person bright and cheerful.

There was nothing bright or cheerful about Mrs. Arbuthnot, though she handled the Sunday school children with routine efficiency. But when Mrs. Wilkins turned from the window and saw her at the club, Mrs. Arbuthnot was not behaving routinely at all. She was staring fixedly at one section of the front page of The Times, holding the paper completely still. Her eyes didn't move. She was just staring, her face looking like a patient, disappointed Madonna.

Acting on an impulse that surprised even herself, the shy and quiet Mrs. Wilkins changed her plans. Instead of heading to the cloakroom and then to Shoolbred's for Mellersh's dinner, she stopped at the table and sat down directly across from Mrs. Arbuthnot.

It was a long, narrow wooden table, so they were sitting very close to each other.

Mrs. Arbuthnot didn't look up. She kept gazing with dreamy eyes at that one spot on the newspaper.

Mrs. Wilkins watched her for a moment, trying to find the courage to speak. She wanted to ask if she had seen the advertisement. She didn't know why it was so important, but she felt a strong urge to ask. How silly to be too afraid to speak, she thought. Mrs. Arbuthnot looked so kind and yet so unhappy. Why couldn't two unhappy people comfort each other with a little real, natural conversation? Why couldn't they talk about how they truly felt, what they wanted, and what they still hoped for? 

Mrs. Wilkins was certain that Mrs. Arbuthnot was reading the exact same ad. Her eyes were focused on the very same column. Was she also picturing what it would be like—the color, the fragrance, the bright light, and the soft sound of the sea lapping against warm rocks? Color, fragrance, light, and the sea—instead of Shaftesbury Avenue, wet buses, the fish counter at Shoolbred's, the subway to Hampstead, dinner, and then the exact same routine tomorrow, the next day, and forever...

Suddenly, Mrs. Wilkins found herself leaning across the table. "Are you reading about the medieval castle and the wistaria?" she heard herself ask.

Naturally, Mrs. Arbuthnot was surprised. But she was not half as surprised as Mrs. Wilkins was by her own boldness.

Mrs. Arbuthnot had never really looked at the messy, tall, awkward woman sitting across from her. She saw a small freckled face and large gray eyes almost hidden under a wet, floppy hat. She stared at Mrs. Wilkins for a moment before answering. She was reading about the medieval castle and the wistaria—or rather, she had read it ten minutes ago and had been lost in a dream of light, color, fragrance, and the soft lapping of the sea against warm rocks ever since.

"Why do you ask me that?" she asked in her low, serious voice, which she had developed from years of working patiently with the poor.

Mrs. Wilkins flushed, suddenly looking very shy and embarrassed. "Oh, only because I saw it too, and I thought perhaps... I thought somehow..." she stammered.

Out of habit, Mrs. Arbuthnot—whose mind was trained to categorize and organize people—began to wonder where this woman fit. If she had to classify her, under what heading would she go?

"And I know you by sight," Mrs. Wilkins rushed on. Like many shy people, once she started talking, she couldn't stop, terrified into speaking more just by the sound of her own voice. "Every Sunday... I see you every Sunday in church."

"In church?" Mrs. Arbuthnot repeated.

"And this seems like such a wonderful thing—this advertisement about the wistaria—and..."

Mrs. Wilkins, who must have been at least thirty, stopped speaking and shifted awkwardly in her chair like an embarrassed schoolgirl.

"It just seems so wonderful," she burst out, "and it's such a miserable day..."

She sat looking at Mrs. Arbuthnot with the pleading eyes of a locked-up dog.

This poor thing, thought Mrs. Arbuthnot, whose life was dedicated to helping others, needs advice.

She patiently prepared to offer it.

"If you see me in church," she said kindly, "I assume you live in Hampstead too?"

"Oh yes," said Mrs. Wilkins. Her head drooped slightly on her long, thin neck, as if the very memory of Hampstead weighed her down. "Oh yes."

"Where?" asked Mrs. Arbuthnot, beginning to gather the facts.

But Mrs. Wilkins only laid her hand gently and affectionately over the advertisement, as if the printed words themselves were precious. "Perhaps that's why this seems so wonderful," she said.

"No, I think it's wonderful regardless," Mrs. Arbuthnot said, forgetting the facts for a moment and sighing softly.

"Then you were reading it?"

"Yes," said Mrs. Arbuthnot, her eyes turning dreamy again.

"Wouldn't it be wonderful?" Mrs. Wilkins whispered.

"Wonderful," Mrs. Arbuthnot agreed. Her face lit up for a second, then faded back into its usual patient expression. "Very wonderful. But it's no use wasting time thinking about such things."

"Oh, but it is!" Mrs. Wilkins replied quickly, surprising them both. It was a strange response coming from someone in such a plain coat and skirt, a crumpled hat, and messy hair. "Just thinking about it is worth it. It's such a change from Hampstead. And sometimes I believe... I really believe that if you want something hard enough, you get it."

Mrs. Arbuthnot watched her patiently. In what category would she place her?

"Perhaps," she said, leaning forward slightly, "you could tell me your name. If we are to be friends"—she smiled her gentle smile—"as I hope we will, we should start at the beginning."

"Oh yes, of course. I'm Mrs. Wilkins," she said. She blushed when Mrs. Arbuthnot didn't immediately respond. "I don't expect my name means anything to you. Sometimes it doesn't mean much to me either. But," she looked around as if seeking help, "I am Mrs. Wilkins."

She didn't like her name. She thought it sounded small and cheap, with a silly twist at the end like the curled-up tail of a pug. But there was nothing she could do about it. She was a Wilkins, and a Wilkins she would remain. Her husband always urged her to introduce herself as "Mrs. Mellersh-Wilkins," but she only did that when he was close enough to hear. She felt adding Mellersh made the name Wilkins sound even worse, like putting an overly grand name on the gates of a small suburban villa.

When he first suggested adding Mellersh, she had objected. After a long pause—Mellersh was far too cautious to speak without a pause, during which he presumably made a mental record of his comment—he had said, highly offended, "But I am not a villa." He had looked at her with the expression of a man hoping, for the hundredth time, that he hadn't married a fool.

Of course he wasn't a villa, Mrs. Wilkins had assured him. She had never suggested he was, and she hadn't meant it that way at all... she was just thinking...

The more she tried to explain, the more Mellersh worried that he had indeed married a fool. They had a long, silent argument—conducted with dignified silence on his part and earnest apologies on hers—about whether she had meant to call him a villa.

I believe, she had thought when it was finally over, that people will argue about anything when they haven't spent a single day apart for two whole years. What we both need is a holiday.

"My husband," Mrs. Wilkins continued, trying to explain herself to Mrs. Arbuthnot, "is a lawyer. He..." She searched for something positive to say about Mellersh, and settled on: "He is very handsome."

"Well," said Mrs. Arbuthnot kindly, "that must be very nice for you."

"Why?" asked Mrs. Wilkins.

"Because," said Mrs. Arbuthnot, slightly surprised—she was used to having her charity-circle pronouncements accepted without question—"because beauty and good looks are a gift, and if they are used properly..."

She trailed off. Mrs. Wilkins's large gray eyes were staring at her, and Mrs. Arbuthnot suddenly realized she was speaking like a schoolteacher lecturing a child. It was a habit she had developed from speaking to audiences who couldn't disagree or interrupt.

But Mrs. Wilkins wasn't listening. A vivid picture had suddenly flashed across her mind. She saw the two of them sitting together under a massive trailing wistaria that climbed over the branches of an unfamiliar tree. She saw herself and Mrs. Arbuthnot—clearly, as if they were already there. And behind them, bright in the warm sunshine, were the old gray walls of the medieval castle. 

She stared at Mrs. Arbuthnot, completely tuned out. Mrs. Arbuthnot stared back, fascinated by the expression on Mrs. Wilkins’s face. It was filled with excitement, glowing and shifting like water under sunlight rippled by a breeze. In this moment, if she had been at a party, people would have looked at Mrs. Wilkins with real interest.

They stared at each other: Mrs. Arbuthnot with curious surprise, and Mrs. Wilkins like someone who had just seen a vision. Of course! That was how they could do it. She couldn't afford it on her own, and even if she could, she wouldn't want to go all alone. But she and Mrs. Arbuthnot could go together...

She leaned across the table. "Why don't we try to rent it?" she whispered.

Mrs. Arbuthnot's eyes widened. "Rent it?"

"Yes," Mrs. Wilkins said, still whispering as if they were sharing a secret. "Instead of just sitting here saying 'how wonderful' and going back to Hampstead without doing anything. Going back to the same dinners and the same fish we've dealt with for years, and will keep dealing with for years to come. In fact," Mrs. Wilkins blushed deeply, frightened by her own boldness but unable to stop, "I see no end to it. There is no end. So there ought to be a break, a little pause—for everyone's sake. It would actually be unselfish of us to go away and be happy for a while, because we would come back much nicer people. Everyone needs a holiday after a while."

"But how do you mean, get it?" asked Mrs. Arbuthnot.

"Take it," Mrs. Wilkins said.

"Take it?"

"Rent it. Hire it. Go there."

"But... do you mean you and me?"

"Yes! Between the two of us. We can share the cost, so it only costs half. And you look... you look exactly like you want it as much as I do. You look like you need a rest, like you need something happy to happen to you."

"But we don't even know each other."

"Just think how well we would know each other if we went away together for a month! And I've saved money for a rainy day, and I'm sure you have too. And this is the rainy day—just look at it outside!"

She is unbalanced, thought Mrs. Arbuthnot. Yet, she felt a strange thrill.

"Think of getting away for a whole month—from everything—to heaven..."

She shouldn't say things like that, Mrs. Arbuthnot thought, thinking of the vicar. Yet, the idea of a rest, a complete escape, felt incredibly tempting.

Habit, however, steadied her. Using her familiar, patient tone, she said, "But heaven isn't somewhere else. It is here and now. We are told so." She became very serious, speaking as she did to the poor. "Heaven is within us. We are told that on the highest authority. You know the poem about the kindred points, don't you..."

"Oh yes, I know them," Mrs. Wilkins interrupted impatiently.

"The kindred points of heaven and home," Mrs. Arbuthnot continued, determined to finish her sentence. "Heaven is in our home."

"It isn't," Mrs. Wilkins said flatly.

Mrs. Arbuthnot was shocked. "Oh, but it is. It is there if we choose to make it so."

"I do choose, and I do try to make it, and it isn't," Mrs. Wilkins replied.

Mrs. Arbuthnot fell silent. She, too, had her doubts about homes sometimes. She looked at Mrs. Wilkins uneasily, feeling a desperate need to classify her. If she could just put Mrs. Wilkins under a proper heading, she felt she would regain her own balance, which felt dangerously shaky. She hadn't had a holiday in years either. The advertisement had set her dreaming, and Mrs. Wilkins's excitement was contagious. Watching her glowing face, Mrs. Arbuthnot felt as if she were being woken up from a long sleep.

Clearly, Mrs. Wilkins was unbalanced. Mrs. Arbuthnot had met unbalanced people before, and they had never affected her own stability. But this woman was making her feel completely wobbly. She felt a sudden, deep urge to escape—away from her strict guideposts of God, Husband, Home, and Duty—and just be happy for once. Which was wrong, of course. It was absolutely wrong. She had her own savings in the Post Office Savings Bank, but the idea of drawing them out to spend on herself was absurd. Surely she couldn't do such a thing? Surely she wouldn't abandon her duty, the poor, and the sick just to go to Italy? A trip to Italy would be wonderful, but life was about having the strength to resist doing the things you wanted to do.

To Mrs. Arbuthnot, the four pillars of life were God, Husband, Home, and Duty. She had rested her head on these facts years ago, after a period of great misery, using them like a pillow. She was terrified of being shaken awake. So she tried hard to find a heading for Mrs. Wilkins to steady her own mind. Looking at her uneasily, she decided to temporarily classify her under: Nerves. It was possible she belonged under Hysteria, which often led to madness, but Mrs. Arbuthnot had learned not to rush people into final categories. She had made mistakes in the past and felt terrible remorse.

Yes, Nerves. The woman probably didn't have enough work to keep her busy, Mrs. Arbuthnot thought. She was rudderless, blown about by sudden impulses. Nerves was definitely her category. Poor little thing, she thought, her compassion returning. All she could see across the table was Mrs. Wilkins’s eager, shy face and thin shoulders, and a childish longing in her eyes for happiness. 

No, fleeting things like vacations didn't bring true happiness. Mrs. Arbuthnot had learned during her long marriage to Frederick—she had married him at twenty and was now thirty-three—where true joy was found. It was found only in living for others and at the feet of God.

Frederick was the kind of husband who drove a wife to the feet of God early in marriage. It had been a short but painful journey. It had taken the entire first year of their marriage, and every step had been a struggle that felt stained with her own heart's blood. But that was over now. She had found peace. Frederick, once her passionately loved groom, had become second only to God on her list of duties and patience. He was a duty she performed dutifully, made quiet by her prayers. For years, she had been happy only by forgetting about personal happiness. She wanted to keep it that way. She wanted to shut out everything that made her long for beautiful things again.

"I would like to be friends," she said earnestly. "Won't you come visit me, or let me visit you? Whenever you feel like talking. I'll give you my address," she said, searching her purse for a card, "so you won't forget."

Mrs. Wilkins ignored the card.

"It's so funny," Mrs. Wilkins said, as if she hadn't heard. "But I see us both—you and me—in that medieval castle this April."

Mrs. Arbuthnot felt uneasy again. "Do you?" she asked, trying to remain stable under the woman's visionary gray eyes. "Do you?"

"Don't you ever see things in a flash before they happen?" Mrs. Wilkins asked.

"Never," said Mrs. Arbuthnot.

She tried to give her usual wise, patient smile that she used with the poor, but she couldn't manage it. The smile faded.

"Of course," she said in a quiet voice, almost as if she were worried the vicar or the Savings Bank would hear, "it would be incredibly beautiful... incredibly beautiful..."

"Even if it were wrong," Mrs. Wilkins said, "it's only for a month."

"That..." Mrs. Arbuthnot began, ready to explain why that was the wrong way to think. But Mrs. Wilkins cut her off.

"Anyway," Mrs. Wilkins said, "I'm sure it's wrong to go on being good for so long that you become miserable. And I can tell you've been good for years, because you look so unhappy." Mrs. Arbuthnot opened her mouth to protest, but Mrs. Wilkins went on. "And I... I've done nothing but duties and things for others since I was a girl, and I don't think anyone loves me any better for it. And I long... oh, I long for something else... something else..."

Was she going to cry? Mrs. Arbuthnot felt extremely uncomfortable and sympathetic. She hoped she wouldn't cry here in this public room with people walking by.

But Mrs. Wilkins, after searching her pockets, pulled out a handkerchief, blew her nose, blinked quickly, and looked at Mrs. Arbuthnot with a shy, quivering smile.

"Would you believe," she whispered, looking ashamed, "that I've never spoken to anyone like this in my life? I don't know what has come over me."

"It's the advertisement," Mrs. Arbuthnot said, nodding seriously.

"Yes," Mrs. Wilkins said, dabbing at her eyes. "And the fact that we're both so... miserable."
