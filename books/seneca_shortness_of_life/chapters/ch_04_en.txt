Chapter IV: Augustus and the Longing for Leisure

You will notice that the most powerful and highly positioned men often let slip remarks where they long for leisure, praise it, and prefer it above all their wealth and status. They wish they could safely step down from their high pedestals; for, "even if nothing external attacks or shatters it, greatness often collapses under its own weight."

The deified Augustus, whom the gods blessed more than any other man, never stopped praying for rest and seeking an escape from public duties. His conversations always returned to one core subject: his hope for leisure. This was his sweet, albeit empty, consolation to make his labors bearable—the thought that one day he would finally live for himself. In a letter to the Senate, promising that his retirement would not lack dignity or diminish his past glory, he wrote: "These things are better proven by actions than promises. However, since that joyful reality is still so far away, my deep desire for that time has led me to taste some of its delight just by talking about it." Leisure seemed so incredibly desirable to him that, unable to achieve it in reality, he anticipated it in his thoughts. The man who saw the entire world depending on him alone, who decided the fate of individuals and nations, found his greatest happiness in imagining the day he would lay aside his power. He had discovered how much sweat was required to maintain the blessings that dazzled the world, and how many hidden anxieties they concealed. Forced into armed conflict first with his countrymen, then with his colleagues, and finally with his own relatives, he spilled blood across land and sea.

Through Macedonia, Sicily, Egypt, Syria, Asia, and nearly every corner of the known world, he followed the path of war. When his troops grew weary of shedding Roman blood, he directed them toward foreign enemies. While he was pacifying the Alps and subduing enemies within a supposedly peaceful empire, and while he was expanding Rome's borders beyond the Rhine, the Euphrates, and the Danube, the swords of Murena, Caepio, Lepidus, Egnatius, and others were being sharpened in Rome itself to assassinate him. He had barely escaped their plots when his own daughter, along with the noble youths bound to her by adultery as if by a sacred oath, began to terrify his declining years. Then came Paulus, and once again, the terrifying prospect of a woman allied with an Antony. No sooner had he cut away these ulcers, taking the limbs with them, than new ones grew in their place. "Just as in a body overloaded with blood, there is always a rupture somewhere." And so he endlessly longed for leisure, finding relief from his labors only in the hope and thought of it. "This was the prayer of the one man who had the power to answer the prayers of all mankind."
