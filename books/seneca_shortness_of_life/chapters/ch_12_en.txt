Chapter 12: The Trifles of Trivial Pursuits

12. You might ask who exactly I mean by "the engrossed"—those who are entirely consumed by busywork. Do not assume I am only talking about the people finally chased out of the law courts by the guard dogs, or those you see either gloriously crushed by their own entourage or scornfully squeezed in someone else's. I do not just mean those whose social obligations drag them from their own homes only to bump against someone else's doors, or those whose corrupt, festering pursuit of profit keeps them constantly on trial.

Even the leisure of some people is merely another form of being engrossed. In their villas or on their couches, in the midst of solitude, completely withdrawn from the world, they remain their own greatest source of anxiety. We should say that such people are living not in leisure, but in busy idleness.

Would you call a man "at leisure" if he painstakingly arranges his antique bronzes—driven by a mania that makes rusted copper artificially expensive—spending the better part of his day fussing over them? What about the man who sits in the public wrestling arena, watching boys brawl—for, to our shame, we now suffer from vices that are not even Roman? Or the man who organizes his pack mules into perfect pairs by age and color? Or the one who sponsors the newest athletes?

Tell me, would you say a man is "at leisure" if he spends hours at the barber shop, having every hair that grew overnight plucked out, engaging in solemn debates over every individual strand? Watch as disarranged locks are carefully restored, or thinning ones combed forward to hide a receding hairline! How furious they become if the barber is even slightly careless—as if he were shearing a real man! How they flare up if a single piece of their mane is snipped too short, if anything lies out of place, or if it fails to fall into perfect ringlets! Who among them wouldn't rather see the state fall into chaos than their own hair? Who is not more concerned with the neatness of his head than the safety of his country? Who wouldn't rather be perfectly groomed than morally upright? Would you call these people "at leisure" when they are entirely occupied by the comb and the mirror?

And what of those obsessed with composing, listening to, and learning songs? They twist the human voice—which Nature designed to be clear and straightforward—into the lazy meanderings of an indolent melody. They are always snapping their fingers to beat time to some song stuck in their heads, and you can catch them humming even when summoned to serious, often melancholic, matters. These people do not have leisure; they have idle occupations.

And their banquets? Heaven knows I cannot count those among their free hours. I see how anxiously they set out their silver plates, how meticulously they arrange the tunics of their pretty slave boys, and how breathlessly they watch the cook carve the wild boar. I see with what speed smooth-faced boys rush to perform their duties at a given signal, how skillfully the birds are carved into exact portions according to rule, and how carefully miserable little boys wipe up the spit of drunkards. Through all this, they seek a reputation for elegance and refined taste. Their evils follow them so deeply into the privacies of life that they cannot even eat or drink without ostentation.

I also wouldn't count among the leisured class those who are carried here and there in sedan chairs and litters, keeping strict schedules for their rides as if it were a crime to miss them. These are people who must be reminded by someone else when to bathe, when to swim, and when to dine. They are so weakened by the excessive laziness of a pampered mind that they cannot even tell if they are hungry on their own!

I hear that one of these pampered people—if you can call it "pampering" to forget how to be human—was lifted out of the bath by hands and placed in his sedan chair, and then asked genuinely, "Am I seated now?" Do you think a man who does not even know if he is sitting knows whether he is alive, whether he can see, or whether he is truly at leisure? It is hard to say whether I pity him more if he actually didn't know, or if he was only pretending. They really do forget many things, but they pretend to forget even more. Some vices delight them simply because they serve as proof of their immense prosperity; to them, it seems lowly and despicable to actually know what you are doing.

And yet people think comedians invent these things to mock luxury! In truth, they leave out far more than they invent. Such a multitude of unbelievable vices has emerged in our age—an age clever only in its decadence—that we can actually accuse the comedians of holding back. To think anyone could be so lost in luxury that he needs another person's word to know if he is sitting down!

This man is not at leisure. You must call him something else—he is sick, or rather, he is dead. "That man is at leisure who also has a perception of his leisure." But this other man, who is only half alive, who needs someone to tell him the posture of his own body—how can he possibly be the master of his own time?
