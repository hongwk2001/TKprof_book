Chapter XX: The Tranquil Conclusion

When you see someone constantly wearing the robes of high office, or whose name is famous in the public square, do not envy them; "these things are bought at the price of life." They will sacrifice all their years just to have a single year named after them. 

Life abandons some in the middle of their early struggles, long before they can reach the peak of their ambition. Others, having crawled through a thousand indignities to achieve ultimate power, are struck by the miserable realization that they have merely toiled for an inscription on a tomb. Still others, reaching extreme old age and clinging to new ambitions as if they were young, collapse from sheer exhaustion in the midst of their vast and shameless efforts. 

Shameful is the man whose final breath leaves him in the middle of a trial—advanced in years, still desperate for the applause of an ignorant crowd, pleading a case for a complete stranger. Disgraceful is the man who collapses in the middle of his duties, exhausted more by his lifestyle than by his actual labor. Disgraceful, too, is the one who dies while collecting payments on an account, drawing a smirk from a long-delayed heir.

I cannot ignore an example that comes to mind: Sextus Turannius, an old man of proven diligence. After turning ninety, when the emperor Gaius Caesar finally relieved him of his duties, he ordered his household to lay him out on his bed and mourn him as if he were dead. The entire house wept over their old master's retirement, and their sorrow did not end until his old job was restored to him. 

"Is it really such a pleasure to die in harness?" 

Yet countless people share this mindset. Their desire for labor outlasts their physical ability. They fight against the frailty of their bodies, viewing old age as a hardship solely because it forces them to step aside. The law does not draft a soldier after fifty, nor summon a senator after sixty; "it is harder for men to grant themselves leisure than to get it from the law."

Meanwhile, as they rob and are robbed, as they shatter each other's peace and make one another miserable, their lives pass without profit, without pleasure, and without any elevation of the mind. No one keeps death in view; no one refrains from long-range hopes. Indeed, some men even plan for things that lie entirely beyond life—massive tombs, the dedication of public works, gifts for their own funeral pyres, and ostentatious ceremonies. 

But in truth, "the funerals of such men should be conducted by the light of torches and wax tapers, as though they had lived but the tiniest span."
