Chapter 16: The Anxiety of the Busy

"Those who forget the past, neglect the present, and fear the future have a life that is both brief and troubled." When they finally reach the end, these poor souls realize too late that they have been endlessly busy doing absolutely nothing. 

Do not mistake their occasional wishes for death as proof that they find life too long. Their foolishness torments them with shifting emotions, rushing them headlong into the very things they dread—often, "they pray for death precisely because they fear it." 

Furthermore, you shouldn't assume they live long simply because their days feel long to them, or because they complain that the hours drag until dinnertime. The truth is, whenever their distractions fail them, they are left restless and empty. They have no idea how to spend their leisure or endure unstructured time. Desperate, they scramble for the next distraction, finding all the time in between utterly unbearable. It is exactly like waiting for an upcoming gladiatorial match or an anticipated show: they want to skip over the intervening days entirely. "All postponement of something hoped for feels agonizingly long."

Yet the time they actually enjoy is fleeting, made even shorter by their own restlessness. They bounce from one pleasure to another, completely unable to settle their desires. To them, the days are not long, but hateful. Conversely, how absurdly short the nights seem when spent drinking or in the arms of a lover! This is why poets, fueling our human frailties, created myths claiming that Jupiter doubled the length of a night to indulge his lust. What does this achieve but to inflame our vices, giving us a divine excuse for our own moral weakness? 

For these men, can the nights they pay for so dearly ever feel like anything but too short? Ultimately, "they lose the day in expectation of the night, and the night in fear of the dawn."
