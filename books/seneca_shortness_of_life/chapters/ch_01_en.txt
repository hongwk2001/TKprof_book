Chapter 1: The Complaint of a Short Life

Most people, Paulinus, bitterly complain about the cruelty of Nature. They lament that we are born for such a brief span, and that our allotted time rushes by so swiftly that, for almost everyone, life ends just as they are finally getting ready to live. 

This isn't merely the grievance of the unthinking masses; even famous men have echoed this supposed universal curse. It was this feeling that made the greatest of physicians declare, "Life is short, art is long." It was this that led Aristotle to unfairly indict Nature, complaining that she granted animals five or ten lifetimes, yet fixed a much shorter limit for humans—despite our capacity for so many great achievements.

But here is the truth: it is not that we have a short time to live, but that we waste much of it. 

"Life is long enough, and it has been given to us in generous measure to accomplish the greatest things, if we invest it all wisely." 

When time is squandered on luxury and carelessness, when it is devoted to no worthy purpose, we are finally forced by the ultimate necessity of death to realize that life has passed us by before we even noticed it passing. 

So it is: "We are not given a short life, but we make it short; and we are not starved of time, but wasteful of it." 

Just as vast wealth is scattered in an instant when it falls to a bad owner, while limited wealth grows when entrusted to a good guardian, so too our life is abundantly long for whoever manages it properly.
