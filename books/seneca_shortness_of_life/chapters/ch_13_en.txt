Chapter XIII: Pedantry vs. Wisdom

It would be exhausting to list everyone who wastes their entire life on trivial games or just baking their bodies in the sun. They are not truly at leisure; rather, "their pleasures are made a busy occupation." No one can deny that those who obsess over useless literary trivia are simply laboring over nonsense—a disease that has now infected a vast number of Romans.

Once, it was a uniquely Greek obsession to argue over how many rowers Ulysses had, whether the *Iliad* or the *Odyssey* was written first, or if the same author wrote both. These are the kinds of trivial questions that, "if you keep them to yourself, in no way pleasure your secret soul, and, if you publish them, make you seem more of a bore than a scholar." But now, this "vain passion for learning useless things" has seized Rome as well.

Just days ago, I heard someone eagerly recounting which Roman general was the first to do this or that: Duilius was the first to win a naval battle; Curius Dentatus the first to parade elephants in a triumph. Even if these facts add nothing to true glory, they are at least connected to real service to the state. There is no real profit in such knowledge, but the illusion of history grabs our attention. We might even forgive those who investigate who first persuaded the Romans to board a ship (it was Claudius, nicknamed *Caudex* because the ancients called a raft of joined boards a *caudex*, giving us the word *codex*).

Perhaps there is some minor point in knowing that Valerius Corvinus was the first to conquer Messana, taking the city's name for himself. Or maybe you will let someone obsess over the fact that Lucius Sulla was the first to showcase unleashed lions in the Circus.

But does it serve any real purpose to know that Pompey was the first to slaughter eighteen elephants in the arena, pitting criminals against them in a mock battle? Here was a celebrated leader of the state, renowned for his kindness, who thought it a magnificent spectacle to destroy human beings in a novel way. Fighting to the death was not enough. Tearing them to pieces was not enough. They had to be crushed by beasts of monstrous bulk! It would be far better to let such atrocities fade into oblivion, lest some future tyrant learn of them and emulate an act so completely devoid of humanity. "O, what blindness does great prosperity cast upon our minds!" Pompey fed miserable human beings to foreign beasts, shedding rivers of blood before an audience that would soon be forced to shed its own. He believed himself above the laws of Nature. Yet later, betrayed by Egyptian treachery, he was murdered by the dagger of a lowly slave, discovering at last what an empty boast his surname of "The Great" truly was.

But to return to my point—to show how people waste their effort on useless trivia—this same pedant noted that Metellus was the only Roman to parade 120 captured elephants, and that Sulla was the last to extend Rome's sacred boundary.

Even if these historians speak the absolute truth, what does it matter? Whose mistakes will be corrected by such trivia? Whose passions will they restrain? "Whom will they make more brave, whom more just, whom more noble-minded?" My friend Fabianus used to say that sometimes he wondered if it was better to pursue no studies at all than to become entangled in these.
