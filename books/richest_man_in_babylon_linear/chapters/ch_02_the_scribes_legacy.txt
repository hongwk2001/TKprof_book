Chapter 2
The Scribe's Legacy

The shop of Mathon the Gold Lender was cool, quiet, and smelled of dried lavender and expensive oils. 

Heavy wool carpets from Persia covered the stone floor, muffling the sounds of the busy city outside. On a polished wooden table sat a set of delicate brass scales, used to weigh the gold and silver coins of Babylon. Behind the table sat Mathon, slowly sipping a cup of pomegranate juice.

Tarkad stepped inside, his heart pounding. The contrast between his dirty, sweat-stained tunic and the luxury of the shop made him feel small. 

Mathon looked up, his sharp eyes taking in Tarkad’s appearance in an instant. He did not look angry, but his face was unreadable. "Welcome, young man. How may the scales of Mathon serve you today?"

Tarkad swallowed hard and approached the table. "I come to ask for a loan, Mathon. Just three silver pieces. I am a willing worker, and I will repay you with interest as soon as I find a trade."

Mathon set his cup down. "Three silver pieces is a small sum to a rich man, but a large sum to a lender who must guard his treasures. Tell me, what security do you bring? Do you have land, or sheep, or a house?"

"I have nothing," Tarkad admitted, looking at the floor. "Only my strength and my word."

Mathon shook his head gently. "If a wild wind blows your word away, what will my scales weigh in its place? Lending is not charity, young man. I lend to help men multiply their gold, not to pay for their daily bread. To lend to a man with no plan and no security is like pouring water onto the dry desert sand. It disappears, and nothing grows."

Tarkad felt a wave of despair. "Then how is a man to start? If I have no gold, and no one will lend to me, must I remain a beggar forever?"

"It is a fair question," a voice spoke from the shadows near the back of the shop. 

Tarkad turned in surprise. Sitting on a low bench was a man in his late thirties, wearing a simple but finely woven blue tunic. His eyes were bright and full of intelligence, and he carried himself with the quiet confidence of someone who had achieved great things. 

It was Arkad, the man widely known as the richest citizen in all of Babylon. 

Mathon bowed his head respectfully. "Arkad. I did not know you were finished reviewing the contract for the brick kilns."

"I was," Arkad said, standing up and walking toward Tarkad. He looked at the young man, his gaze warm but evaluating. "I heard your plea, young friend. You ask Mathon for silver to buy a trade, but your empty pockets show that you do not yet know how to manage even the coppers you find. 

Let me tell you a story. When I was young, I looked around me. I saw all the good things that could bring happiness and contentment. I realized that wealth made all of these things much more powerful. Wealth is power. With wealth, many things become possible. You can decorate your home with the richest furnishings. You can sail across distant seas. You can feast on the delicacies of faraway lands. You can buy ornaments from the gold worker and the stone polisher. You can even build mighty temples for the Gods. You can do all these things, and many more. These things delight the senses and deeply satisfy the soul.

When I realized all of this, I made a decision. I decided that I would claim my share of the good things in life. I refused to be someone who just stands on the sidelines, enviously watching others enjoy themselves. I refused to be satisfied with wearing the cheapest clothes that looked barely respectable. I refused to settle for the life of a poor man. Instead, I decided to make myself a guest at this banquet of good things.

I was the son of a humble merchant. I came from a large family with no hope of an inheritance. I was not blessed with any superior intelligence or special talents. I decided that achieving my desires would require two things: time and study.

As for time, all people have plenty of it. Every single one of you has let enough time slip by to have made yourselves wealthy. Yet you have nothing to show for it except your good families, which you can be justly proud of.

As for study, do you remember what our wise teacher taught us? He said that learning comes in two forms: the first form is the knowledge we learn and memorize, and the second form is the training that teaches us how to discover the things we do not know.

Therefore, I decided to find out exactly how a person could accumulate wealth. Once I found the answer, I made it my personal mission to do it, and to do it well. 

I found a job working as a scribe in the hall of records. Every day, I worked long hours carving on clay tablets. I labored week after week, and month after month. But I had absolutely nothing to show for all my earnings. Food, clothing, offerings to the gods, and other things I can't even remember simply swallowed up everything I made. But my determination never faded.

Then, one day, Algamish the money lender came to the house of the city master. He ordered a copy of the Ninth Law. He told me, 'I need this in two days. If the job is finished by then, I will give you two copper coins.'

So I worked incredibly hard, but the law was very long. When Algamish returned, the job was still unfinished. He was furious. If I had been his slave, he would have beaten me. But I knew the city master would not let him hurt me, so I wasn't afraid. I looked at him and said, 'Algamish, you are a very wealthy man. Tell me how I can become rich too. In return, I will carve on this clay all night long. By the time the sun rises, it will be completely finished.'

He smiled at me and replied, 'You are a bold rascal, but we have a deal.'

I carved all through the night. My back ached, and the smell of the burning wick gave me a terrible headache. My eyes hurt so much I could barely see. But when he returned at sunrise, the clay tablets were completely finished.

'Now,' I said, 'tell me what you promised.'

'You have kept your end of the bargain, my son,' he said to me kindly. 'And I am ready to keep mine. I will tell you the things you want to know. I am growing old, and an old tongue loves to talk. When a young person comes to an older one for advice, he receives the wisdom of many years. Pay close attention to my words. If you don't, you will fail to grasp the truth I am about to share. 

I found the road to wealth when I decided that a part of all I earned was mine to keep. And so will you.'

Then he continued to look at me with a piercing glance, but he said no more.

'Is that all?' I asked.

'That was enough to change the heart of a sheep herder into the heart of a money lender,' he replied.

'But all I earn is mine to keep, isn't it?' I demanded.

'Far from it,' he replied. 'Do you not pay the garment-maker? Do you not pay the sandal-maker? Do you not pay for the things you eat? Can you live in Babylon without spending? What do you have to show for your earnings of the past month? What about the past year? Fool! You pay everyone but yourself. You might as well be a slave, working just for what your master gives you to eat and wear. If you kept one-tenth of all you earned for yourself, how much would you have in ten years?'

My knowledge of numbers did not fail me. I answered, 'As much as I earn in one year.'

'You speak only half the truth,' he retorted. 'Every gold piece you save becomes a slave to work for you. Every copper it earns is its child that can also earn for you. If you want to become wealthy, what you save must earn money. Its children must also earn money. Then they will all help to give you the abundance you crave.

A part of all you earn is yours to keep. It should be no less than a tenth, no matter how little you earn. It can be more if you can afford it. Pay yourself first. Do not buy more from the clothes-maker and the sandal-maker than you can afford out of the rest. 

Wealth, like a tree, grows from a tiny seed. The first copper you save is the seed from which your tree of wealth will grow. The sooner you plant that seed, the sooner the tree will grow. The more faithfully you nourish and water that tree with consistent savings, the sooner you can bask in contentment beneath its shade.'

Saying this, he took his clay tablets and went away.

I thought a lot about what he told me. It seemed reasonable, so I decided to try it. Each time I was paid, I took one out of every ten copper pieces and hid it away. Strange as it may seem, I was not any shorter on funds than before. I managed to get along without that money. But as my hoard began to grow, I was often tempted. I wanted to spend it on the good things the merchants displayed, but I wisely held back.

A full year after Algamish had gone, he returned and spoke to me. 'Son, have you paid yourself at least one-tenth of all you earned for the past year?'

I answered proudly, 'Yes, master, I have.' 

'That is good,' he answered, beaming upon me. 'And what have you done with it?'

'I gave it to Azmur, the brickmaker. He told me he was traveling over the far seas. He said he would buy the rare jewels of the Phoenicians for me in Tyre. When he returns, we will sell these at high prices and divide the earnings.'

'Every fool must learn,' he growled. 'But why trust a brickmaker's knowledge about jewels? Would you go to the breadmaker to ask about the stars? Your savings are gone, young man. You have yanked your wealth-tree up by the roots. But plant another one and try again. Next time, if you want advice about jewels, go to the jewel merchant. Anyone who takes advice about his savings from someone inexperienced will pay with those very savings.'

And it happened exactly as he said. The Phoenicians sold Azmur worthless bits of glass that looked like gems. But I started saving every tenth copper again.

Twelve months later, Algamish came back and spoke to me. 'What progress have you made since I last saw you?'

'I have paid myself faithfully,' I replied. 'I entrusted my savings to Agger the shieldmaker to buy bronze. Every fourth month, he pays me rental interest.'

'That is good. And what do you do with the rental interest?' 

'I throw a great feast with honey, fine wine, and spiced cake. I also bought myself a scarlet tunic. Someday, I will buy a young donkey to ride.' 

Algamish laughed at this. 'You are eating the children of your savings. How do you expect them to work for you then? And how can they have children that will also work for you? First, get yourself an army of golden slaves. After that, you can enjoy many rich banquets without any regret.'

I did not see him again for two years. When he finally returned, he was a very old man. He said to me, 'Arkad, have you achieved the wealth you dreamed of yet?'

And I answered, 'Not yet all that I desire. But I have some, and it earns more money, and its earnings earn even more.'

'And do you still take the advice of brickmakers?'

'About brickmaking, they give good advice,' I retorted.

Algamish smiled. 'Arkad, you have learned your lessons well. First, you learned to live on less than you earn. Next, you learned to seek advice only from those who are qualified. Finally, you have learned to make gold work for you. Because of this, I am making you my partner to look after my lands in Nippur.'

I went to Nippur and took charge of his properties. When Algamish passed away, I shared in his estate under the law. 

Opportunity is a proud goddess. She wastes no time with those who are unprepared."

Arkad looked at Tarkad, who was listening in silence.

"Some men speak of willpower," Arkad continued. "Willpower is simply an unflinching purpose. It is the determination to fulfill a task you have set for yourself. If I set a task for myself, no matter how small, I will see it through. Suppose I say to myself, 'For a hundred days, as I walk across the bridge into the city, I will pick a pebble from the road and throw it into the stream.' If I say it, I will do it. If I cross the bridge on the seventh day and forget, I will retrace my steps and cast that single pebble. I would never say, 'Tomorrow I will throw two pebbles, and that will be just as good.' On the twentieth day, I will not say, 'Arkad, this is useless. Just throw a handful right now.' No, when I set a task for myself, I finish it. Because of this, I am careful not to start difficult or impractical tasks.

So, Tarkad, do not ask Mathon for silver to spend. Ask yourself if you have the willpower to keep a part of all you earn. A part of all you earn is yours to keep."

Tarkad felt a new light in his mind. He looked at Arkad, then at Mathon, and bowed. "I have no security to borrow silver, Arkad. But I have a shovel. If you have labor, I will work, and I will keep one-tenth."

"Come to my office at sunrise," Arkad said with a warm smile. "We are digging a new canal, and a willing worker is always welcome."
