Chapter 2
The Scribe's Legacy

The shop of Mathon the Gold Lender was cool, quiet, and smelled of dried lavender and expensive oils. 

Heavy wool carpets from Persia covered the stone floor, muffling the sounds of the busy city outside. On a polished wooden table sat a set of delicate brass scales, used to weigh the gold and silver coins of Babylon. Behind the table sat Mathon, slowly sipping a cup of pomegranate juice.

Tarkad stepped inside, his heart pounding. The contrast between his dirty, sweat-stained tunic and the luxury of the shop made him feel small. 

Mathon looked up, his sharp eyes taking in Tarkad’s appearance in an instant. He did not look angry, but his face was unreadable. "Welcome, young man. How may the scales of Mathon serve you today?"

Tarkad swallowed hard and approached the table. "I come to ask for a loan, Mathon. Just three silver pieces. I am a willing worker, and I will repay you with interest as soon as I find a trade."

Mathon set his cup down. "Three silver pieces is a small sum to a rich man, but a large sum to a lender who must guard his treasures. Tell me, what security do you bring? Do you have land, or sheep, or a house?"

"I have nothing," Tarkad admitted, looking at the floor. "Only my strength and my word."

Mathon shook his head gently. "If a wild wind blows your word away, what will my scales weigh in its place? Lending is not charity, young man. I lend to help men multiply their gold, not to pay for their daily bread. To lend to a man with no plan and no security is like pouring water onto the dry desert sand. It disappears, and nothing grows."

Tarkad felt a wave of despair. "Then how is a man to start? If I have no gold, and no one will lend to me, must I remain a beggar forever?"

"It is a fair question," a voice spoke from the shadows near the back of the shop. 

Tarkad turned in surprise. Sitting on a low bench was a man in his late thirties, wearing a simple but finely woven blue tunic. His eyes were bright and full of intelligence, and he carried himself with the quiet confidence of someone who had achieved great things. 

It was Arkad. 

Mathon bowed his head respectfully. "Arkad. I did not know you were finished reviewing the contract for the brick kilns."

"I was," Arkad said, standing up and walking toward Tarkad. He looked at the young man, his gaze warm but evaluating. "I heard your plea, young friend. You ask Mathon for silver to buy a trade, but your empty pockets show that you do not yet know how to manage even the coppers you find."

"I have had bad luck," Tarkad muttered defensively.

"Luck is a lazy man's excuse," Arkad replied. "I look at you, and I see myself twenty years ago. I, too, was young, healthy, and desperate. I, too, wanted gold but had only empty hands. But I learned that gold does not go to those who merely wish for it. It goes to those who understand its laws."

Arkad stepped closer. "I will not give you three silver pieces. If I did, you would buy a meal, pay a creditor, and be back in this shop next week, just as poor as you are today. But I will offer you something better. I am building a new storehouse near the harbor. Come work for me tomorrow. If you work hard, I will pay you fair wages, and I will teach you the secrets of wealth."

Tarkad hesitated. "Work? I want to be a merchant, not a laborer."

Arkad smiled. "If you are too proud to sweat for a copper, you will never be wise enough to keep a gold piece. Let me tell you how my own journey began."

Arkad sat on the edge of the wooden table. 

"When I was a youth," Arkad began, "I had no inheritance and no special talents. I got a job as a scribe in the Hall of Records. For long, tedious hours, I carved laws and transactions onto wet clay tablets. The pay was small, and my purse was always empty. 

One day, Algamish, the wealthy money lender, came to the hall. He needed a copy of an important law carved before the next morning. It was a massive task. I looked at him and said, 'Algamish, you are a very rich man. If you promise to tell me the secret of wealth, I will work all night and have the tablet ready for you by sunrise.'

Algamish laughed, but he agreed. I carved all night, my hands cramping and my eyes burning by the light of the oil lamp. At sunrise, as the birds began to chirp, Algamish returned. The tablet was finished. 

He kept his word. He looked at me and said, 'Listen closely, young scribe. I found the road to wealth when I decided that a part of all I earned was mine to keep.'

I waited for him to say more, but he simply stood there. 'Is that all?' I asked. 

'It was enough to turn a poor scribe into a wealthy lender,' Algamish replied. 'Tell me, when you receive your wages, how much do you keep for yourself?'

'All of it,' I answered. 

'Fool!' Algamish said. 'You pay the garment-maker. You pay the sandal-maker. You pay for your bread and your beer. You pay everyone in Babylon but yourself. You work all month just to give your earnings to others. You are no different than a slave working for food and shelter. If you kept just one-tenth of all you earned, how much would you have in ten years?'

I did the math in my head. 'A full year's wages,' I admitted. 

'More than that,' Algamish corrected. 'Every gold piece you save is a slave that works for you. Every copper it earns is its child that can also earn for you. If you want to be rich, your savings must earn money, and those earnings must earn more. You must raise an army of golden slaves.'

Algamish left, and I resolved to try his advice. The next time I was paid, I took one copper out of every ten and hid it in a jar. I had to live more simply, but I found that I did not miss the tenth copper. 

But my first attempt ended in disaster. After a year, I had saved a modest sum. I gave it to a brickmaker who was traveling to Phoenicia, asking him to buy rare jewels for me to sell at a profit. 

When Algamish returned and heard what I had done, he shook his head in anger. 'Why would you trust a brickmaker to buy jewels? Does a builder ask a baker about the stars? No, you ask the astronomer. You have thrown your savings away!'

He was right. The Phoenicians sold the brickmaker worthless pieces of colored glass. My savings were gone. I had to start over. 

But I did not give up. I saved for another year, and this time, I lent my savings to Aggar, the shieldmaker, so he could buy bronze. He paid me regular interest every month. 

I took that interest and threw a great feast with honey, wine, and cake. 

When Algamish saw me again, he laughed. 'You are eating the children of your savings! How do you expect them to work for you if you consume them? Build your army first, Arkad, and then you can enjoy your banquets without regret.'

I followed his advice. I saved, I reinvested the interest, and my golden army grew. When Algamish saw that I had mastered the rules of money, he made me a partner in his estates. That is how I began to build my fortune."

Arkad stood up and looked at Tarkad. "The secret is simple, young friend. A part of all you earn is yours to keep. It must be no less than one-tenth, no matter how small your earnings are. Pay yourself first, before you pay your creditors. 

Now, the choice is yours. Will you go back to the alleys and hide from Dabasir, or will you come to the harbor tomorrow and learn how to make your money work for you?"

Tarkad looked at Arkad, then at Mathon, whose scales seemed to glisten in the quiet room. For the first time in months, Tarkad felt a spark of hope that did not rely on luck. 

"I will be at the harbor at sunrise, Arkad," Tarkad said, his voice firm. 

"Good," Arkad replied with a nod. "Bring your strength. We have a city to build."
