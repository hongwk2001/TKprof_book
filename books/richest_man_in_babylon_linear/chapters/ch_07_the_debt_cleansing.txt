Chapter 7
The Debt Cleansing

The caravan returned to Babylon with a triumphant clash of brass bells. 

Tarkad had traded wisely in Damascus, following the Third and Fourth Laws of Gold by seeking the advice of the caravan's veteran traders. He returned not only with his horse, but with two full bags of gold pieces—his earned share of the profits. 

Instead of hiding in the alleys, Tarkad rode straight to the central district. He did not go to a tavern, nor did he buy fine clothes. He walked directly into the shop of Mathon the Gold Lender. 

Mathon looked up from his ledger, his eyes widening in surprise. "Tarkad. You return with your head held high, and your horse looks well-fed. Did Damascus treat you kindly?"

Tarkad placed the two bags of gold on the table. "Damascus was profitable, Mathon. But I am not here to boast. I am here to face my debts. I want to write them down, and I want a plan to repay every copper."

Mathon’s face softened. He gestured to a set of wet clay tablets drying on a shelf. "A man who wishes to pay his debts is a man who has found his soul. Sit, Tarkad. Let us carve your path to freedom."

For the next hour, Tarkad listed every person he owed money to. He carved their names and the exact amounts into the soft clay. There was Dabasir, the camel trader; Kauskor, the food merchant; his old landlord; and several others. The total was daunting, but Tarkad did not flinch. 

When the list was finished, Mathon tapped the tablet. 

"To clear these debts, Tarkad, you must follow a strict rule. It is the rule of the three portions. It has saved many men, and it will save you. 

First: One-tenth of all you earn is yours to keep. This is your savings. It must never be touched, for it is the seed of your future wealth. 

Second: Seven-tenths of your earnings must be used to support your household. This pays for your food, your shelter, your clothing, and your family's needs. You must live strictly within this budget. 

Third: Two-tenths of your earnings must be divided honestly and proportionally among those to whom you owe money. Every time you are paid, you must take these two-tenths and distribute it to your creditors. Even if it is only a few coppers per person, you must pay it consistently."

Tarkad looked at the clay tablet. "But will my creditors accept this? Some of them are very angry. They want all their gold now."

Mathon smiled. "A creditor wants his gold, yes. But he would rather receive a small, guaranteed payment every month than nothing at all from a debtor who is hiding in the dark. Go to them. Face them like a free man. Show them this tablet and give them your promise."

Tarkad took the wet clay tablet and walked out into the streets. 

His first stop was the stall of Kauskor, the food merchant, who had refused to sell him bread on credit just weeks ago. When Kauskor saw Tarkad approaching, his face hardened. 

"I have no bread for you, Tarkad!" Kauskor shouted. "Repay what you owe first!"

Tarkad did not run. He stepped up to the counter and placed the clay tablet before the merchant. "Kauskor, I have returned from Damascus. I have work, and I have a plan. I owe you ten copper pieces. I cannot pay you all today, but every month, I will bring you two coppers until the debt is cleared. Here is your first payment." 

Tarkad handed him two copper coins. Kauskor stared at the coins, then at the clay tablet, his anger melting into surprise. "You... you actually came to pay? Very well, Tarkad. I will accept this, and I will mark it in my ledger."

Next, Tarkad went to the stables to find Dabasir. The camel trader was busy brushing a young donkey. When Dabasir saw Tarkad, he stopped and crossed his arms. 

"Well, boy? Did the desert teach you anything?"

Tarkad handed Dabasir a silver piece. "I owe you two copper pieces and a silver piece, Dabasir. Here is the silver. I will bring the coppers next month. I am following the plan of the three portions."

Dabasir looked at the silver coin, then let out a booming laugh. "You have the soul of a free man, Tarkad! I knew you had the strength. I accept your payment, and I will gladly trade with you again."

Tarkad spent the rest of the day visiting his other creditors. Some insulted him, and some grumbled, but every single one of them accepted the coins he offered. By sunset, Tarkad’s purse was lighter, but his heart felt lighter than it had in years. 

He walked back to his small rented room. He sat on his straw mat and looked at his ledger. 

He had paid his creditors two-tenths of his earnings. He had spent seven-tenths on basic food and rent. And in his leather bag, untouched and safe, lay one-tenth of his Damascus gold. 

He was no longer running. He was no longer hiding. He was a debtor with a plan, and he knew that with every passing month, the chains of his past were breaking.
