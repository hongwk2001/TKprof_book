Chapter 4
The Gold Lender's Scales

For six months, Tarkad followed the first two cures faithfully. 

Every time he was paid at the harbor, he took one-tenth of his earnings and placed it in a small leather bag. He lived simply, choosing only what was necessary and ignoring his desires. Slowly, the bag grew heavier. The sound of coins jingling in his pocket brought him a deep sense of satisfaction. 

Tarkad had saved his first bag of gold. 

But with gold came a new kind of worry. One evening, his brother-in-law, a young man named Hadan who was always full of grand ideas, approached him. Hadan had heard about Tarkad's savings. 

"Tarkad," Hadan said, his eyes wide with excitement. "I have a rare opportunity. A merchant from Damascus is selling a shipment of fine bronze shields at a very low price. If we buy them now, we can sell them to the King's guards next month for twice the price. I only need your bag of gold to secure the deal. We will split the profits!"

Tarkad was tempted. The thought of doubling his gold in a single month was intoxicating. But he remembered Arkad's fourth cure: *Guard your treasures from loss.* 

Anxious and unsure, Tarkad decided to visit Mathon the Gold Lender. 

When Tarkad entered the quiet shop, Mathon was sitting behind his table, adjusting the weights on his brass scales. 

"Ah, the young worker," Mathon said with a faint smile. "You look troubled. Have your pockets grown light again?"

"No," Tarkad said, placing his leather bag on the table. "They have grown heavy. I have saved my first bag of gold, Mathon. But now I must decide what to do with it."

Mathon picked up the bag, feeling its weight. He poured the gold coins onto one of the brass pans of his scales. He placed small stone weights on the other pan until the two sides hung perfectly level. 

"It is a good start," Mathon said. "You have earned the right to hold this. Now, tell me your worry."

Tarkad explained Hadan’s proposal. "He promises we will double the gold in a month. He is my sister's husband, and I want to help him. But I am afraid to lose what I worked so hard to save."

Mathon leaned back, rubbing his chin. "You are wise to be cautious, Tarkad. Gold is a strange thing. It is easy to find, but hard to keep. When a man has gold, many seek to take it from him. 

Tell me, does Hadan have security? Does he own land or goods that I can seize if the shields do not sell?"

"No," Tarkad admitted. "He has only his enthusiasm."

"And does he know the business of armor?" Mathon asked. "Has he ever bought or sold shields before?"

"No. He is a potter by trade."

Mathon gestured to the brass scales. "Look at these scales. They balance only when the weight on both sides is equal. In the world of gold, security is the weight that balances risk. If you lend your gold to a potter to buy shields, there is no balance. The risk is heavy, and your gold will fall into the abyss. 

If you want to help your brother-in-law, you must do it in a way that does not bring his burdens onto your own shoulders. Let me tell you a story. 

Once, in a far-off village, there lived a farmer who understood the language of beasts. Every evening, he liked to sit in the barn and listen to his animals talk. 

One night, he heard the heavy ox complaining to the donkey. 'My life is full of labor,' the ox groaned. 'From sunrise to sunset, I pull the heavy plow through the mud. My shoulders are sore, and I am always exhausted. But you, donkey, have an easy life. You are draped in a colorful blanket, and your only job is to carry the master to the market occasionally. Otherwise, you stand here and eat green grass.'

The donkey, being a sympathetic beast, wanted to help. 'My friend,' the donkey said, 'you work too hard. Tomorrow morning, when the plowman comes, lay down on the ground and groan. Pretend to be sick. If you do, the master will not make you work, and you can rest all day.'

The ox thanked the donkey and followed his advice. The next morning, when the plowman saw the ox was apparently sick, he reported it to the farmer. 

The farmer knew what had happened. He looked at the plowman and said, 'If the ox is sick, then take the donkey and harness him to the plow. He must do the ox's work today.'

All day, the donkey pulled the heavy plow. His shoulders grew sore, his legs trembled, and he realized how brutal the ox's labor truly was. When he returned to the barn that evening, he was exhausted and bitter. 

The ox looked at him with gratitude. 'You are my savior, donkey! Because of your wise advice, I enjoyed a day of peaceful rest.'

The donkey glared at him. 'I was a fool,' the donkey muttered. 'In trying to help you, I ended up pulling your plow. From now on, you can do your own work, or you can starve. I will not carry your burden again.'"

Mathon looked at Tarkad, letting the story settle in the quiet room. 

"Do you see the lesson, Tarkad? It is a noble thing to want to help your family. But if you give your savings to Hadan, you are taking the plow from his shoulders and putting it onto your own. If the deal fails, you will lose your gold, your relationship with Hadan will be ruined, and he will still be a poor potter. 

If you lend, lend only where there is security. If you invest, invest only with those who know the trade. If Hadan wants to buy shields, tell him to work for an armor merchant first and learn the business. Do not let his impatience cost you your hard-earned gold."

Tarkad looked at his gold on the scales. The desire for quick wealth had faded, replaced by a cold realization of the risk. 

"You are right, Mathon," Tarkad said, taking his bag of gold back. "I will not lend it to Hadan for this deal. I will keep saving until I find an investment where the principal is safe and the partner is wise."

"You have spoken like a true merchant," Mathon said with approval. "Keep your gold safe, Tarkad. It is better to have a small, safe interest than to lose your treasure in a gamble."

Tarkad thanked the lender and walked out of the shop, holding his purse tightly. He had not only saved his gold, but he had also saved himself from pulling another man's plow.
