Chapter 3
The Temple of Learning

Babylon was in a quiet crisis. 

King Sargon had returned from his victories in the north, only to find the royal treasury depleted and the streets filled with discontent. He stood in the throne room, looking out over the grand city. 

"Chancellor," the King addressed his advisor, "why is Babylon so poor? We built great canals, we built massive temples, yet our people have no money. Where has all the gold gone?"

"Your Majesty," the Chancellor replied, "the gold has flowed into the pockets of the few who know how to acquire it. The average citizen works hard, but they do not know how to hold onto their earnings."

"Then we must teach them," the King declared. "Who is the wealthiest man in the city? It is Arkad. Summon him. He must teach our people how to become prosperous, so that Babylon may once again be a city of abundance."

Arkad was summoned, and he accepted the task. He agreed to teach a class of one hundred citizens in the Temple of Learning. If these one hundred could master the laws of gold, they could go out and teach others across the city. 

Meanwhile, Tarkad had spent two weeks working hard at the harbor, carrying heavy crates of timber under the hot sun. He had saved a few coppers, but he felt he was only scratching the surface of what Arkad had promised. 

One evening, as Tarkad was leaving the docks, Mathon the Gold Lender stopped him. 

"Tarkad," Mathon said, "I have watched you. You have not missed a day of work, and you have not begged for a single loan. Because you have shown a willing spirit, I have used my influence to secure you a seat in the King's new class. Arkad will begin teaching tomorrow in the Temple of Learning."

Tarkad’s heart soared. "A seat in the Temple? Thank you, Mathon."

The next morning, Tarkad entered the grand courtyard of the Temple of Learning. The hall was filled with one hundred men—weavers, brickmakers, farmers, and young men like himself. They sat on low benches in a semi-circle. 

Arkad stood at the front, next to a large stand holding several blank clay tablets. 

"My fellow citizens," Arkad began, his voice carrying clearly through the hall. "By order of King Sargon, I stand before you to teach you how to cure a lean purse. Over the next seven days, I will give you seven cures for poverty. These are the rules that built my own fortune. 

Let us begin with the First Cure. 

To fatten your purse, you must start by saving a portion of everything you earn. For every ten coins you place in your purse, take out only nine to spend. The tenth coin must remain. Your purse will begin to fatten immediately, and the weight of it will bring a pleasant feeling to your soul. 

Tarkad, sitting in the third row, nodded. He was already doing this with his harbor wages, and he could testify that having even a few coppers left at the end of the week gave him a sense of dignity. 

Arkad turned to the second tablet. "The Second Cure is to control your expenditures. Many of you will say, 'How can I save one-tenth when my earnings are not even enough for my daily needs?' 

I tell you this: what we call our 'necessary expenses' will always grow to equal our income unless we protest. Do not confuse your desires with your necessities. Write down every item you wish to spend money on. Select only those that are truly necessary, and cross out the rest. Let your budget be your shield against useless spending. 

Tarkad remembered the honey cakes and fine tunics he used to buy on credit. He realized those were desires, not needs, and they had nearly ruined him. 

Arkad continued. "The Third Cure is to make your gold multiply. Saving is only the start. If your gold sits idle in a jar, it does no work. You must put your savings to work so that they may produce interest. Every copper must earn more coppers, creating a stream of wealth that flows constantly into your purse. 

The Fourth Cure is to guard your treasures from loss. Before you invest your savings, you must study the risks. Do not be tempted by schemes that promise impossible returns. Guard your gold by investing only where your principal is safe and where you can claim it if needed. Seek the advice of wise men who are experienced in handling money. 

The Fifth Cure," Arkad said, "is to make of your dwelling a profitable investment. If a man must pay high rent to a landlord for a small, dark room, he has nothing to show for his money. But if he owns his own home, with a patch of land where his children can play and his wife can grow herbs, he reduces his cost of living and builds a valuable asset. 

Arkad then looked out over the audience, his expression growing serious. "Now we come to the Sixth Cure, which is to ensure a future income. No man remains young forever. The day will come when you can no longer work. Therefore, you must prepare a safety net for your old age and for the protection of your family when you are gone. 

Arkad pointed out the window toward the massive outer walls of Babylon, where crews of men were carrying heavy stone blocks. 

"Look at the walls of Babylon," Arkad said. "Why do we build them? We build them to protect our city from invaders. We build them so that our families may sleep in peace, knowing they are safe from danger. 

Your savings must be your personal walls. You must buy land or make safe investments that will continue to yield income even when you are old, or if tragedy strikes your household. A man without a safety net is like a city without walls—defenseless against the storms of life."

Tarkad stared at the distant walls. He realized that his own life had been completely defenseless. A single week of illness would have left him starving on the streets. 

Arkad turned to the final tablet. "The Seventh and final Cure is to increase your ability to earn. You must cultivate your own powers, study to become wiser, and acquire more skill in your trade. If you are a brickmaker, study how to make stronger bricks faster. If you are a scribe, study how to carve cleaner letters. The more skill you have, the more the world will value your labor and the more gold you will earn."

Arkad stepped back from the tablets. "These are the seven cures, my friends. They are not magical spells; they are practical habits. Go forth, practice them, and teach them to your neighbors. Let us make Babylon a city of wealth once more."

As the class dismissed, Tarkad sat on his bench for a long moment. He looked at the notes he had carved onto his small wax tablet. 

He was still poor, but as he walked out of the temple into the golden afternoon light, he felt as though he had been given a map to a hidden treasure. And for the first time, he knew exactly where to take his first step.
