Chapter 1
The Golden City of Barefoot Men

The dust of Babylon was hot, and it tasted of copper. 

Tarkad pressed his back against the cool mud-brick wall of a narrow alleyway, pulling his tattered cloak tightly around his shoulders. He kept his head low, watching the crowded thoroughfare just a few paces away. Every time a tall figure in a clean linen tunic walked past, Tarkad’s stomach did a painful, empty flip. He was hiding. 

Earlier that morning, he had spotted Dabasir, the bony camel trader, marching down the market street. Tarkad owed Dabasir only two copper pieces, but to a man who had not eaten in two days, those two coppers might as well have been mountains of solid gold. Dabasir had a loud voice and a short temper, and Tarkad had no desire to be publicly shamed in the middle of the city. 

He waited until the crowd thickened, then slipped out of the alley. The sun was climbing toward its peak, baking the great metropolis. Babylon was the wonder of the ancient world, a city of towering ziggurats, lush hanging gardens, and massive brass gates. Yet, as Tarkad walked, he saw two very different Babylons. 

Down the center of the paved streets rode the wealthy. They sat proudly in noisy, gold-trimmed chariots, their horses adorned with colored leather and bronze bells. Merchants in rich purple robes argued over prices, their fingers glittering with heavy rings. But on either side of the road, in the dusty gutters, walked the other Babylon. 

These were the tradesmen, the weavers, the brickmakers, and the beggars. Most of them walked barefoot, their backs bent, their faces lined with exhaustion. Among them marched long columns of enslaved water carriers, carrying heavy goatskins from the Euphrates River to water the gardens of the wealthy. 

Tarkad looked down at his own dirty feet. He was young, healthy, and capable of hard work. Yet his purse was as empty as his stomach. He had spent the last two years chasing quick schemes, borrowing a silver piece here and a copper piece there, always believing that tomorrow his luck would change. Now, tomorrow had arrived, and he had nothing to show for it but a list of angry creditors.

Desperate for a distraction and perhaps a bit of shade, Tarkad walked toward the outer district where the craftsmen worked. He soon heard the familiar metallic ring of hammers and the low, melodious strumming of a stringed instrument. 

He stepped into the open courtyard of Bansir, the chariot builder. 

Bansir was sitting on a low stone wall, staring blankly at an unfinished chariot. His tools lay scattered in the dust at his feet. He was a broad-shouldered man with hands calloused from years of shaping wood and hammering brass, but today he looked completely defeated. 

Next to him sat Kobbi, the musician. Kobbi was gently plucking the bronze strings of a small lyre, coaxing a sad, drifting tune from the instrument. 

"You look like a man who has just watched his house burn down, Bansir," Tarkad said, stepping into the shade of the awning. 

Bansir did not look up, but he let out a heavy sigh. "In a way, Tarkad, it has. Or rather, the dream of my house has burned. I have been sitting here for hours, looking at this chariot. The wood is good, the brass fittings are strong, and the customer will pay me well when it is finished. Yet, I cannot bring myself to pick up my hammer."

Kobbi stopped plucking the strings. "A craftsman who hates his work? That is not like you, my friend."

"I do not hate the work," Bansir replied, finally raising his eyes. "I hate what the work buys me. Look at us. I have built the finest chariots in Babylon for twenty years. You, Kobbi, play the lyre so beautifully that even the palace guards stop to listen. And Tarkad here... you have the quick mind of a scribe. Yet, what do we have to show for all our years in this golden city?"

Bansir stood up, pacing the small courtyard. "Last night, I lay awake staring at the stars. I realized that if I work until my hair is white and my hands can no longer hold a chisel, I will still be just as poor as I am today. My wife will still wear faded rags. My children will grow up to be barefoot tradesmen, just like me. We live in the richest city on earth, surrounded by gold, yet not a single piece of it clings to us."

Kobbi nodded slowly, the sad tune starting up again on his lyre. "I know the feeling well. My purse is a constant disappointment. If I buy strings for my lyre, we eat only barley broth for a week. I want to wear fine robes. I want to have a purse that jingles with spare silver. But how? We work hard every day, yet our purses remain as thin as dried leaves."

Tarkad sat down on a block of unused cedar. The raw hunger in his stomach made his voice sharp. "You talk of empty purses, but at least you have homes and tools. I spent the morning ducking into doorways to avoid Dabasir. My stomach is hollow. I would work for anyone who would pay me a single copper today, but there is no work to be found that pays enough to buy both bread and freedom."

He looked at his friends. "We are like the water bearers. We carry the weight of Babylon on our shoulders, but the water we bring only quenches the thirst of others."

Bansir stopped his pacing. "Exactly! But why? Why do some men have gold flowing into their chests like the Euphrates, while we must bargain for every crust of bread? Is it because they are favored by the Gods? Or is there a secret we simply do not know?"

"There must be a secret," Kobbi agreed. "Some men start with nothing and build fortunes. Look at Arkad. He was once a scribe, no different from the rest of us. He worked in the hall of records for small wages. Yet now, he is the richest man in all of Babylon. Even the King seeks his advice on gold."

Tarkad’s eyes brightened slightly at the mention of Arkad. "I have seen Arkad. He rides in a chariot of polished cedar, and his robes are the color of a summer sky. But they say he is generous. He does not hoard his gold; he spends it, and yet his wealth only grows."

"Then why do we not ask him how he did it?" Bansir asked. 

"Arkad is a busy man," Kobbi warned. "He associates with lords and merchants. Why would he speak to barefoot craftsmen and a starving debtor?"

Tarkad stood up, his hunger momentarily forgotten as a spark of determination took hold. "If we do not ask, the answer will always be no. But Arkad is not the only man who knows about gold. There is also Mathon."

Bansir frowned. "Mathon? The gold lender? He is a cold man. He weighs every copper on his brass scales as if it were his own heart."

"He is cautious, not cold," Tarkad corrected. "Mathon sees the flow of all the gold in Babylon. He knows who is rising and who is falling. I am going to see him. I am going to ask him for a loan—not to buy bread, but to start a trade. If he refuses me, I will ask him how a man without gold can begin to earn it."

Kobbi laughed, a dry, skeptical sound. "A debtor asking a lender for financial wisdom? Mathon will throw you out of his shop, Tarkad."

"Perhaps," Tarkad said, adjusting his cloak and stepping back out into the blazing sun. "But staying here in the dust will not fill my purse or my stomach. I would rather be thrown out of a gold shop while trying to learn, than starve in an alleyway doing nothing."

With those words, Tarkad turned and walked back toward the bustling center of the city, his mind set on the shop of Mathon the Gold Lender. 

Behind him, the sad notes of Kobbi's lyre faded into the noise of the Babylonian afternoon.
