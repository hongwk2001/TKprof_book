Epilogue
The Nottingham Letters

Letter 1

From Professor Alfred Shrewsbury
Department of Archaeology, Nottingham University
Nottingham, England

To Professor Franklin Caldwell
British Scientific Expedition
Hillah, Iraq

November 7, 1934

Dear Franklin,

I am writing to confirm that the five clay tablets you excavated from the ruins of Babylon have arrived safely at our laboratory. I am absolutely thrilled. 

Your notes indicate that they were found inside a sealed clay jar, buried deep beneath the foundation stones of a large ancient villa near the Euphrates. The tablets are remarkably well-preserved, and the cuneiform script is exceptionally clean. 

I am starting the translation immediately. Judging by my initial look at the first tablet, these are not royal decrees or religious texts. They appear to be the personal diary and financial records of a citizen of Babylon. I will write to you again as soon as I have translated more. 

Sincerely,
Alfred Shrewsbury

---

Letter 2

November 22, 1934

Dear Franklin,

I have completed the translation of the first three tablets, and I am astonished by what they reveal. 

These tablets belonged to a man named Tarkad. He describes himself as a former debtor who returned to Babylon determined to pay off his debts and live as a free man. 

What is fascinating is that Tarkad’s financial problems in 3000 BC were exactly the same as the problems we face in England today. He was in debt to local merchants, camel traders, and gold lenders. 

To solve his problems, Tarkad carved a plan onto these tablets. He divided his income into three strict portions: 

First, one-tenth was saved to build his own wealth. 
Second, seven-tenths was used to support his household (rent, food, clothing). 
Third, two-tenths was divided proportionally among his creditors to systematically repay his debts. 

He records his visits to his creditors, including a food merchant named Kauskor and a camel trader named Dabasir. Some grumbled, but they all accepted the plan. In the third tablet, Tarkad proudly records that his savings are growing while his debts are steadily disappearing. 

It is incredible to think that five thousand years ago, a young man was using the exact same budgeting principles that modern financial planners recommend today. 

Sincerely,
Alfred

---

Letter 3

May 8, 1935

Dear Franklin,

Please forgive my long delay in writing. The reason for my silence is rather unusual. 

Last winter, my wife Evelyn and I found ourselves in a serious financial crisis. Our household expenses had got out of hand. We owed money to the grocer, the tailor, and we were struggling with our monthly mortgage payments to the bank. I was constantly stressed, and we could see no way out. 

One evening, as I was looking over the translation of Tarkad's tablets, Evelyn said to me, "Alfred, if this formula worked for a debtor in ancient Babylon, why shouldn't it work for us in Nottingham?"

We decided to try it. We treated Tarkad’s tablets not just as historical artifacts, but as active advice. 

Evelyn created a strict budget. We limited our living expenses to exactly seventy percent of my university salary. We took ten percent and placed it in a savings account at the bank. And we took the remaining twenty percent and divided it among our creditors. 

I wrote to our bank, the grocer, and the department store, explaining that we would pay them a fixed twenty percent of our income every month until the debts were cleared. 

The results have been miraculous, Franklin! Our creditors were surprised, but they gladly accepted the regular payments. Today, we have completely paid off the grocer and the tailor, our mortgage is under control, and we have a growing savings account that gives us peace of mind. 

Tarkad’s ancient plan has saved us from ruin. 

Sincerely,
Alfred

---

Letter 4

October 12, 1935

Dear Franklin,

I am writing this final letter to tell you that we are now completely debt-free. 

We continue to save our ten percent, and our savings are now earning a healthy interest at the bank. Evelyn and I feel like free citizens for the first time in our lives. 

If, in your continued excavations among the ruins of Babylon, you happen to encounter the ghost of that ancient traveler, Tarkad, please do me a favor. 

Whisper to him that his scribbling upon those clay tablets, so long ago, has earned the lifelong gratitude of a couple of university folks back here in England. 

Sincerely,
Alfred Shrewsbury
