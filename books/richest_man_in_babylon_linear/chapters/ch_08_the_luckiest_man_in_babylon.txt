Chapter 8
The Luckiest Man in Babylon

Twenty-five years had passed. 

Tarkad, now a mature man in his late forties, sat easily upon his spirited horse as his caravan approached the outer gates of Babylon. His hair had a touch of grey at the temples, and his face was lined by the desert sun, but his eyes were bright and full of energy. 

He was no longer the starving youth hiding in alleys. Tarkad was now one of the most successful merchant princes of Babylon, partnered with Mathon and the aging Arkad. He had cleared every debt decades ago, and his own name was carved on ledgers of great credit across the world. 

Riding next to him was Hadan Gula, the grandson of Tarkad's late friend and business partner, Arad Gula. 

Hadan Gula was a spoiled, cynical youth. He wore robes of the finest purple silk, and his fingers were heavy with gold rings and precious gems. He looked at the dusty road with disgust, constantly adjusting his seat. 

"Why must we travel by horse in this heat, Tarkad?" Hadan Gula complained. "A man of your wealth should ride in a soft carriage. And why do you continue to manage these caravans yourself? Work is for slaves and poor men. A rich man should only enjoy his leisure."

Tarkad smiled, looking at the distant walls of Babylon. "Work is not a curse, Hadan Gula. It is the best friend a man will ever have. It was work that saved me from the alleys, and it was work that built your grandfather's fortune."

Hadan Gula laughed, a bitter, arrogant sound. "My grandfather was a great lord! He was born to wealth and luxury. Do not compare his noble life to the sweat of laborers."

Tarkad reined in his horse, stopping at a small hill overlooking the green valley of Babylon. He looked squarely at the young man. 

"Your grandfather, Arad Gula, was not born a lord," Tarkad said, his voice calm but firm. "He was born a slave."

Hadan Gula gasped, his face turning pale. "A slave? That is impossible! He was the most respected merchant in Damascus!"

"It is the truth," Tarkad said. "And if you want to understand why his name is still honored, you must listen to his story. 

Years ago, when I was still a young man repaying my debts, I met Arad Gula. He was chained in a column of slaves, sold by a master who had gone bankrupt. He was sent to work in the brick kilns. 

But Arad Gula did not have the soul of a slave. He looked at the muddy clay and said to himself, *If I must make bricks, I will make the strongest bricks in Babylon.* 

He worked with such diligence and speed that the overseer noticed him. Soon, he was made a foreman, managing other workers. His master, seeing his value, gave him a small share of the profits from the bricks. 

Arad Gula did not spend those profits. He lived on basic barley broth and saved every copper. He used his savings to buy flour and honey, and at night, after his labor in the kilns was done, he baked sweet honey cakes. He carried them through the streets, selling them to the guards and merchants. 

I was one of his customers. I was just starting my own small trading stall, and I saw how hard he worked. We became friends. 

One day, Arad Gula’s master needed silver urgently to pay a debt. Because I had saved gold under the guidance of Arkad and Mathon, I was able to partner with Arad Gula. Together, we bought his freedom from his master. 

From that day on, Arad Gula was a free partner in my caravans. He loved work. He used to say to me, 'Tarkad, some men think work is only for the unfortunate. But work is the key that opens every lock. The Gods appreciate a man who puts his shoulder to the wheel, and they reward him with abundance.'

He moved to Damascus, managed our northern trade, and became a legend. He did not achieve this because of luck, and he did not inherit it. He built it with the callouses on his hands and the determination in his soul."

Tarkad looked at Hadan Gula’s bejeweled fingers. 

"You wear his gold, Hadan Gula, but you do not carry his spirit. You think work is beneath you. But a man who does not work is like a tree without roots—the first strong wind will blow him over. If you do not learn the value of labor, the wealth your grandfather built will slip through your fingers like dry sand."

Hadan Gula looked down at his hands. The arrogance had left his face, replaced by a quiet, deep shame. He slowly slipped the heavy gold rings off his fingers and placed them in his purse. 

"I did not know," Hadan Gula whispered. "I thought... I thought work was a sign of failure."

"Work is the ultimate measure of a free man," Tarkad said, putting his horse back into a walk. "It gives you self-respect, it gives you purpose, and it attracts the gold that builds empires."

Hadan Gula rode in silence for a long moment, looking at the city walls as they drew closer. 

"Tarkad," the young man said, his voice quiet but determined. "When we enter the gates, do not send me to the guest house. Send me to the warehouses. I want to learn the trade from the bottom. I want to work, as my grandfather did."

Tarkad smiled, a warm, proud look in his eyes. "Welcome to Babylon, Hadan Gula. Your grandfather would be proud."
