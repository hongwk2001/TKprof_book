Chapter 10
The Legacy of Babylon

The sun was setting over Babylon, casting a warm, golden-red glow across the sprawling, majestic city. 

From the high rooftop terrace of Arkad’s estate, the view was magnificent. The Euphrates River wound through the center of the metropolis like a ribbon of polished bronze, reflecting the crimson sky. In the distance, the towering Ziggurat of Bel rose majestically above the rooftops, its eternal fire sending a thin column of blue smoke into the quiet evening air. The sounds of the city below—the clank of copper vessels at the wells, the distant call of harbor guards, and the rumble of carts—drifted up as a soft, continuous hum. 

High upon this terrace, Arkad sat in a comfortable chair of carved cedar, wrapped in a soft woolen blanket from Damascus. His hair was now completely white, and his hands were thin and frail, showing the weight of his seventy-five years. But his eyes remained as bright and clear as they had been decades ago. 

Standing beside him, looking out over the busy city they both loved, was Tarkad. 

"The years have flown by like the desert wind, Tarkad," Arkad said, his voice soft but steady. "It seems like only yesterday you were standing in the shadows of Mathon's shop, a desperate youth begging for three silver pieces."

Tarkad smiled, a deep feeling of gratitude in his heart. "If you had given me those three silver pieces, Arkad, I would have eaten for a day and remained a beggar. Instead, you gave me a shovel, a seat in the Temple of Learning, and a clay tablet. You gave me a future."

Arkad nodded, looking at the city. "You have used the wisdom well. You worked hard at the harbor, cleared your debts copper by copper, built your caravans, and today, you are a pillar of Babylon’s trade. But more importantly, you have passed the wisdom on. Mathon told me how you took young Hadan Gula into your warehouses to teach him how to work."

"He is working hard, Arkad," Tarkad said. "He has his grandfather's strength in him. He only needed a path."

"That is the true secret of Babylon," Arkad said, gesturing to the city below. "Look at this grand valley, Tarkad. It is flat, dry, and hot. It has no forests, no mines, and not even natural stone for building. We are not even located on a natural trade route. By all rights, this place should be a barren wilderness, yet it is the wealthiest city in the world. Why? Because everything you see was made by the energy and wisdom of its people. 

Babylon is an outstanding example of man's ability to achieve great objectives, using whatever means are at his disposal. We had only two natural resources—a fertile soil and the water in the Euphrates. But our engineers did not complain. They built dams and immense irrigation canals, some of which are so wide that when empty of water, a dozen horses can be ridden abreast along their bottoms. They reclaimed the swamps at the mouths of the rivers. They turned a desert into an agricultural paradise, yielding crops the like of which the world has never seen before. 

And look at our walls, Tarkad. These walls, started by King Nabopolassar and finished by our great King Nebuchadnezzar, are a wonder of the world. They stand one hundred and sixty feet high—equivalent to fifteen men standing on each other's shoulders—and they stretch for miles around our city. They are so wide at the top that a six-horse chariot can be driven around them. Invading armies with ten thousand horsemen and hundreds of regiments of foot soldiers have laid siege to us for years, but always in vain. The walls have never been captured, for they protect a people who are productive, organized, and wise in the ways of finance. 

We are a city of financiers, traders, and artisans. We invented money as a means of exchange, written titles to property, and promissory notes. While the rest of the world is still hacking at trees with stone axes, our smiths forge bronze tools and our jewelers create works of art that will be treasured for generations. 

But I know, Tarkad, that kings will pass, and empires will fall. A day may come when the energizing human forces that maintain this city are withdrawn, and these great walls will disintegrate and go back to the earth from which they were built. The winds will scatter the desert dust over our palaces, and our ziggurats will become earthen hills, forgotten by travelers. 

Yet, even if the bricks of Babylon crumble to dust, the wisdom we carved on these clay tablets will endure. Money is the medium by which success is measured, and it is governed today by the exact same simple laws that will govern it six thousand years from now. 

I am old, Tarkad, and my time is drawing to a close. I am passing this scribe’s legacy to you."

Arkad reached onto the table beside him and picked up the heavy clay tablet, wrapped in its faded blue silk cloth. He placed it in Tarkad's hands. 

"Keep these tablets safe. Teach the seven cures to the young. Write the five laws on the hearts of the willing. Let the world know that the rules of money do not change with the kings or the seasons."

Tarkad held the heavy tablet against his chest, just as he had on the trail to Damascus so many years ago. 

"I will protect them, Arkad," Tarkad promised. "And I will teach them to anyone who has the determination to find the way."

"Then I am content," Arkad said, closing his eyes with a peaceful smile. "Go now, Tarkad. Go build your caravans. The future belongs to those who work, save, and grow."

Tarkad bowed respectfully to his mentor and walked down the stone stairs of the estate. 

As he stepped out into the streets, the bells of the evening caravan began to ring, their clear notes echoing through the city. Tarkad walked with a firm, confident stride, ready to meet the tomorrow he had built. 

And high above, the golden city of Babylon stood strong, a monument to the timeless wisdom of the men who walked its streets.
