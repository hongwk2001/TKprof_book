Chapter 4
The Gold Lender's Scales

For six long, arduous months, Tarkad followed the first two cures for a lean purse with absolute, unwavering faithfulness. 

Every single time he was paid at the bustling Euphrates harbor—where he spent his days carrying heavy bales of wool, barley, and building stone under a merciless sun that baked the river clay to dust—he did not touch his entire wage. The moment the copper and silver coins were pressed into his sweat-slicked palm by the shouting harbor master, Tarkad immediately took one-tenth of the sum and placed it into a small, sturdy leather bag that hung beneath his tunic, close to his chest. 

At first, this habit felt like a heavy sacrifice. When the sweet aroma of roasted meats, seasoned with wild cumin and garlic, wafted from the street vendors' carts, his empty stomach would rumble in protest. When he saw other harbor workers spending their evenings drinking the sweet, dark date wine of Babylon and wearing soft, new linen tunics, he felt the sting of his own poverty. His old robe was thin and patched with coarse threads, and his feet were calloused from walking barefoot on the gravel paths. Yet he gritted his teeth and walked past the taverns. He lived on simple barley cakes, wild green onions, and clear water from the public wells. He chose only what was absolutely necessary to keep his strength, ignoring his desires and his pride. 

Slowly, almost imperceptibly, the miracle began to happen. The leather bag grew heavier. It no longer hung flat against his ribs; it took on a round, comforting shape. And when he walked back to his modest room in the evening, the sound of the coins jingling against one another brought him a deep, satisfying sense of peace. He began to realize that the gold and silver he kept was a loyal servant that belonged to him, while the money he spent belonged to others. For the first time in his life, Tarkad had saved a substantial bag of gold. He was no longer a beggar living hand-to-mouth; he was a man with capital.

But with gold came a new and unexpected kind of worry. He had never owned anything of real value before, and the fear of losing it began to haunt his thoughts. He kept the bag hidden beneath a loose brick in the corner of his room, checking on it several times a night to make sure it had not been stolen by thieves or nibbled by rats.

One evening, as the shadows of the great temples stretched long across the clay streets, Tarkad’s brother-in-law, a young man named Hadan, approached him. Hadan had married Tarkad’s sister, a gentle and devoted woman who wanted nothing more than to see her husband succeed. Hadan was always full of grand ideas, talking constantly of schemes that would make him rich overnight, though his pockets were usually empty and his hands were rarely calloused. Somehow, Hadan had heard rumors of Tarkad’s secret savings.

"Tarkad!" Hadan said, his eyes wide and bright with a feverish excitement as he stepped into Tarkad’s small lodging. "I have come to you because a rare, once-in-a-lifetime opportunity has presented itself. A merchant from Damascus has just arrived at the northern gates with a cargo of fine bronze shields. Because he is in a great hurry to return to his own land before the winter rains block the mountain passes, he is selling the entire shipment at a very low price. If we can buy them now, we can hold them for just a month. The King's guards are planning to recruit new soldiers next month, and they will need armor. We can sell the shields to the palace commanders for twice what we paid. It is a certain profit! I only need your bag of gold to secure the purchase, and we will split the gains equally. You will double your wealth in a single moon!"

Tarkad felt a strong pull in his chest. The thought of doubling his hard-earned gold in a mere thirty days was incredibly intoxicating. It would take him another six months of breaking his back at the harbor to save another bag like this one. But with Hadan’s plan, it would happen effortlessly. 

Yet, as he looked at Hadan’s flushed, eager face, a cold wind of caution blew through his mind. He remembered Arkad's fourth cure: *Guard your treasures from loss.* He knew that Hadan was a potter by trade, a man who spent his days shaping wet clay on a wheel, not a merchant who knew the ways of weapons and trade routes. 

Anxious and deeply unsure of what to do, Tarkad decided to seek the counsel of one who knew the secrets of money better than anyone else: Mathon, the Gold Lender of Babylon.

***

The evening was warm when Tarkad walked down the grand highway of the King. He turned into a narrow side street and entered the shop of Mathon, the dealer in gold, rare jewels, and expensive fabrics. The front of the shop was quiet and filled with the rich smells of cedarwood and dried spices. Colorful rugs hung from the walls, and brass bowls gleamed in the dim light of oil lamps. 

Glancing neither to the right nor the left at the beautiful things on display, Tarkad passed through the heavy curtain that led to the living quarters at the rear. There he found the sallow-faced Mathon lounging comfortably upon a thick, woven rug. A black slave named Andol was serving him a meal of roasted lamb and dried figs.

Mathon looked up, his sallow face breaking into a friendly, knowing smile. "Ah, the young harbor worker," he said. "You look troubled, Tarkad. Have your pockets grown light again? Have you come to borrow a few coppers to buy a meal, or has some clever merchant enticed you to spend what you do not have?"

"No, Mathon," Tarkad replied, stepping forward and placing his heavy leather bag on the table. "They have not grown light. In fact, they have grown heavy. For six months, I have worked and saved. I have followed the cures of Arkad, and this is my first bag of gold. But now that I have it, I am filled with anxiety. I do not know what to do with it, and I crave your wise advice."

Mathon’s eyebrows rose in surprise. He leaned back on his elbow and gestured to Andol. "Hear that, Andol! This young man comes to the gold lender not to ask for gold, but to ask for advice! Many men come to this room with tears in their eyes, begging for coins to pay for their foolish mistakes. But they never want my advice. Yet, who is more qualified to advise than the one who sees the ruin of fools and the success of the wise every single day? 

"Sit with me, Tarkad," Mathon continued, his voice warm. "You shall be my guest for the evening. Andol, bring a rug for my friend. Bring him a plate of this juicy lamb, some of the barley bread, and fill the large cup with our best wine. He has earned the right to eat at my table."

As Tarkad sat and ate, the heavy weight of his anxiety began to lift. When the meal was finished, Mathon reached out and picked up Tarkad’s leather bag. He felt its weight, nodding with approval, and then poured the glittering coins onto one of the brass pans of his scales. He carefully placed small, polished stone weights on the other pan until the two sides hung perfectly level in the air.

"It is a good, honest start," Mathon said, watching the scales balance. "You have earned the right to hold this treasure. It represents your labor, your self-discipline, and your future. Now, tell me what troubles you."

Tarkad explained Hadan’s proposal in detail. He spoke of the bronze shields, the Damascus merchant, the King's guards, and the promise of doubling his gold in a month. "Hadan is my sister's husband," Tarkad said quietly. "My sister loves him dearly and begs me to help him. She believes this is the opportunity that will make them prosperous. I want to help my family, but I am terrified of losing the gold I worked so hard to save. What should I do?"

Mathon leaned back, rubbing his chin, his sallow face turning serious. "You are wise to hesitate, Tarkad. Gold is a strange and slippery thing. It is easy to find ways to spend it, but hard to keep it safe. When a man has gold, many who have none will try to convince him to part with it. Tell me, does Hadan have security? Does he own land, houses, or valuable merchandise that I could seize if the guards do not buy the shields?"

"No," Tarkad admitted. "He has no land and no property. He lives in a rented room and has only his wheel and his clay."

"And does he know the business of armor?" Mathon asked, his sharp eyes locking onto Tarkad's. "Has he ever bought or sold shields before? Does he know the quality of Damascus bronze, or how to negotiate with the palace commanders?"

"No," Tarkad said. "He is a potter. He has only his enthusiasm and his dreams."

Mathon pointed a sallow finger at the brass scales on the table. "Look at these scales, Tarkad. They balance only when the weight on both sides is equal. In the world of gold, security is the weight that balances risk. If you lend your gold to a potter to buy shields, there is no balance. The risk is incredibly heavy, and your gold will fall into the abyss of loss. 

"If you desire to help your brother-in-law, you must do so in a way that does not bring his burdens onto your own shoulders. Let me tell you a story that is rarely told over the blacksmiths' fires, but contains great wisdom for those who will listen.

"Once, in a far-off village near Nineveh, there lived a farmer who was blessed by the gods with the ability to understand the language of beasts. Every evening, when the sun went down, he liked to sit in the farmyard and listen to his animals talk.

"One night, he heard the heavy ox complaining bitterly to the donkey. 'My life is full of endless labor,' the ox groaned. 'From the rising of the sun to its setting, I pull the heavy wooden plow through the deep, sticky mud. My shoulders are raw, my legs are weary, and I am always exhausted. But you, donkey, have a life of pure luxury. You are draped in a beautiful, colorful blanket, and your only job is to carry our master to the market occasionally. Otherwise, you stand here in the shade, eating sweet green grass and resting all day long.'

"Now, the donkey, despite his stubborn nature, was a sympathetic beast and felt sorry for his friend. 'My good friend,' the donkey said, 'you do work far too hard, and I want to help ease your burden. Tomorrow morning, when the slave comes to harness you to the plow, do not stand up. Lay down upon the dirt and bellow loudly, as if you are sick and dying. If you do this, the master will not force you to work, and you can rest in the barn all day.'

"The ox thanked the donkey warmly and followed his advice. The next morning, when the slave came to the barn, the ox lay on the ground and groaned, refusing to move. The slave returned to the farmer and reported that the ox was sick and could not pull the plow.

"The farmer, who knew exactly what had happened, looked at the slave and said, 'If the ox cannot work, the plowing must still go on. Take the donkey, harness him to the plow, and make him do the work today.'

"All that day, the donkey found himself pulling the heavy wooden plow through the rough dirt. His legs trembled with fatigue, his shoulders grew sore where the wood chafed his skin, and he realized how brutal the ox's labor truly was. When he was finally released at sunset and returned to the barn, he was exhausted, bitter, and angry.

"The ox looked at him with big, grateful eyes. 'You are my savior, donkey! Because of your wise advice, I have enjoyed a day of peaceful rest and sweet straw.'

"The donkey glared at him, his ears flattened. 'I was a fool,' the donkey muttered. 'In trying to help you, I ended up pulling your plow. From now on, you can do your own work, or you can starve. I will not carry your burden again.' And from that day on, they never spoke to one another, and their friendship was ended.

"Can you see the moral of this story, Tarkad?" Mathon asked.

"It is a good story," Tarkad replied, "but I want to make sure I understand it fully."

"The moral is simple," Mathon explained. "If you want to help your friend or your family, do so in a way that does not bring their burdens upon yourself. Do not be like the simplehearted donkey who ended up pulling the plow because of his desire to be helpful."

***

Tarkad looked at the scales and then at the gold lender. "I see the wisdom in your words. But tell me, Mathon, you lend gold to many people. Do they not repay you? How do you decide who is a safe risk?"

Mathon smiled, the sallow skin around his eyes wrinkling. "I would not be a successful lender if I did not get my gold back with its proper rental. The lender must be wise, and he must judge carefully. He must decide whether his gold will perform a useful purpose for the borrower and return to him in safety, or whether it will be wasted by someone who cannot use it wisely. 

"Come, let me show you the secrets of my token chest. It will tell you the stories of the people of Babylon."

Mathon reached down and brought out a chest that was as long as his arm. It was covered in red pigskin and decorated with intricate bronze designs of lions and bulls. He placed it on the floor between them and opened the heavy lid. Tarkad leaned forward, his eyes wide. Inside the chest lay many small, strange objects, each wrapped in cloth or resting on velvet.

"Whenever I lend to someone, I require a token," Mathon said, picking up a beautiful bronze necklace that lay upon a piece of faded scarlet cloth. "It stays here until the loan is fully paid. If they repay, I return the token. If they fail, the token remains to remind me of my mistake. 

"This bronze necklace and the scarlet cloth will always stay in my chest. They belonged to a dear friend of mine, a merchant with whom I traded for many years. He was a wise and honorable man, but he went to the East and brought back a bride. She was a dazzling creature, beautiful but vain, and not like the women of Babylon. She craved luxury, and my friend spent his gold lavishly to satisfy her every whim. When all his wealth was gone, he came to me in great distress. I counseled him, and I lent him gold to rebuild his business. He swore by the Great Bull that he would repay me. 

"But his wife's demands did not stop. In a bitter quarrel, she dared him to pierce his own heart, and in his despair, he drove a knife into his chest. When she saw what she had done, she was filled with remorse and threw herself into the Euphrates River. The loan was never repaid, and my friend is gone. This token tells me, Tarkad, that humans in the grip of intense, irrational emotions are never safe risks for the gold lender.

"But look at this," Mathon continued, reaching into the chest and pulling out a simple ring carved from ox bone. "This belongs to a poor farmer. A few years ago, the locusts came and devoured all his crops. His family was starving. He came to me, and I lent him a small sum to buy grain. He was an honest man, and as soon as his next harvest came in, he repaid me every copper. 

"Later, he came to me again. A traveler had told him of a breed of goats in a distant northern land that had long, soft hair that could be woven into rugs of extraordinary beauty. The farmer wanted to buy a herd, but had no gold. I trusted his work ethic and his knowledge of animals, so I lent him the gold to make the journey. Today, his herd is growing, and next year, he will bring me the finest rugs in Babylon. I will sell them to the nobles, and soon I will return his ox-bone ring. This loan was safe because it was based on honest labor and practical knowledge.

"But if people borrow to cover up their own foolishness, you must be extremely cautious. Look at this heavy gold bracelet inset with precious gems." Mathon held up the glittering ornament. "The lady who owns this is old, wrinkled, and talks so much that she drives me mad. Once, her family had great wealth, but they lost it through bad investments. She has a son whom she wanted to make into a wealthy merchant. She came to me and borrowed gold so her son could buy a partnership with a caravan owner. 

"But the caravan owner was a rascal. He took the gold, led the boy into a distant city, and then snuck away in the night while the boy was sleeping, leaving him penniless. Now, I get no interest on my loan—only endless talk and complaints from the mother. The bracelet is valuable enough to cover the debt, but the loan itself was a failure. The mother's romantic dreams for her son blinded her to the risk.

"Now, this piece of rough packing rope," Mathon said, holding up a small knot of hemp, "belongs to Nebatur, the camel trader. Whenever Nebatur needs to buy a new herd of camels, he brings me this knot, and I lend him whatever he needs. He is a wise, experienced trader who knows the value of every beast in the desert. I trust his judgment completely. Honorable merchants like Nebatur are the lifeblood of Babylon. It profits me to help them, because their success keeps the city prosperous.

"But look at this Egyptian turquoise beetle." Mathon tossed the carved stone onto the floor with a look of disdain. "The young man who owns this does not care if I ever get my gold back. When I ask him for payment, he simply whines that bad luck pursues him. The token actually belongs to his father, an honorable farmer of small means who pledged his own land and his herd to support his son's wild ideas. The young man wanted to get rich quickly, and his business collapsed because he had no experience. 

"Youth is ambitious, Tarkad. Youth always wants to take shortcuts to wealth and the luxury that comes with it. But youth does not understand that hopeless debt is like a deep, dark pit. You can fall into it in a single day, but you will struggle for years to climb out. It is a pit of sorrow and regret, where the sun does not shine and your nights are filled with sleepless torment. Yet, I do not discourage borrowing. Borrowing is good if it is for a wise, productive purpose. I made my own fortune using borrowed gold. But it must be done with caution."

***

Mathon looked at Tarkad, his expression soft but firm. "If Hadan came to me asking to borrow fifty pieces of gold, I would ask him what he plans to do with it. If he told me he wanted to buy bronze shields, I would ask him what he knows about the armor trade. He knows nothing. His ambition is noble, but it is not practical. I would tell him no.

"Tell me, Tarkad, how many months did it take you to save this bag of gold?"

"Six months of hard labor at the harbor," Tarkad replied, looking at the brass scales.

"And during those months, did you deny yourself the things your heart desired?"

"Yes, every single day. I wore patched robes and ate only barley and onions."

"Then it took half a year of your life's labor to save this treasure," Mathon said, his voice dropping to a whisper. "Do you think your sister would want you to risk half a year of your life, all your sweat and self-denial, just so her husband could experiment with a trade he knows nothing about?"

"No," Tarkad admitted. "If I explained it to her in those words, she would not want me to risk it."

"Then go to them," Mathon advised. "Say to Hadan: 'I have labored for six months, denying myself food and comfort to save this bag of gold. I want to see you succeed, Hadan. If you will bring me a detailed plan that my wise friend Mathon approves, a plan where the risk is small and the profit is certain, I will gladly lend you a portion of my savings. But I will not risk the entire savings of my labor on an untested dream.' 

"Do this, Tarkad. If Hadan has the soul of a true merchant, he will find a way to start small and prove he can succeed. If he fails with a small amount, he will not owe you more than he can hope to repay, and your treasure will still be safe.

"Keep your gold, Tarkad. Do not let it leave your pouch unless you have a safe way to pull it back again. Remember what I have carved beneath the lid of my token box."

Tarkad looked down at the carved wood. In bold, deep letters, the words read:

*BETTER A LITTLE CAUTION THAN A GREAT REGRET*

Tarkad reached out and took his leather bag of gold from the table. The intoxicating desire for quick wealth had faded, replaced by the clear, cool light of wisdom. He felt a deep sense of relief.

"You are right, Mathon," Tarkad said, slipping the bag back beneath his tunic. "I will not lend the gold to Hadan for this shields deal. I will keep my savings safe, and I will only invest them when I am certain the principal is secure."

"You speak like a true merchant," Mathon said, clapping him on the shoulder as Andol filled their cups once more. "Keep your gold safe, and let it work for you slowly and surely. It is far better to earn a small, safe return than to lose your entire treasure in a gamble."

Tarkad thanked the wise lender and walked out into the cool Babylonian night. He held his purse tightly, his heart at peace, knowing that he had saved himself from pulling another man's plow.
