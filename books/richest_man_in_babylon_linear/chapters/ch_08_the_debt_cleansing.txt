Chapter 7
The Debt Cleansing

The dusty road from Damascus was long, hot, and choked with the grit of a hundred dry miles. But as the merchant caravan finally crested the southern hills overlooking the Euphrates, the heavy brass bells hanging from the lead camels began to ring with a triumphant, rhythmic cadence that matched the beating of Tarkad’s heart. 

For many days, Tarkad had ridden his horse alongside the lumbering beasts, his body weary but his mind clearer than it had ever been. He had gone to Damascus as a desperate young man fleeing his own shadow, but under the guidance of Dabasir and the protective eye of Arkad’s caravan, he had worked hard and traded wisely. He had adhered strictly to the rules of investment he had been taught, seeking the counsel of veteran merchants and refusing the temptations of quick, unearned wealth. Now, he returned to the golden city of Babylon not as a beggar, but as a man of business. Securely packed in his saddlebags were two heavy leather pouches filled with silver and gold—his honest share of the caravan’s trading profits.

Yet, as the massive bronze gates of Babylon loomed closer, casting their long shadows over the arriving travelers, Tarkad felt no desire to seek out the noisy taverns or purchase the fine, dyed robes that he once would have craved. The memory of his hunger was still fresh, but fresher still was the memory of Dabasir’s words: "your debts are yours enemies who have run you out of Babylon."

Instead of hiding in the winding, narrow alleys of the city’s poorer quarters, Tarkad guided his horse straight toward the bustling central merchant district. The smells of roasting meat, sweet sesame cakes, and fresh barley beer wafted from the eating houses, but Tarkad kept his eyes fixed ahead. He did not stop until he reached the heavy timbered doorway of the shop belonging to Mathon, the gold lender.

The shop was quiet, smelling of old parchment, drying clay, and the faint, metallic scent of copper and silver coins. Mathon sat behind his heavy wooden table, his scales resting beside him. He was carefully writing in a large ledger by the light of a small oil lamp. When the shadow of Tarkad’s tall figure crossed the threshold, the gold lender looked up, his sharp eyes narrowing as he assessed the young man.

"Tarkad," Mathon said, his voice level and cool. "You return to Babylon. Your horse looks strong and well-fed, and you hold your head straight upon your shoulders. Did Damascus offer you the fortune you sought?"

Tarkad stepped into the shop, unbuckled the two leather pouches from his belt, and placed them onto the wooden table with a heavy, metallic clink. "Damascus was profitable, Mathon," Tarkad said quietly. "I worked hard, I watched the markets, and I listened to the wisdom of those who knew the laws of gold. But I did not come here today to boast of my profits, nor did I come to borrow more. I am here to face my debts. I want to write them down, every single one of them, and I want to find a way to repay every copper I owe."

A warm, rare smile softened the stern lines of Mathon’s face. He set down his reed pen and gestured toward a shelf where several damp, grey clay tablets lay ready for the stylus. "A man who wishes to pay his debts is a man who has found his soul," Mathon said softly. "Too many men run from their creditors, thinking only of borrowing more, until they are swallowed by the desert or sold into the slave pens. Sit, Tarkad. Let us carve your path to freedom."

Tarkad sat on a low wooden stool, and Mathon placed a fresh, soft clay tablet and a sharp wooden stylus before him. For the next hour, the young man dug deep into his memories of the dark years, listing every person he had borrowed from during his days of desperate hunger and foolish extravagance. 

"Let us be precise, Tarkad," Mathon instructed, pointing to the wet clay. "An honest man does not hide from the numbers. Write down the name of every creditor, and next to it, the exact amount of silver and copper you owe them."

With a steady hand, Tarkad began to carve the records. 

First, he wrote the name of Fahru, the cloth weaver, to whom he owed two pieces of silver and six pieces of copper for a fine cloak he had bought to look wealthy. 

Next was Sinjar, the couch maker, whom he owed one piece of silver. 

Then came Ahmar, a young man he had once called his closest friend, who had lent him three pieces of silver and one piece of copper when Tarkad’s purse was completely empty. 

He carved the name of Zankar, another former companion, who had trusted him with four silver pieces and seven coppers. 

Next was Askamir, to whom he owed one silver piece and three coppers. 

Then came Harinsir, the jewelmaker, who had sold him a small trinket on credit for six pieces of silver and two coppers. 

He added Diarbeker, an old friend of his father’s, who had lent him four pieces of silver and one piece of copper out of respect for his family. 

Then he wrote the name of Alkahad, the house owner, who had rented him a small room and whom he owed fourteen pieces of silver in unpaid rent. 

He did not omit Mathon himself, carving the gold lender's name and the debt of nine pieces of silver. 

Finally, he wrote the name of Birejik, the farmer, who had sold him grain and was owed one piece of silver and seven coppers.

When the list was complete, Tarkad stared at the names and numbers carved into the grey clay. The total was daunting—one hundred and nineteen pieces of silver and one hundred and forty-one pieces of copper. It was a massive sum for a young man who had so recently been starving.

Mathon leaned forward, tapping the edge of the tablet. "To clear this weight from your shoulders, Tarkad, you must follow a strict and ancient formula. It is the plan of the three portions, and under my advice, you will commit to it. It has led many men out of the deepest ruins of debt and into a life of wealth and self-respect. Hear it well:

"First, the plan provides for your own future. One-tenth of all you earn, from this day forward, must be set aside as your own to keep. It is your savings, and you must never spend it, for it is the seed of your future wealth. A man who keeps gold and silver in his purse that he does not need to spend is good to his family, loyal to his king, and walks with a proud heart. But the man who has nothing is bitter, unkind to his family, and indifferent to his king.

"Second, the plan provides that you must support your household. Seven-tenths of all you earn shall be used to provide a home, food to eat, clothes to wear, and a bit extra for the enjoyment of life. You must live strictly within this limit. It is the core of the plan. You must never spend more than seven-tenths, and you must never buy what you cannot pay for out of this portion.

"Third, the plan provides that your debts shall be paid. Therefore, each time you receive your earnings, two-tenths of the total shall be divided honorably, fairly, and proportionally among all those who have trusted you and to whom you are indebted. In this way, every creditor will be paid in full over time, and your self-respect will return."

Tarkad looked at the tablet, his brow furrowed. "But Mathon, will my creditors accept this? Some of them are very angry with me. They have threatened me, and they want all of their gold immediately. Two-tenths divided among ten men will mean only a few coppers for each of them every month. Will they not throw the coins in my face?"

"A creditor is a human being, Tarkad," Mathon replied with a knowing smile. "He wants his gold, yes. But he knows that a debtor who hides in the dark will pay him nothing. He would far rather receive a small, guaranteed payment every month from a man of honor who faces him directly, than receive empty promises and see no coins at all. Go to them. Face them like a free man. Show them this tablet, explain the plan, and give them your promise."

Tarkad stood up, holding the wet clay tablet in his hands. He felt a strange new strength in his chest. "I will do as you say, Mathon. I will face my enemies and reward my friends."

Before he began his rounds, Tarkad sought out his wife, Vira. In the darkest days of his poverty, when the creditors had swarmed his home and there was no bread to eat, she had been forced to return to the house of her father in shame. Tarkad went to her father's house, not to beg, but to show her the profits he had earned and the plan he had carved on clay. 

Vira looked at the tablet and then at Tarkad’s steady eyes. Seeing the change in him, she chose to return to his side, determined to help him rebuild their home and live within the seven-tenths of his earnings.

With his wife’s support, Tarkad set out into the streets of Babylon to visit his creditors. 

His first stop was the stall of Kauskor, the food merchant, who had once chased him from the eating house when Tarkad had tried to beg for a scrap of bread. When Kauskor saw Tarkad approaching, his face hardened, and he crossed his arms over his chest.

"I have no food for you, Tarkad!" Kauskor shouted across the busy street. "And I have no patience left. Repay the coppers you owe me before you dare show your face here again!"

Tarkad did not flinch or turn away. He stepped up to the wooden counter, placed the clay tablet before the merchant, and looked him in the eye. "Kauskor, I have returned from Damascus. I have worked hard, and I have a plan to repay my debts. I owe you ten copper pieces. I cannot pay you the entire sum today, but I have committed to a plan under Mathon's guidance. Every time the moon is full, I will bring you two-tenths of my earnings, divided fairly among my creditors. Today, I bring you two copper pieces. It is the first of many payments, and I give you my word as a free man that I will return every month until the debt is cleared."

Kauskor looked at the two copper coins sitting on the counter, then at the clay tablet, and finally at Tarkad’s calm, resolute face. The anger slowly drained from the merchant's eyes, replaced by quiet astonishment. 

"You... You actually returned to pay me?" Kauskor mumbled, picking up the coppers. "Many men borrow and run, never to be seen again. To see a debtor return with coins and a written plan is a rare thing. Very well, Tarkad. I accept your payment, and I will mark it in my ledger. May the gods speed your work."

Encouraged by this first success, Tarkad walked through the city to find Ahmar, the friend he had once borrowed three pieces of silver and a copper from. Ahmar was sitting outside a leather shop, and when he saw Tarkad, his expression grew bitter. 

"So, the runaway has returned," Ahmar said scornfully. "Do you come to borrow more, or do you have the silver you took from me under the guise of friendship?"

Tarkad felt a hot flush of shame, but he stood his ground. "Ahmar, I was wrong to run, and I was wrong to neglect my debt to you. I have no excuses. But I have returned to make it right. I have a plan to pay every man I owe, and I will deal fairly with all. I cannot pay you in full today, but I bring you your share of my first earnings." 

He handed Ahmar the silver and copper coins that represented his portion. Ahmar looked at the coins, then spat on the ground, reviling Tarkad for his slowness. "This is but a pittance compared to what you took! You expect me to wait months for the rest?"

"I expect nothing but your patience, Ahmar," Tarkad said quietly. "I will pay you this portion every month, and no more. If you do not wish to accept it, I will distribute it to the others, but I assure you that if you are patient, you will be paid in full." 

Seeing Tarkad’s determination, Ahmar grumbled and pocketed the coins, turning his back but offering no further insults.

Next, Tarkad sought out Birejik, the farmer, who was selling grain from a wagon near the market. When Tarkad explained the plan and handed him his share, Birejik was so pleased that his eyes filled with tears. "You are an honest man, Tarkad! I didst badly need this help, for the drought has been hard on my crops. I will gladly accept whatever you can bring each month."

However, not all visits were easy. When Tarkad visited Alkahad, the house owner, the older man flew into a rage. Alkahad was a wealthy, disagreeable landlord who had threatened to have Tarkad arrested when he lived in his rooms.

"Three coppers?" Alkahad shrieked, waving the coins in the air. "This is an insult! I want my fourteen pieces of silver now! I will make trouble for you in the courts, Tarkad! I will have you thrown into the slave pens!"

Tarkad remained calm, remembering Mathon's advice. "Alkahad, if you throw me into the slave pens, I will earn nothing, and you will receive nothing. But if I am permitted to work, well-fed and without worry, I will be able to pay you every month until the debt is cleared. The choice is yours. Take this portion, or receive nothing at all."

Alkahad stared at Tarkad, his mouth opening and closing in frustration. Finally, he snatched the coins and muttered, "Take your tablet and go, Tarkad. But if you fail to bring me my coins next month, I will have the guards at your door."

By the time the sun began to set, casting a golden-red glow over the city of Babylon, Tarkad’s purse was much lighter, but his heart felt as light as air. He had visited every creditor on his list, and though some had reviled him, all had accepted their coins. He had faced his enemies, and he had proven to himself that he had the soul of a free man.



Tablet No. IV

Again the moon shines full in the velvet sky above Babylon. I, Tarkad, have worked hard with a free and focused mind. My good wife, Vira, hath supported my intentions, helping me manage our home with great care. Because of our wise determination, I have earned during the past moon, buying camels of sound wind and good legs for Nebatur—the great trader commissioned by our king Sargon to purchase herds for the royal expedition—the sum of nineteen pieces of silver.

This sum I have divided strictly according to the plan. One-tenth, which is one piece of silver and nine coppers, have I set aside to keep as my own, placing it in my leather chest where no man hath claim. Seven-tenths, which is thirteen silver pieces and three coppers, have I divided with my good wife to pay for our living, food, and rent. Two-tenths, which is three silver pieces and eight coppers, have I divided among my creditors as evenly as could be done in coppers.

I did not see Ahmar on my rounds, but I left his share with his wife, who thanked me. Birejik, the farmer, was so pleased when I handed him his coins that he would kiss my hand in gratitude. Old Alkahad alone was grouchy, muttering that I must pay faster. To which I replied that if I were permitted to be well-fed and not worried, that alone would enable me to earn more and pay faster. All the others thanked me and spoke well of my efforts.

Therefore, at the end of one moon, my indebtedness is reduced by almost four pieces of silver, and I possess almost two pieces of silver besides, which are mine to keep. My heart is lighter than it hath been for many years.



Again the moon shines full. I have worked hard during the past weeks, but with poor success. Few camels have I been able to purchase for Nebatur, for other buyers were active in the markets. Only eleven pieces of silver have I earned. 

Nevertheless, my good wife and I have stood firmly by the plan. We have bought no new raiment, and we have eaten little but herbs and simple bread, refusing to touch the one-tenth that belongs to our future.

Again, I paid ourselves one-tenth of the eleven pieces of silver, and we lived upon the seven-tenths. I was surprised when I visited the creditors. Ahmar, who had previously reviled me, commended my payment, even though it was very small. Birejik did the same, offering me a basket of fresh onions. Alkahad flew into a rage when he saw the few coppers I brought, but when I told him he could give back his portion if he did not wish to accept it, he quieted down and kept the coins. The others, as before, were content and wished me well.



Again the moon shines full, and I am greatly rejoiced. I intercepted a fine herd of camels arriving from the north and bought many sound ones for Nebatur. Therefore, my earnings for this moon were forty-two pieces of silver. 

This moon, my wife and myself have bought much-needed sandals and new raiment, for our old clothes were in tatters. Also, we have dined well on roasted goat meat and fowl, celebrating the goodness of the plan.

More than eight pieces of silver we have paid to our creditors, dividing it honorably. Even Alkahad did not protest when I handed him his share, but took it with a nod of his head. Great indeed is the plan, for it leadeth us out of debt and giveth us wealth which is ours to keep.



Three times the moon has been full since I last carved upon this clay. Each time, I paid to myself one-tenth of all I earned. Each time, my good wife and I have lived upon seven-tenths, even though at times when work was slow it was difficult. Each time, I have paid to my creditors two-tenths.

In my leather purse, I now have twenty-one pieces of silver that are mine, untouched and secure. It maketh my head to stand straight upon my shoulders, and it maketh me proud to walk among my friends and neighbors. My wife keepeth our home well, and she is becomingly gowned in clean, simple cloth. We are happy to live together, no longer haunted by the fear of the knock at the door. The plan is of untold value. Hath it not made an honorable man of a desperate debtor?



Tablet No. V

Again the moon shines full, and as I look upon the night sky, I remember that it is long since I carved upon the clay. Twelve moons, in truth, have come and gone. But this day, I will not neglect my record, because upon this day, I have paid the last copper of my debts.

This is the day upon which my good wife and my thankful self celebrate with great feasting, for our determination hath been achieved, and the chains of our past are broken forever.

Many things occurred upon my final visit to my creditors that I shall long remember. Ahmar, who had once spat upon the ground and called me a thief, looked upon me with respect. He begged my forgiveness for his unkind words and said that I was, of all others, the man he most desired to have as a friend and partner in his business.

Old Alkahad, the landlord whom I had feared so much, smiled and patted my shoulder. "you wert once like a piece of soft clay, Tarkad, to be pressed and molded by any hand that touched you," he said. "But now, you are like a piece of solid bronze, capable of holding a sharp edge. If you needest silver or gold at any time to expand your business, come to me, and I will gladly lend to you."

Nor is he the only one who holdeth me in high regard. Many merchants in the market speak deferentially to me now, and my good wife looketh upon me with a light in her eyes that does make a man have confidence in himself.

Yet, I know it is the plan that hath made my success. It hath enabled me to pay all my debts and, at the same time, to jingle both gold and silver in my purse. I do commend it to all who wish to get ahead. For truly, if it will enable a starving, desperate debtor to pay his debts and build a fortune, will it not aid any man to find his independence? Nor am I, myself, finished with it, for I am convinced that if I follow it further, it will make me rich and honored among the wealthiest men of Babylon.
