# Chapter IX. The Army on the March

1. We now discuss army encampment and observing the enemy's signs. Move swiftly across mountains and stay close to valleys.

> Zhang Yu: During the Later Han Dynasty, Ma Yuan defeated the rebel captain Wu-tu Qiang by seizing all advantageous positions commanding water and forage, rather than forcing a direct battle. The rebels, trapped in the hills without provisions, were starved into total surrender. This demonstrates the critical advantage of staying near valleys where resources are plentiful.

2. Camp in high places, facing the sun. Do not climb heights in order to fight. This is the essence of mountain warfare.

> Du Mu: Facing the sun traditionally implies facing south, taking advantage of warmth and visibility.
> Chen Hao: Facing the sun can also mean facing east, greeting the morning light.

3. After crossing a river, immediately distance yourself from it.

> Cao Cao: Moving away tempts the enemy to cross the river after you, making them vulnerable.
> Zhang Yu: Distancing yourself ensures your army has ample space to maneuver without being backed against the water.

4. When an invading force crosses a river to attack, never meet them in mid-stream. Wait until half of their army has crossed, then strike.

> Li Quan: Han Xin famously defeated Long Ju at the Wei River using this tactic. Han Xin secretly built a sandbag dam upstream. He led half his army across to attack, feigned defeat, and retreated. When Long Ju eagerly pursued him into the riverbed, Han Xin broke the dam. The floodwaters cut Long Ju's army in half, allowing Han Xin to annihilate the stranded vanguard. 

5. If you are eager for battle, do not meet the invader near a river they must cross. Let them commit to the crossing first.

6. Moor your boats upstream of the enemy, facing the sun. Never move upstream to attack. This is the essence of river warfare.

> Zhang Yu: Whether deploying troops on the bank or anchoring boats in the stream, you must hold the higher ground (upstream) and face the sun for better visibility.
> Du Mu: Because water flows downhill, camping downstream risks the enemy opening sluice gates to sweep your forces away in a flood. Chancellor Zhuge Liang noted that advancing against the current is a severe tactical disadvantage.

7. When crossing salt-marshes, your only goal is to traverse them swiftly and without delay.

8. If forced to fight in a salt-marsh, ensure you have water and grass nearby, and position your back against a clump of trees. This is the essence of marsh warfare.

> Li Quan: Trees indicate firmer ground, reducing the risk of sinking into treacherous mud.
> Du Mu: The trees serve as a natural barrier to protect your rear from flanking maneuvers.

9. In dry, flat country, take an easily accessible position with rising ground to your right and rear. Keep the danger in front and safety behind you. This is the essence of flatland warfare.

> Du Mu: The strategist Tai Kung advised that an army should ideally have a stream or marsh on its left and a hill or mound on its right to anchor its flanks.

10. These four principles of warfare—for mountains, rivers, marshes, and plains—enabled the Yellow Emperor to conquer four rival sovereigns.

> Mei Yao-chen: Some scholars question if the Yellow Emperor actually conquered four emperors.
> Cao Cao: The Yellow Emperor instituted a feudal system of vassal princes, who originally styled themselves as independent "Emperors" before their defeat.
> Li Quan: The foundational art of war originated during the Yellow Emperor's reign, passed down by his chief minister, Feng Hou.

11. All armies prefer high ground over low, and sunlit places over dark ones.

> Mei Yao-chen: High ground is healthier and offers a commanding tactical view. Low ground is damp, disease-prone, and disadvantageous for defense.

12. If you care for your men and camp on hard, dry ground, the army will be free from disease. A healthy army spells victory.

> Cao Cao: Always secure fresh water and open pastures to graze your pack animals.
> Zhang Yu: A dry, temperate climate prevents the outbreak of camp illnesses like dysentery.

13. When occupying a hill or bank, secure the sunny side with the slope anchoring your right rear. This protects your soldiers while weaponizing the natural terrain.

14. When upstream rains cause a river to swell and froth, wait for the waters to subside before attempting to ford.

15. Avoid treacherous terrain at all costs: precipitous cliffs with raging torrents, deep natural hollows, confined gorges, tangled thickets, quagmires, and narrow crevasses. Flee them swiftly.

> Mei Yao-chen: A crevasse represents a narrow, perilous path between towering, overhanging cliffs.
> Tu Mu: Tangled terrain is broken ground covered with rocks, dense woods, and hidden pitfalls.
> Jia Lin & Zhang Yu: These are deadly choke points and defiles where an army cannot maneuver.

16. While you avoid such death-traps, maneuver the enemy into them. Face the hazards yourself, but force the enemy to back into them.

17. Carefully scour any nearby hills, ponds with aquatic grass, reedy hollows, or dense undergrowth. These are prime hiding spots for ambushes and enemy spies.

> Zhang Yu: Always remain vigilant against infiltrators who hide in thick cover to observe troop movements and eavesdrop on command instructions.

18. If the enemy is close but remains quiet, they are relying on the natural strength of their defensive position.

19. If the enemy keeps their distance but tries to provoke a battle, they are desperate for you to advance into a trap.

> Du Mu: If they genuinely wanted an open fight, they would march directly up to us. By staying away, they hope to lure us out of our secure position.

20. If the enemy's camp is suspiciously easy to access, they are offering a bait.

21. Movement in a forest indicates an advancing enemy. The sudden appearance of screens or blinds in thick grass means the enemy is trying to make you suspicious.

> Cao Cao: Moving trees mean they are actively felling timber to clear a marching path.
> Zhang Yu: Scouts observing from high ground can spot the canopy shaking as troops cut their way through.
> Du Yu: If the enemy builds obvious screens in the grass, they have likely already fled. The screens are a psychological trick to make you suspect an ambush and delay your pursuit.

22. Birds suddenly taking flight indicate an ambush below. Startled beasts signify a sudden, impending attack.

> Zhang Yu: When birds flying in a straight line suddenly shoot straight up into the air, they have been spooked by soldiers hiding in the brush beneath them.

23. A high, narrow column of dust signals advancing chariots. Low, widespread dust signifies marching infantry. Dust branching in multiple directions means foraging parties are collecting firewood. Small, shifting clouds of dust mean the army is pitching camp.

> Zhang Yu: Vanguard scouts constantly monitor dust clouds. Light cavalry maneuvering to survey camp defenses will kick up small, localized dust clouds as they secure the perimeter.

24. Humble rhetoric coupled with increased preparations means the enemy is about to attack. Violent threats and aggressive posturing mean the enemy is preparing to retreat.

> Du Mu: Feigned weakness breeds contempt and carelessness in your ranks, setting you up for a surprise strike.
> Zhang Yu: General Tian Dan used this brilliantly against the Yan army. He fed them false rumors, making them arrogant, then feigned surrender to lull them to sleep. Once their guard was down, he routed them with a sudden ambush using oxen with burning torches tied to their tails.

25. When light chariots deploy first to the flanks, the enemy is forming their battle lines.

26. Peace proposals made without a sworn covenant or hostages indicate a hidden plot.

> Li Quan: Legitimate treaties are always secured by oaths and high-ranking hostages.
> Wang Xi & Zhang Yu: An unprompted peace offer on a frivolous pretext is a stalling tactic or a trap.

27. When enemy troops frantically run about falling into rank, the critical moment of their attack has arrived.

28. When some enemy units advance while others retreat, they are dangling a lure to break your formation.

29. When soldiers stand leaning heavily on their spears, they are weak from starvation.

30. If men sent to fetch water drink it before returning, the entire army is suffering from severe thirst.

> Du Mu: The desperation of a single water-bearer reveals the exhaustion of the whole camp.

31. If the enemy sees a clear advantage but makes no move to take it, their soldiers are completely exhausted.

32. If birds gather unbothered on a campsite, it has been abandoned. Shouting and clamor at night betrays fear and nervousness in the enemy ranks.

> Chen Hao: Flocking birds are a reliable sign to confirm an enemy's secret retreat.

33. Unrest in the camp means the general's authority is weak. Shifting banners indicate sedition. Angry officers mean the soldiers are pushed to the breaking point.

> Du Mu: If officers openly resent their commander, it means he has overworked the army to the point of mutiny.

34. When an army feeds grain to its horses, slaughters its beasts of burden for meat, and refuses to hang their cooking pots—showing they will not return to their tents—they are a desperate force resolved to fight to the death.

35. Soldiers whispering in small groups or speaking in hushed tones indicate deep-seated mutiny and disaffection.

36. Too many rewards show the enemy general is out of options; too many punishments show an army in dire distress.

> Du Mu: A hard-pressed general fears mutiny, so he desperately hands out lavish rewards to buy loyalty, or excessive punishments to maintain control by terror.

37. A commander who begins with blustering tyranny but ends up terrified of his own men's numbers is a fool of the highest order.

> Cao Cao, Du Yu, Jia Lin, Mei Yao-chen, Wang Xi: A general who bullies his troops without earning their respect will quickly find himself living in terror of a mutiny.

38. Envoys arriving with overly complimentary words mean the enemy desperately wants a truce.

> Du Mu: Sending hostages and flattering envoys indicates their strength is shattered, and they need breathing room.

39. If enemy troops march up aggressively but stand facing you for a long time without fighting or leaving, the situation demands extreme vigilance.

> Cao Cao: This standoff is almost certainly a diversion to pin you down while they execute a flank attack or spring an ambush.

40. In war, sheer numbers are not everything. If you merely match the enemy in size, direct assault may fail, but you can still win by concentrating your forces, watching the enemy closely, and gathering reinforcements.

> Li Quan: Ultimately, the side that effectively fields the most strength at the decisive point wins.
> Zhang Yu: Even if numbers are even, you can tip the balance by mobilizing camp followers or waiting for a tactical opening. However, relying on foreign mercenaries is a perilous gamble.

41. A commander who lacks foresight and underestimates his opponent will inevitably be captured.

> Chen Hao: If tiny scorpions and bees carry lethal poison, imagine the danger of a hostile state! Never treat even a weak enemy with contempt.

42. If you punish soldiers before securing their loyalty, they will rebel. If they are loyal but you fail to enforce discipline, they will become useless.

43. Therefore, lead your soldiers with humanity, but bind them with iron discipline. This is the certain road to victory.

44. If orders are consistently enforced during training, the army will be disciplined. If not, discipline will crumble in battle.

45. When a general trusts his men but absolutely insists on obedience, the resulting discipline benefits both commander and soldier.

> Du Mu: In peacetime, show your men kindness but demand respect. When battle comes, they will obey without hesitation because they trust you.
> Zhang Yu: Mutual trust is key. The art of command is knowing when to act. Do not obsess over petty mistakes or show indecision. Constant wavering and nervous fussing will destroy an army's confidence faster than the enemy.
