Chapter 5: Energy and Force (Shih)

1. Sun Tzu said: Commanding a large army is no different in principle from commanding a few men; it is merely a matter of organization and dividing them into groups.

> Du Mu: This brings to mind Han Hsin’s famous reply to the first Han Emperor. The Emperor asked, "How large an army do you think I could lead?" Han Hsin replied, "Not more than 100,000 men, Your Majesty." The Emperor then asked, "And you?" Han Hsin smiled and said, "The more, the better."

2. Directing a vast force in battle is exactly like directing a small one; it is simply a matter of establishing clear signs and signals.

3. To ensure that your entire army can withstand the brunt of the enemy’s attack and remain unshaken, you must combine direct (zheng) and indirect (qi) maneuvers.

> Li Quan: Facing the enemy head-on is a direct maneuver; launching lateral diversions and flanking attacks is indirect.
> 
> Jia Lin: In the presence of the enemy, deploy your troops in a conventional, standard formation (direct), but employ unexpected, abnormal tactics (indirect) to secure victory.
> 
> Mei Yao-chen: Direct maneuvers are passive, while indirect maneuvers are active. Passivity means waiting for an opportunity; activity seizes the victory itself.
> 
> He Shi: You must confuse the enemy, making them view a straightforward attack as a secret ambush, and vice versa. Thus, the direct can become the indirect, and the indirect can become the direct. Han Hsin once marched ostensibly toward Lin-jin, but suddenly threw a large force across the Yellow River using wooden tubs, utterly shocking his opponent. The obvious march was the direct maneuver; the river crossing was the indirect surprise.
> 
> Zhang Yu: Military writers often debate the definitions of direct and indirect. What is crucial to grasp is that the two are mutually interchangeable, flowing into each other like a continuous circle. The ultimate secret lies in confusing the enemy so deeply that they cannot fathom your true intentions.

4. The impact of your army should be like a grindstone crashing into an egg. This is achieved by mastering the science of weak points and strong points—striking the enemy's vulnerabilities with your overwhelming strength.

5. In all fighting, the direct method may be used to join the battle, but indirect methods are required to secure the victory.

> Zhang Yu: Steadily develop indirect tactics, either by pounding the enemy’s flanks or falling unexpectedly upon their rear.

6. When efficiently applied, indirect tactics are as inexhaustible as Heaven and Earth, and as unending as the flow of rivers and streams. Like the sun and moon, they set only to rise again; like the four seasons, they pass away only to return once more.

> Du Yu and Zhang Yu: This illustrates the infinite permutations of combining direct and indirect tactics.

7. There are only five musical notes, yet their combinations produce more melodies than can ever be heard.

8. There are only five primary colors, yet their combinations produce more hues than can ever be seen.

9. There are only five cardinal tastes, yet their combinations yield more flavors than can ever be tasted.

10. In battle, there are only two methods of attack: the direct and the indirect. Yet these two in combination give rise to an endless series of maneuvers.

11. The direct and the indirect lead into each other endlessly. It is like moving in a circle—you never reach an end. Who could possibly exhaust the possibilities of their combinations?

12. The onset of troops should be like the rush of a torrent, so powerful that it rolls boulders along its course.

13. The quality of a commander's decision is like the well-timed swoop of a falcon, enabling it to strike and destroy its prey.

> Du Mu: This metaphor highlights the instinct of self-restraint. A falcon waits patiently and judges the exact right moment to strike. Similarly, troops must be disciplined enough to hold their fire until the precise instant it will be most devastating.

14. Therefore, the expert commander will be terrifying in his initial strike, and razor-sharp in his decision-making.

> Wang Xi: This is the essence of seizing the psychological moment in warfare.

15. Energy (momentum) may be likened to the drawing back of a crossbow; decision, to the pulling of the trigger.

16. Amid the turmoil and tumult of battle, your formations may appear chaotic, yet there is no real disorder. Your array may seem topsy-turvy, without head or tail, yet it will be completely proof against defeat.

> Mei Yao-chen: When an army is perfectly subdivided with clear signals, the constant splitting, joining, dispersing, and collecting during a battle may look like chaos to an observer. Yet, because every move is orchestrated, a rout is out of the question.

17. To simulate disorder, you must have perfect discipline; to simulate fear, you must possess extreme courage; to simulate weakness, you must have overwhelming strength.

> Cao Cao: These deceptions serve to break up the enemy's formation and conceal your true condition.
> 
> Du Mu: If you wish to feign confusion to lure the enemy, your troops must first be perfectly disciplined so they don't actually rout. To feign fear, your troops must be courageous enough to hold their nerve during a fake retreat. To parade weakness and make the enemy overconfident, you must secretly possess overwhelming strength.

18. Hiding order beneath the cloak of disorder is simply a matter of careful organization; concealing courage under a show of fear requires immense latent energy; masking strength with weakness is achieved through clever tactical positioning.

> Du Mu: When we have a clear advantage but deliberately make no move, the enemy will naturally believe we are afraid.
> 
> Zhang Yu: The first Han Emperor, wishing to crush the Xiongnu, sent spies to assess them. The Xiongnu hid all their strong men and healthy horses, leaving only the infirm and emaciated visible. The spies urged an attack. Only Lou Jing warned: "When two nations war, they usually display their strength. Seeing only weakness is a ruse; it is a trap." The Emperor ignored this, attacked, and found his army disastrously surrounded.

19. Thus, a commander skilled at keeping the enemy on the move uses deceitful appearances, forcing the enemy to act according to his will.

> Cao Cao: Make a display of weakness and lack of supplies to tempt them.
> 
> Du Mu: If you are stronger, feign weakness to lure the enemy in. If you are weaker, feign strength to keep them at bay. Consider the tactician Sun Bin: knowing his army had a reputation for cowardice, he ordered his men to light 100,000 campfires the first night, 50,000 the next, and 20,000 the third. The pursuing enemy general, Pang Juan, thought the army was deserting in droves and recklessly rushed ahead. Sun Bin set an ambush at a narrow pass, writing on a stripped tree: "Under this tree shall Pang Juan die." When Pang Juan lit a torch to read the words, Sun Bin's archers unleashed a volley, destroying the enemy.

20. He sacrifices a small advantage so that the enemy may snatch at it. By holding out baits, he keeps the enemy marching; then, with a body of picked men, he lies in wait to ambush them.

> Li Jing: He uses a small bait, but lies in wait with the main body of his elite troops.

21. The clever combatant relies on the effect of combined energy and momentum, and does not demand too much from individuals.

> Du Mu: A wise commander considers the power of his army as a whole. Only then does he assign individuals based on their specific talents. He never demands perfection from those unsuited for a task.

22. Because he knows how to utilize combined energy, his fighting men become like rolling logs or stones. It is the nature of a log or stone to remain motionless on flat ground, but to roll when on a slope. If it is square, it comes to a halt; if it is round, it rolls down rapidly.

> Cao Cao: This is the use of natural, inherent power—setting up conditions where momentum does the work for you.

23. Thus, the energy developed by expert commanders is like the unstoppable momentum of a round boulder rolling down a mountain thousands of feet high. This is the essence of energy and strategic advantage.

> Du Mu: The chief lesson of this chapter is the paramount importance of rapid execution, sudden rushes, and leveraging momentum. With these, massive results can be achieved even with small forces.
