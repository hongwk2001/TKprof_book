# Chapter 10: Terrain

**1. Sun Tzu said: We may distinguish six types of terrain:** 
1. Accessible ground 
2. Entangling ground 
3. Temporizing ground 
4. Narrow passes 
5. Precipitous heights 
6. Positions at a great distance from the enemy

> **Mei Yao-ch'en on Accessible Ground:** "This is ground plentifully provided with roads and means of communication."
> 
> **Mei Yao-ch'en on Entangling Ground:** "This is 'net-like' country. The metaphor implies a landscape filled with natural snares—swamps, dense thickets, or intricate terrain—where venturing forward is easy, but retreating is like trying to untangle yourself from a net."

**2. Accessible Ground:** Ground that can be freely traversed by both sides is called accessible.

**3. Strategy for Accessible Ground:** In accessible ground, be the first to occupy high and sunny positions, and carefully guard your supply lines. Then you will be able to fight with an advantage.
> **Du Yu:** "Do not allow the enemy to cut your communications."

**4. Entangling Ground:** Ground that can be abandoned but is hard to re-occupy is called entangling.

**5. Strategy for Entangling Ground:** In entangling ground, if the enemy is unprepared, you may sally forth and defeat them. But if the enemy is prepared for your coming, and you fail to defeat them, returning will be impossible, and disaster will follow.

**6. Temporizing Ground:** When the position offers no advantage to either side for making the first move, it is called temporizing ground.
> **Du Mu:** "Each side finds it inconvenient to move, and the situation remains at a deadlock."

**7. Strategy for Temporizing Ground:** In temporizing ground, even if the enemy offers an attractive bait, do not advance. Instead, retreat and entice the enemy to move; when part of their army has come out, you may deliver your attack with a decisive advantage.
> **Du Yu:** "Entice them by turning your back and pretending to flee."

**8. Narrow Passes:** If you can occupy them first, garrison them strongly and await the enemy.
> **Du Yu:** "The initiative will lie with us, and by making sudden and unexpected attacks we shall have the enemy at our mercy."

**9. Strategy for Narrow Passes:** If the enemy occupies a pass before you, do not follow them if it is fully garrisoned. You may follow only if it is weakly garrisoned.

**10. Precipitous Heights:** If you arrive before your adversary, occupy the high and sunny spots, and wait for them to come up.
> **Cao Cao:** "The particular advantage of securing heights and defiles is that your actions cannot then be dictated by the enemy."
>
> **Zhang Yu:** "P'ei Hsing-chien was sent on an expedition against the Turkic tribes. At night he pitched his camp as usual, completely fortified by a wall and ditch, when suddenly he gave orders to shift the army's quarters to a nearby hill. His officers were highly displeased, protesting the extra fatigue. P'ei Hsing-chien paid no heed and moved the camp quickly. That night, a terrific storm flooded their former encampment to a depth of over twelve feet. The recalcitrant officers were amazed and admitted their mistake. 'How did you know?' they asked. He replied: 'From now on, obey orders without asking unnecessary questions.' High and sunny places are advantageous not only for fighting, but because they are immune from disastrous floods."

**11. Strategy for Precipitous Heights:** If the enemy has occupied heights before you, do not follow them, but retreat and try to entice them away.

**12. Distant Positions:** If you are situated at a great distance from the enemy, and the strength of the two armies is equal, it is not easy to provoke a battle, and fighting will be to your disadvantage.
> **Du Yu:** "By the time we engage, we would be exhausted while our adversary remains fresh and keen."

**13. Conclusion on Terrain:** These six are the principles connected with terrain. The general who has attained a responsible post must carefully study them.

**14. Six Calamities of an Army:** An army is exposed to six calamities, not arising from natural causes, but from faults for which the general is responsible. These are: 
1. Flight 
2. Insubordination 
3. Collapse 
4. Ruin 
5. Disorganization 
6. Rout

**15. 1. Flight:** Other conditions being equal, if one force is hurled against another ten times its size, the result will be the flight of the former.

**16. 2. Insubordination and 3. Collapse:** When the common soldiers are too strong and their officers too weak, the result is insubordination.
> **Du Mu:** "T'ien Pu was sent to Wei in 821 A.D. with orders to lead an army. His soldiers treated him with utmost contempt, riding about the camp on donkeys, thousands at a time. T'ien Pu was powerless to stop it. After some months, he attempted to engage the enemy, but his troops turned tail and dispersed. He then committed suicide by cutting his throat."

When the officers are too strong and the common soldiers too weak, the result is collapse.
> **Cao Cao:** "The officers are energetic and want to press on, but the common soldiers are feeble and suddenly collapse under the pressure."

**17. 4. Ruin:** When the higher officers are angry and insubordinate, and on meeting the enemy give battle on their own account out of resentment, before the commander-in-chief can determine if he is in a position to fight, the result is ruin.
> **Wang Xi:** "The general is angry without cause, and does not appreciate the ability of his subordinate officers; thus he arouses fierce resentment and brings an avalanche of ruin upon his head."

**18. 5. Disorganization:** When the general is weak and without authority; when his orders are not clear and distinct; when there are no fixed duties assigned to officers and men, and the ranks are formed in a slovenly, haphazard manner, the result is utter disorganization.
> **Wei Liao Tzu:** "If the commander gives his orders with decision, the soldiers will not wait to hear them twice; if his moves are made without vacillation, the soldiers will not be in two minds about doing their duty."
>
> **Wu Qi (Wu Tzu):** "The most fatal defect in a military leader is diffidence; the worst calamities that befall an army arise from hesitation."
>
> **Du Mu:** "This happens when neither officers nor men have any regular routine."

**19. 6. Rout:** When a general, unable to estimate the enemy's strength, allows an inferior force to engage a larger one, or hurls a weak detachment against a powerful one, and neglects to place picked soldiers in the front rank, the result must be a rout.
> **Zhang Yu:** "Whenever there is fighting to be done, the keenest spirits should be appointed to serve in the front ranks, both to strengthen the resolution of our own men and to demoralize the enemy."

**20. Conclusion on Calamities:** These are six ways of courting defeat, which must be carefully noted by the general who has attained a responsible post.

**21. The Test of a Great General:** The natural formation of the country is the soldier's best ally; but the power of estimating the adversary, controlling the forces of victory, and shrewdly calculating difficulties, dangers, and distances, constitutes the test of a great general.
> **Chen Hao:** "The advantages of weather and season are not equal to those connected with the strategic advantages of the ground."

**22. Knowledge is Victory:** He who knows these things, and in fighting puts his knowledge into practice, will win his battles. He who knows them not, nor practices them, will surely be defeated.

**23. Independent Command:** If fighting is sure to result in victory, then you must fight, even though the ruler forbids it; if fighting will not result in victory, then you must not fight, even at the ruler's bidding.
> **Huang Shi-gong:** "The responsibility of setting an army in motion must devolve on the general alone; if advance and retreat are controlled from the Palace, brilliant results will hardly be achieved. Hence the god-like ruler and the enlightened monarch are content to play a humble part in furthering their country's cause."
>
> **Zhang Yu:** "Decrees from the Son of Heaven do not penetrate the walls of a camp. A general in the field must be free to make independent decisions based on real-time realities."

**24. The Jewel of the Kingdom:** The general who advances without coveting fame and retreats without fearing disgrace, whose only thought is to protect his country and do good service for his sovereign, is the jewel of the kingdom.
> **He Shi:** "Such a man, even if he had to suffer unjust punishment from a ruler who misunderstood his retreat, would not regret his conduct."

**25. Soldiers as Family:** Regard your soldiers as your children, and they will follow you into the deepest valleys; look on them as your own beloved sons, and they will stand by you even unto death.
> **Du Mu:** "Wu Qi wore the same clothes and ate the same food as the meanest of his soldiers, refused to have either a horse to ride or a mat to sleep on, carried his own surplus rations, and shared every hardship. When a soldier suffered from an abscess, Wu Qi himself sucked out the virus. The soldier's mother wailed, 'Your son is only a common soldier, yet the commander-in-chief himself sucked the poison from his sore.' A woman replied, 'Years ago, Lord Wu did the same for my husband, who never left him and died fighting. Now he has done the same for your son, and he too will fall fighting.'"
>
> **Li Quan:** "The Viscount of Chu invaded the state of Xiao during winter. The Duke of Shen said, 'Many soldiers are suffering from the cold.' So the Viscount made a round of the army, comforting the men; straightway they felt as if clothed in garments lined with floss silk."

**26. Discipline and Indulgence:** If, however, you are indulgent but unable to make your authority felt; kind-hearted but unable to enforce your commands; and incapable, moreover, of quelling disorder: then your soldiers must be likened to spoilt children; they are useless for any practical purpose.
> **Li Jing:** "If you can make your soldiers afraid of you, they will not be afraid of the enemy. Discipline must override natural affection."
>
> **Du Mu:** "In 219 A.D., Lu Meng ordered his army not to molest inhabitants. An officer appropriated a bamboo hat to wear over his helmet against the rain. Lu Meng ordered his summary execution, crying as he did so. This filled the army with awe, and thereafter even articles dropped in the highway were not picked up."

**27. Halfway to Victory (The Self):** If we know that our own men are in a condition to attack, but are unaware that the enemy is not open to attack, we have gone only halfway towards victory.
> **Cao Cao:** "The issue in this case is uncertain. Confidence in your own troops is not enough without recognizing the enemy's vulnerabilities."

**28. Halfway to Victory (The Enemy):** If we know that the enemy is open to attack, but are unaware that our own men are not in a condition to attack, we have gone only halfway towards victory.

**29. Halfway to Victory (The Terrain):** If we know that the enemy is open to attack, and also know that our men are in a condition to attack, but are unaware that the nature of the ground makes fighting impracticable, we have still gone only halfway towards victory.

**30. The Prepared Soldier:** Hence the experienced soldier, once in motion, is never bewildered; once he has broken camp, he is never at a loss.
> **Du Mu:** "He has taken his measures so thoroughly as to ensure victory beforehand."
>
> **Zhang Yu:** "He does not move recklessly, so that when he does move, he makes no mistakes."

**31. Know Heaven and Earth:** Hence the saying: If you know the enemy and know yourself, your victory will not stand in doubt; if you know Heaven and know Earth, you may make your victory complete.
> **Li Quan:** "Given a knowledge of three things—the affairs of men, the seasons of heaven, and the natural advantages of earth—victory will invariably crown your battles."
