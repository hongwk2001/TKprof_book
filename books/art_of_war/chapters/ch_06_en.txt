# Chapter VI. Weak Points and Strong

> **Zhang Yu:**  
> "Chapter IV, on Tactical Dispositions, treated the offensive and the defensive; Chapter V, on Energy, dealt with direct and indirect methods. The good general first masters the theory of attack and defense, then turns his attention to direct and indirect methods. He studies the art of varying and combining these before proceeding to weak and strong points. For the use of direct or indirect methods arises out of attack and defense, and the perception of weak and strong points depends on those methods. Hence, this chapter logically follows the chapter on Energy."

**1.** Sun Tzu said: Whoever arrives first at the battlefield and awaits the enemy will be fresh for the fight; whoever arrives second and must rush into battle will be exhausted.

**2.** Therefore, the skilled commander imposes his will on the enemy, but does not allow the enemy's will to be imposed on him.

**3.** By offering tactical advantages, he entices the enemy to approach of their own accord; or, by inflicting damage, he makes it impossible for the enemy to draw near.

**4.** If the enemy is resting, he can harass them; if they are well-supplied, he can starve them out; if they are quietly encamped, he can force them to move.

**5.** Appear at points the enemy must rush to defend; march swiftly to places where you are not expected.

**6.** An army may march great distances without distress if it moves through territory where the enemy is absent.

> **Cao Cao:**  
> *"Emerge from the void, strike at vulnerable points, shun places that are defended, attack in unexpected quarters."*  
>   
> **Explanation:** The "void" refers to undefended or unanticipated avenues of approach. Cao Cao emphasizes that attacking empty space—where the enemy is not—guarantees freedom of movement and shock value.

**7.** You can be certain of succeeding in your attacks if you only attack places that are undefended. You can ensure the safety of your defense if you only hold positions that cannot be attacked.

> **Wang Xi:**  
> *"Undefended places are weak points; that is to say, where the general is lacking in capacity, or the soldiers in spirit; where the walls are not strong enough, or the precautions not strict enough; where relief comes too late, or provisions are too scanty, or the defenders are at variance amongst themselves."*
> 
> **Du Mu, Chen Hao, and Mei Yao-chen:**  
> *"In order to make your defense quite safe, you must defend even those places that are not likely to be attacked."*
> 
> **Du Mu:**  
> *"How much more, then, those that will be attacked."*
> 
> **Zhang Yu:**  
> *"He who is skilled in attack flashes forth from the topmost heights of heaven, making it impossible for the enemy to guard against him. This being so, the places that I shall attack are precisely those that the enemy cannot defend... He who is skilled in defense hides in the most secret recesses of the earth, making it impossible for the enemy to estimate his whereabouts. This being so, the places that I shall hold are precisely those that the enemy cannot attack."*  
>   
> **Explanation:** The metaphors of "heights of heaven" and "recesses of the earth" represent the extremes of mobility and concealment. An attacker strikes like lightning from above (unpredictable and overwhelming), while a defender blends seamlessly into the terrain (invisible and impregnable).

**8.** Hence, a general is skilled in attack whose opponent does not know what to defend; and he is skilled in defense whose opponent does not know what to attack.

**9.** O divine art of subtlety and secrecy! Through you we learn to be invisible, through you inaudible; and hence we hold the enemy's fate in our hands.

**10.** You may advance and be absolutely irresistible if you strike at the enemy's weak points; you may retreat and be safe from pursuit if your movements are swifter than those of the enemy.

**11.** If we wish to fight, the enemy can be forced into an engagement even if they are sheltered behind a high rampart and a deep ditch. All we need to do is attack some other place that they will be obligated to relieve.

> **Du Mu:**  
> *"If the enemy is the invading party, we can cut his line of communications and occupy the roads by which he will have to return; if we are the invaders, we may direct our attack against the sovereign himself."*

**12.** If we do not wish to fight, we can prevent the enemy from engaging us even if our encampment lines are merely traced out on the ground. All we need to do is throw something odd and unaccountable in their way.

> **Jia Lin:**  
> *"Even though we have constructed neither wall nor ditch."*
> 
> **Li Quan:**  
> *"We puzzle him by strange and unusual dispositions."*
> 
> **Du Mu:**  
> *"When Zhuge Liang was occupying Yangping and about to be attacked by Sima Yi, he suddenly struck his colors, stopped the beating of the drums, and flung open the city gates, showing only a few men engaged in sweeping and sprinkling the ground. This unexpected proceeding had the intended effect; for Sima Yi, suspecting an ambush, actually drew off his army and retreated."*  
>   
> **Explanation:** This is a classic example of the "Empty Fort Strategy." By projecting extreme vulnerability and acting completely contrary to expected defensive behavior, Zhuge Liang created overwhelming psychological doubt, weaponizing the enemy commander's own caution against him.

**13.** By discovering the enemy's dispositions while remaining invisible ourselves, we can keep our forces concentrated, while the enemy's must be divided.

> **Zhang Yu and Mei Yao-chen:**  
> *"If the enemy's dispositions are visible, we can make for him in one body; whereas, our own dispositions being kept secret, the enemy will be obliged to divide his forces in order to guard against attack from every quarter."*

**14.** We can form a single united body, while the enemy must split up into fractions. Hence, there will be a whole pitted against separate parts of a whole, meaning we shall be many against the enemy's few.

**15.** And if we are thus able to attack an inferior force with a superior one, our opponents will be in dire straits.

**16.** The spot where we intend to fight must not be made known; for then the enemy will have to prepare against possible attacks at several different points; and with their forces distributed in many directions, the numbers we face at any given point will be proportionately few.

**17.** For should the enemy strengthen their vanguard, they will weaken their rear; should they strengthen their rear, they will weaken their vanguard; should they strengthen their left, they will weaken their right; should they strengthen their right, they will weaken their left. If they send reinforcements everywhere, they will everywhere be weak.

**18.** Numerical weakness comes from having to prepare against possible attacks; numerical strength comes from compelling our adversary to make these preparations against us.

**19.** Knowing the place and the time of the coming battle, we may concentrate from the greatest distances in order to fight.

**20.** But if neither time nor place is known, then the left wing will be unable to succor the right, the right equally unable to succor the left, the vanguard unable to relieve the rear, or the rear to support the vanguard. How much more so if the furthest portions of the army are anything under a hundred *li* apart, and even the nearest are separated by several *li*!

> **Zhang Yu:**  
> *"If we do not know the place where our opponents mean to concentrate or the day on which they will join battle, our unity will be forfeited through our preparations for defense, and the positions we hold will be insecure. Suddenly happening upon a powerful foe, we shall be brought to battle in a flurried condition, and no mutual support will be possible between wings, vanguard or rear, especially if there is any great distance between the foremost and hindmost divisions of the army."*

**21.** Though according to my estimate the soldiers of Yueh exceed our own in number, that shall advantage them nothing in the matter of victory. I say then that victory can be achieved.

> **Zhang Yu:**  
> *"In the chapter on Tactical Dispositions it is said, 'One may know how to conquer without being able to do it,' whereas here we have the statement that 'victory can be achieved.' The explanation is, that in the former chapter, where the offensive and defensive are under discussion, it is said that if the enemy is fully prepared, one cannot make certain of beating him. But the present passage refers particularly to the soldiers of Yueh who, according to Sun Tzu's calculations, will be kept in ignorance of the time and place of the impending struggle. That is why he says here that victory can be achieved."*

**22.** Though the enemy may be stronger in numbers, we can prevent them from fighting. Scheme so as to discover their plans and the likelihood of their success.

> **Jia Lin:**  
> *"Know beforehand all plans conducive to our success and to the enemy's failure."*

**23.** Rouse the enemy, and learn the principle of their activity or inactivity. Force them to reveal themselves, so as to find out their vulnerable spots.

> **Zhang Yu:**  
> *"By noting the joy or anger shown by the enemy on being thus disturbed, we shall be able to conclude whether his policy is to lie low or the reverse. For instance, Zhuge Liang sent the scornful present of a woman's head-dress to Sima Yi, in order to goad him out of his Fabian tactics."*  
>   
> **Explanation:** Zhang Yu highlights psychological warfare. By provoking an emotional response (like insulting a cautious general's masculinity to force him to attack), a commander forces the enemy to abandon their deliberate strategy and react impulsively, thereby exposing vulnerabilities.

**24.** Carefully compare the opposing army with your own, so that you may know where strength is superabundant and where it is deficient.

**25.** In making tactical dispositions, the highest pitch you can attain is to conceal them; conceal your dispositions, and you will be safe from the prying of the subtlest spies, from the machinations of the wisest brains.

> **Du Mu:**  
> *"Though the enemy may have clever and capable officers, they will not be able to lay any plans against us."*

**26.** How victory may be produced for them out of the enemy's own tactics—that is what the multitude cannot comprehend.

**27.** All men can see the tactics whereby I conquer, but what none can see is the strategy out of which victory is evolved.

**28.** Do not repeat the tactics which have gained you one victory, but let your methods be regulated by the infinite variety of circumstances.

> **Wang Xi:**  
> *"There is but one root-principle underlying victory, but the tactics which lead up to it are infinite in number."*

**29.** Military tactics are like unto water; for water in its natural course runs away from high places and hastens downwards.

**30.** So in war, the way is to avoid what is strong and to strike at what is weak.

**31.** Water shapes its course according to the nature of the ground over which it flows; the soldier works out their victory in relation to the foe whom they are facing.

**32.** Therefore, just as water retains no constant shape, so in warfare there are no constant conditions.

**33.** He who can modify his tactics in relation to his opponent and thereby succeed in winning, may be called a heaven-born captain.

**34.** The five elements (water, fire, wood, metal, earth) are not always equally predominant; the four seasons make way for each other in turn. There are short days and long; the moon has its periods of waning and waxing.

> **Wang Xi:**  
> *"They predominate alternately."*  
>   
> **Explanation:** This natural metaphor underscores the essence of Sun Tzu's philosophy: adaptation. Just as nature is in a constant state of flux, battlefield conditions are always changing. The master strategist does not rely on rigid doctrines but remains fluid, adapting to the enemy's actions as naturally as water flows downhill or seasons change.
