# Chapter 3: Attack by Stratagem

1. Sun Tzu said: In the practical art of war, the highest excellence is to capture the enemy's country whole and intact; shattering and destroying it is an inferior strategy. Likewise, it is always better to capture an entire army, regiment, detachment, or company than to destroy them.
> Sima Fa: *(Context on Military Organization)* An army corps consisted nominally of 12,500 men.
> Cao Cao: A regiment contained 500 men, a detachment anywhere between 100 and 500, and a company from 5 to 100 men.
> Zhang Yu: A detachment is exactly 100 men, and a company is exactly 5 men. *(Note: These commentators are clarifying the scale of units to emphasize that preservation is superior to destruction at every level of the military hierarchy).*

2. Therefore, to fight and conquer in all your battles is not supreme excellence. Supreme excellence consists in breaking the enemy's resistance without fighting.

3. Thus, the highest form of generalship is to thwart the enemy's plans before they are executed.
> He Shi: When the enemy has formulated a plan of attack against us, we must anticipate him by delivering our own preemptive strike.

The next best strategy is to prevent the junction of the enemy's forces, isolating them.

The third best is to attack the enemy's army in the open field.

The worst policy of all is to besiege walled cities.

4. The rule is not to besiege walled cities if it can possibly be avoided. 

The preparation of mantlets, movable shelters, and various implements of war will take up three whole months.
> Cao Cao: Mantlets are large defensive shields.
> Li Quan: They protect the heads of troops assaulting the city walls at close quarters.
> Du Mu: Mantlets are wheeled vehicles used in repelling attacks. The movable shelters are specialized armored transports, often called "wooden donkeys."
> Chen Hao: I disagree that mantlets are wheeled vehicles; they are simply heavy shields. *(Note: These commentators are debating specific ancient siege technologies, highlighting the immense material and logistical burden of a siege).*

And the piling up of earthen mounds against the walls will take three months more.

5. The general, unable to control his irritation, will launch his men to the assault like swarming ants, resulting in one-third of his men being slain, while the town remains untaken. Such are the disastrous effects of a siege.
> Cao Cao: This vividly describes the spectacle of an army of ants climbing a wall. Losing patience at the long delay, a general may make a premature, chaotic attempt to storm the fortress before his siege engines are ready, leading to massive casualties.

6. Therefore, the skillful leader subdues the enemy's troops without any fighting. He captures their cities without laying siege to them. He overthrows their kingdom without lengthy operations in the field.
> Jia Lin: He overthrows the government and its military capability, but intentionally does no harm to the civilian population or infrastructure.

7. With his forces completely intact, he will dispute the mastery of the Empire, and thus, without losing a single man, his triumph will be complete. 

This is the method of attacking by stratagem.

8. The rule in war is: 
- If our forces outnumber the enemy ten to one, surround him.
- If five to one, attack him.
- If twice as numerous, divide our army into two.
> Cao Cao: Being two to the enemy's one, we may use one part of our army as the regular (orthodox) force to engage the enemy, and the other as a special (unorthodox) diversion.
> Zhang Yu: If our force is twice as numerous, it should be split into two divisions: one to meet the enemy from the front, and one to fall upon his rear. If he replies to the frontal attack, he is crushed from behind; if he turns to the rearward attack, he is crushed in front.
> Du Mu: I take exception to this saying; dividing one's army violates a fundamental principle of war. *(Note: Du Mu advocates for concentrated force, showing that classical strategists often debated Sun Tzu's maxims based on context).*

9. 
- If equally matched, we can offer battle.
> Li Quan: If attackers and attacked are equally matched in strength, only the truly able general will fight and win.
> He Shi: I agree with Li Quan.

- If slightly inferior in numbers, we can avoid the enemy.
> Zhang Yu: This applies only if all other factors are equal. A small difference in numbers is often counterbalanced by superior energy, discipline, and morale.

- If quite unequal in every way, we must flee from him.

10. Hence, though an obstinate fight may be made by a small force, in the end, it must be captured by the larger, superior force.

11. The general is the bulwark of the State: if the bulwark is complete at all points, the State will be strong; if the bulwark is defective, the State will be weak.
> Li Quan: "Defective" indicates a gap or deficiency. If the general's ability is imperfect—meaning he is not thoroughly versed in his profession—his army will lack strength, leaving the State vulnerable.

12. There are three ways in which a ruler can bring misfortune upon his own army:

13. (1) By commanding the army to advance or retreat, being ignorant of the fact that it cannot obey. This is called "hobbling the army."
> Li Quan: It is like tying together the legs of a thoroughbred horse so that it is unable to gallop.
> Tai Gong: A kingdom should not be governed from without, and an army should not be directed from within. *(Note: The ruler in the capital should not micromanage the general on the battlefield).*

14. (2) By attempting to govern an army in the same way he administers a kingdom, being ignorant of the specific conditions that obtain in an army. This causes restlessness in the soldiers' minds.
> Cao Cao: The military sphere and the civil sphere are wholly distinct; you cannot handle an army with kid gloves.
> Zhang Yu: Humanity and justice are the principles on which to govern a state, but not an army. Opportunism, flexibility, and strict discipline are military virtues, distinct from civil virtues.

15. (3) By employing the officers of his army without discrimination, through ignorance of the military principle of adaptation to circumstances. This shakes the confidence of the soldiers.
> Mei Yao-chen: This refers to a ruler misallocating talent, placing the wrong officers in the wrong roles.
> Du Yu: If a general is ignorant of the principle of adaptability, he must never be entrusted with a position of authority.
> Du Mu: The skillful employer of men will use the wise man, the brave man, the covetous man, and the stupid man. For the wise man delights in establishing his merit, the brave man likes to show his courage in action, the covetous man is quick at seizing advantages, and the stupid man has no fear of death. *(Note: A great leader finds a use for every type of personality by adapting to their specific traits).*

16. When the army is restless and distrustful, trouble is sure to come from rival feudal princes. This brings anarchy into the army and flings victory away.

17. Thus, we may know that there are five essentials for victory:

(1) He will win who knows when to fight and when not to fight.
> Zhang Yu: If he can fight, he advances and takes the offensive; if he cannot fight, he retreats and remains on the defensive. He will invariably conquer who knows the precise moment to take the offensive or defensive.

(2) He will win who knows how to handle both superior and inferior forces.
> Li Quan: This is the general's ability to estimate numbers correctly and deploy them effectively.
> Zhang Yu: By applying the art of war, it is possible with a lesser force to defeat a greater, and vice versa. The secret lies in having an eye for terrain and in not letting the right moment slip by.
> Wu Qi: With a superior force, make for easy ground; with an inferior one, make for difficult ground.

(3) He will win whose army is animated by the same spirit throughout all its ranks.

(4) He will win who, prepared himself, waits to take the enemy unprepared.

(5) He will win who has military capacity and is not interfered with by the sovereign.
> Du Yu: As Wang Zi says: "It is the sovereign's function to give broad instructions, but to decide on battle is the exclusive function of the general."

Victory lies in the knowledge of these five points.

18. Hence the saying: If you know the enemy and know yourself, you need not fear the result of a hundred battles. 

If you know yourself but not the enemy, for every victory gained you will also suffer a defeat.
> Li Quan: *(A Historical Example)* Fu Jian, prince of Qin, marched with a vast army against the Jin Emperor in 383 A.D. When warned not to despise an enemy who commanded brilliant men like Xie An and Huan Chong, he boastfully replied: "I have the population of eight provinces at my back, infantry and horsemen numbering a million; why, they could dam up the Yangtze River itself by merely throwing their whips into the stream! What danger have I to fear?" Nevertheless, his forces were soon disastrously routed at the Fei River, and he was obliged to beat a hasty retreat.

If you know neither the enemy nor yourself, you will succumb in every battle.
> Zhang Yu: Knowing the enemy enables you to take the offensive; knowing yourself enables you to stand on the defensive. Attack is the secret of defense; defense is the planning of an attack.
