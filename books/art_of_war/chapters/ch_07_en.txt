Chapter VII. Maneuvering

1. Sun Tzu said: "In war, the general receives his commands from the sovereign."

> **Zhang Yu**: "The sovereign’s commands are the ultimate authority, but once an army crosses the border, the general's authority must be absolute on the field."

2. After gathering an army and concentrating his forces, a general must unify and harmonize its elements before pitching camp.

> **Cao Cao**: "He brings together the disparate elements of the army—different units, different classes of men—and weaves them into a cohesive whole."

3. Next comes tactical maneuvering—the most difficult phase of all. The true challenge of maneuvering lies in turning a devious route into a direct one, and forging an advantage out of misfortune.

> **Du Mu**: "Maneuvering is the art of moving a mass of men over great distances to gain a positional advantage, avoiding the enemy's strengths while targeting his weaknesses."

4. Thus, by taking a long, circuitous route to lure the enemy off guard, you can start after them yet arrive before them. This demonstrates mastery of the artifice of deviation.

> **Cao Cao**: "Make the enemy believe you are far away and posing no immediate threat, then strike where they least expect."

5. Maneuvering a disciplined army brings advantage; maneuvering a chaotic multitude invites disaster.

> **Li Quan**: "If the army is disciplined, maneuvering brings victory. If it lacks training and cohesion, trying to maneuver it will only result in confusion and defeat."

6. If you march a fully equipped army to seize an advantage, you will likely arrive too late. Conversely, dispatching a flying column for this purpose means abandoning its baggage and critical stores.

> **Du Mu**: "Speed is essential, but speed without supplies is a death sentence. To rush a heavy army is impossible; to strip it of its baggage is perilous."

7. Therefore, if you order your men to roll up their armor and march day and night without resting, pushing double the usual distance to cover a hundred li for a tactical edge, the commanders of all three divisions will fall into enemy hands.

8. Your strongest men will arrive first, the exhausted will lag behind, and only one-tenth of your force will reach the destination.

> **Zhang Yu**: "When men are pushed past their physical limits, order breaks down. The army strings out, and it becomes easy prey for a waiting enemy."

9. If you march fifty li to outmaneuver the enemy, you will lose the commander of your vanguard, and only half your force will arrive.

10. If you march thirty li with the same objective, only two-thirds of your army will reach the goal.

11. Understand this: an army without its baggage train is doomed; without provisions, it is lost; without supply bases, it cannot survive.

> **Cao Cao**: "Food and supplies are the lifeblood of the army. A hungry army cannot fight, no matter how brave."

12. We cannot forge alliances until we fully grasp the hidden designs of our neighbors.

> **Li Quan**: "Do not ally with a state whose ultimate goals clash with your own, for they will betray you when the time is right."

13. We are unfit to lead an army on the march unless we intimately know the terrain—its mountains and forests, its pitfalls and precipices, its marshes and swamps.

> **Du Mu**: "A general ignorant of the terrain is fighting blind. He will lead his men into traps and ambushes."

14. We cannot turn natural terrain to our advantage without employing local guides.

> **Zhang Yu**: "Maps can only show so much. Local guides know the hidden paths, the seasonal changes, and the secrets of the land."

15. In war, practice dissimulation, and you will succeed. Move only when there is a tangible advantage to be won.

> **Cao Cao**: "Deception is the core of warfare. Never let the enemy know your true intentions until it is too late for them to react."

16. Whether to concentrate or divide your forces must be dictated entirely by circumstance.

17. Let your speed be as swift as the wind, your formations as impenetrable as the forest.

> **Du Mu**: "When you move, move so quickly the enemy cannot react. When you camp, be so disciplined that the enemy can find no weakness to exploit."

18. In raiding and plundering, strike like blazing fire; in holding your ground, stand immovable as a mountain.

> **Li Quan**: "Fire consumes everything in its path, leaving nothing behind. A mountain stands firm, unaffected by the storms that rage around it."

19. Let your strategies remain as dark and unfathomable as the night, and when you strike, fall like a sudden thunderbolt.

> **Zhang Yu**: "Your plans must be hidden in absolute secrecy. When execution comes, it must be so sudden and overwhelming that the enemy has no time to blink."

20. When plundering the countryside, divide the spoils evenly among your troops; when capturing new territory, carve it into allotments to reward your soldiers.

> **Cao Cao**: "Rewards must be fair and swift to maintain morale. A general who hoards the spoils will soon find himself without an army."

21. Ponder and deliberate deeply before making any move.

22. The victor will be the one who has mastered the art of deviation. This is the essence of maneuvering.

23. The Book of Army Management states: "On the battlefield, the spoken word does not carry far enough; hence the creation of gongs and drums. Ordinary objects cannot be seen clearly enough; hence the creation of banners and flags."

> **Du Mu**: "The din of battle drowns out voices, and the dust obscures vision. Instruments and banners are the voice and hands of the general."

24. Gongs and drums, banners and flags serve to focus the eyes and ears of the entire host upon a single, unified command.

25. With the army forged into a single, cohesive body, it becomes impossible for the brave to advance alone, or for the cowardly to retreat alone. This is the art of commanding massive formations.

> **Zhang Yu**: "Unity is strength. The bravest warrior is useless if he attacks alone, and the most cowardly cannot flee if the formation holds firm."

26. In night battles, rely heavily on signal fires and drums; in day battles, use banners and flags to guide the eyes and ears of your troops.

27. An entire army can be robbed of its fighting spirit; a commanding general can be stripped of his presence of mind.

> **Cao Cao**: "Morale is fragile. If the spirit is broken, the army is defeated. If the general's mind is clouded, disaster will follow."

28. A soldier’s spirit is sharpest in the morning. By noon, it begins to flag. By evening, his mind wanders, thinking only of returning to camp.

> **Du Mu**: "This refers to the initial enthusiasm of a campaign, which wanes as fatigue and hardship take their toll."

29. A brilliant general avoids an enemy when their spirit is keen. Instead, he strikes when they are sluggish, exhausted, and longing for home. This is the art of mastering moods.

30. Remain disciplined and calm while waiting for chaos and disorder to consume the enemy. This is the art of retaining self-possession.

> **Li Quan**: "Let the enemy exhaust themselves with frenzied movements. Wait with a clear mind and a steady hand."

31. Arrive at the objective first and wait at ease while the enemy struggles to reach it. Be well-fed while the enemy starves. This is the art of husbanding strength.

> **Zhang Yu**: "Position is power. The one who waits at ease commands the battlefield."

32. Do not intercept an enemy marching with perfect banners, and do not attack formations drawn up in calm, confident discipline. This is the art of studying circumstances.

> **Cao Cao**: "Do not fight an enemy who is fully prepared and flawless in their formation. Wait for a mistake."

33. It is a military axiom: never advance uphill against an enemy, and never oppose them when they charge downhill.

34. Do not pursue an enemy who fakes a retreat. Do not attack soldiers whose fighting temper is sharp.

> **Du Mu**: "A feigned retreat is a trap designed to stretch your lines and draw you into an ambush."

35. Do not swallow the bait offered by the enemy. Do not interfere with an army that is heading home.

> **Li Quan**: "An army returning home fights with the desperation of men who see the end of their suffering. Let them pass."

36. When you surround an enemy, always leave them an avenue of escape. Do not press a desperate foe too hard, lest they fight to the death.

> **Cao Cao**: "Show the enemy a way out, and they will run. Close all doors, and they will fight with the fury of the doomed."

37. Such is the supreme art of warfare.
