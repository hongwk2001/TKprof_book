# Chapter 3: Soaring

I

For almost the entire time my uncle was planning and building his massive Crest Hill estate, I was busy in a small valley between that huge construction site and Lady Grove. I was immersed in increasingly expensive and ambitious experiments with flight. This work was the main focus of my life during the peak of the Tono-Bungay medicine empire's success.

I have already explained how I came to dedicate myself to this research. Disgusted with the usual adventures of life, I returned to my college studies, approaching them this time with a man's determination rather than a boy's ambition. From the very start, I excelled. I believe it was largely due to a special aptitude—a unique talent my mind happened to possess. It is one of those random gifts people get that has nothing to do with their actual character; being proud or modest about it is equally silly. I accomplished an enormous amount of work during those years. For a time, I worked with a fierce concentration that used up almost every ounce of energy I had. I solved a series of problems about the stability of objects flying in the air and the internal movements of the wind. I also completely revolutionized a major part of explosive engine theory. These findings were published in academic journals like the Philosophical Transactions and the Mathematical Journal, so there is no need to explain them here. Honestly, I doubt I could explain them here anyway. When doing specialized work, you develop a sort of mental shorthand. I have never taught or lectured—meaning I have never had to explain mechanical concepts in everyday language—and trying to do so now would be incredibly tedious.

At first, my work was mostly theoretical. I could test my early ideas using very small models, a turntable to simulate motion through the air, and basic materials like cane, whalebone, and silk. But eventually, unpredictable factors arose—things like human limits and gaps in experimental knowledge—where I simply had to guess and try. Then I had to increase the scale of my experiments, and soon they grew enormously. I worked almost simultaneously on the balance and stability of gliders and on steering inflated gas bags, which was a particularly expensive branch of research. I was definitely influenced by the same spirit of lavish spending that was currently consuming my uncle. Before long, my setup above Lady Grove had grown into a large painted wooden cabin that could house six men, where I sometimes lived for three weeks straight, a gas tank, a garage, three large metal-roofed sheds, a launching platform for gliders, a workshop, and more. We even built a rough road. We brought in gas from the town of Cheaping and electricity from Woking, where I also found a friendly workshop to handle larger tasks. I was lucky enough to find a heaven-sent assistant named Cothope. He was self-educated, a former military engineer, and one of the best, most practical working engineers alive. Without him, I doubt I would have achieved half of what I did. At times, he was more of a collaborator than an assistant, and he has stuck by me to this day. Other men came and went as I needed them.

I don't know how to explain to someone who hasn't experienced it the unique satisfaction of sustained research when money is no object. It is different from any other human effort. You are completely free from the exhausting conflicts with other people—at least as far as the actual work goes. For me, that was its greatest reward. Scientific truth is the most elusive mistress; she hides in strange places and requires a difficult, exhausting journey to find, but she is always there! If you reach her, she will never let you down; she belongs to you and to all of humanity forever. She is reality—the only true reality I have found in this chaotic life. She won't sulk, misunderstand you, or cheat you out of your reward because of petty doubts. You cannot change her with advertising or noise, and you cannot suffocate her with vulgarity. The things you build when serving her are permanent, unlike anything else in human life. That, I believe, is the unique satisfaction and lasting reward of science.

Taking up experimental work completely changed my personal habits. I've mentioned before how I once had a period of intense discipline at Wimblehurst, and how London's endless distractions later demoralized me when I moved to South Kensington. I also gave up much of my personal pride when I left science to develop Tono-Bungay. However, poverty kept me disciplined, and my youthful romanticism kept me well-behaved until my marriage was established. But after that, I let myself go in every way. I still worked hard, but I never cared if I was reaching my full potential, or if my occasional lazy moods could be avoided. Once I became wealthy, I ate too much, drank freely, and followed my impulses carelessly. I saw no reason to act differently. I never pushed myself to my limits. Even the emotional crisis of my divorce didn't immediately change my lack of personal discipline. At first, I found it hard to focus on science, which is much more demanding than business, but I coped by smoking. I smoked an excessive number of cigars. They gave me deep moods of depression, which I usually treated by simply lighting another cigar. I didn't realize how weak my morals and nerves had become until I reached the practical side of my research: I had to find out what it actually felt like to fly a glider and what a person could accomplish with one.

I fell into these lazy habits despite having a strong natural tendency toward discipline. I've never liked self-indulgence. I instinctively distrust a lifestyle of slack jaws and fat bellies. I prefer things that are bare, simple, austere, and disciplined—clean lines and cold colors. But in these prosperous times, when everyone has too much and the struggle for survival is just about advertising and trying to impress the neighbors, we lose ourselves. There is no urgent need for personal courage, strong nerves, or stark beauty. In the past, most people didn't overeat simply because they couldn't, and almost everyone stayed fit through unavoidable physical labor and personal danger. Now, if you set your standards low enough and swallow your pride, almost anyone can indulge in excess. You can drift through modern life dodging challenges, indulging yourself, and slacking off. You can live without ever being truly hungry, frightened, or deeply moved; your highest emotional peak might just be a sentimental thrill, and your first real encounter with basic human necessity might be the sweat on your deathbed. That was how my uncle lived, and it was very nearly how I lived, too.

But the glider snapped me to attention. I had to find out how these machines moved through the air, and the only way to do that was to ride one down. For a while, I refused to face it.

Writing a book offers a kind of distance, I suppose. At any rate, I find I can write down a confession here that I've never been able to tell anyone face-to-face: the terrifying struggle it took to force myself to do something that any island boy in the West Indies could probably do without blinking—throwing myself into the wind for my first flight. The first attempt was bound to be the worst. It was a gamble with my life, and I figured my chances of dying or getting hurt were about equal to my chances of success. I saw that fact with crystal clarity. I started with a glider I thought was built like the Wright brothers' airplane, but I couldn't be certain. It might flip over. I might upset the balance. It might dive nose-first and crush both itself and me. Flying required sharp attention; I couldn't just jump off with my eyes closed, or get angry, or get drunk to do it. I had to use my body weight to balance. And when I finally did it, it was horrifying—for ten seconds. For about ten seconds, as I swept through the air lying flat on that terrifying frame with the wind in my eyes, the rushing ground below filled me with sickening, helpless terror. I felt a violent, shaking sensation in my brain and spine, and I groaned out loud. I gritted my teeth and groaned. The sound was forced out of me against my will. My panic reached its absolute peak. And then, you know, it just ended!

Suddenly, my terror was gone. I was flying through the air, perfectly upright and steady, and nothing had gone wrong. I felt intensely alive, my nerves pulled tight as a bowstring. I moved a leg, turned, and shouted in a mix of fear and triumph as I corrected the turn, leaned the other way, and steadied the machine.

I thought I was going to hit a crow that flew across my path. It was bizarre how quietly and suddenly it appeared out of nowhere, and I yelled helplessly, "Get out of the way!" The bird folded into a V-shape, flapped wildly, shot up to the right, and vanished from sight. Then I saw the shadow of my airplane gliding steadily ahead of me, and the grass below seemed to stream out behind it. The grass! It wasn't actually moving past me at an impossibly fast speed after all.

When I finally glided down to the safe, flat patch of green I had chosen, I was as calm and ready as a city commuter stepping off a moving bus, and I had learned much more than just how to fly. I pulled the nose up at exactly the right moment, leveled out, and touched the ground as softly as a snowflake on a calm day. I lay flat for a second, then knelt up and got to my feet. I was trembling, but incredibly satisfied with myself. Cothope was already running down the hill toward me.

From that day on, I went into training, and I stayed in training for many months. I had delayed my experiments for almost six weeks making various excuses because I was terrified of that first flight, and because business life had made me physically and mentally soft. The shame of my cowardice pushed me hard, even though it was probably a secret only I knew. I felt that Cothope, at least, might suspect. Well—I was determined he would never suspect again.

It's funny that I remember that shame, guilt, and its aftermath much more clearly than the weeks of hesitation before I actually flew. For a while, I gave up alcohol and smoking completely. I ate very little, and every day I did something to test my nerves and muscles. I flew the glider as often as possible. I swapped the London train for a motorcycle, risking the busy southbound traffic, and I even tried horseback riding to see if it offered any thrills. But they gave me calm, fully-trained horses, and I developed a rather unfair contempt for the predictability of riding compared to the wild adventure of machines. I also practiced walking along the top of the high wall behind the Lady Grove garden, and eventually forced myself to jump across the wide gap where the gate was. Even if those exercises didn't completely cure my natural fear of heights, they trained my willpower to the point where the fear didn't matter. Soon, I was no longer afraid of flying; instead, I was eager to go higher. I began to view gliding—which, even over the deepest valleys, rarely dropped more than forty feet—as a mere joke compared to what real flight could be. I started dreaming of the crisp, sharp air high above the beech forests. It was mostly to satisfy that desire, rather than as a practical next step in my research, that I soon poured my energy and most of my personal fortune into developing a steerable hot-air balloon.

II

I had progressed far past those early stages. I had survived two major crashes and a broken rib, which my aunt nursed back to health with intense energy,. I was even building a reputation in the aviation world when, suddenly, the Honorable Beatrice Normandy walked back into my life. It felt as if she had never really left. With her dark eyes and the familiar messy wave of hair falling across her forehead, she came riding down a grassy path in the woods below Lady Grove on a massive black horse. The old Earl of Carnaby and her half-brother, Archie Garvell, were with her. My uncle had been bothering me about the hot-water pipes at his Crest Hill mansion, and we were walking on a path that crossed theirs when we suddenly bumped into them. The old Earl was actually trespassing on our land, so he cheerfully called out and stopped to chat.

At first, I didn't even notice Beatrice. I was fascinated by Lord Carnaby, a remarkable living relic of his own brilliant past. I had heard of him but had never seen him in person. For a sixty-five-year-old man who had supposedly committed every sin in the book and ruined the most promising political career of his generation, he looked incredibly fit and healthy. He was a skinny little man with a tanned face and grayish-blue eyes, and his harsh, cracked voice was his worst feature.

"Hope you don't mind us coming this way, Ponderevo," he called out. My uncle, who was sometimes overly eager and generous with noble titles, replied, "Not at all, my lord, not at all! Glad you make use of it!"

"You're building a massive place over the hill," Carnaby noted.

"Thought I'd make a real show of it for once," my uncle said. "It looks so big because it's spread out to catch the sun."

"Air and sunlight," the Earl agreed. "You can't have too much of them. But back in the old days, they used to build for shelter, water, and access to the main road."

It was only then that I realized the quiet figure sitting behind the Earl was Beatrice.

For a moment, I had forgotten her so completely that I thought she hadn't changed at all since the days when she used to watch me from behind Lady Drew's skirts. She was looking at me, her pretty forehead wrinkled in confusion beneath her wide-brimmed hat—she was wearing a gray hat and an unbuttoned, loose coat—trying, I suppose, to remember where she had seen me before. Her shaded eyes met mine with an unspoken question.

It seemed unbelievable to me that she didn't remember.

"Well," the Earl said, giving his horse a nudge.

Garvell was busy patting his restless horse's neck, ignoring me entirely. He nodded over his shoulder and followed the Earl. His movement seemed to unlock a chain of memories for her. She suddenly glanced at him, then looked back at me, her face lighting up with recognition that quickly turned into a warm smile. She hesitated as if she was going to speak to me, gave a broad, understanding smile, and then turned to follow the others. All three broke into a light gallop, and she never looked back. I stood at the crossroads for a second or two, watching her ride away, before I realized that my uncle was already a few paces ahead, talking over his shoulder, assuming I was right behind him. I turned and hurried to catch up. My mind was completely consumed by Beatrice and this unexpected encounter. I had remembered her simply as a Normandy. I had completely forgotten that Garvell was the son, and she the stepdaughter, of our neighbor, Lady Osprey. In fact, I had probably forgotten entirely that Lady Osprey was even our neighbor. There was no reason I should have remembered it. It was shocking to find her here in the Surrey countryside when I had never imagined her living anywhere else but at Bladesover Park—which was nearly forty miles away and twenty years in the past. She was so full of life—and so unchanged! She had that same quick, warm flush in her cheeks. It felt like it was just yesterday that we had kissed among the fern stems...

"Huh?" I asked, distracted.

"I said, he's a class act," my uncle said. "You can say whatever you want about the aristocracy, George, but Lord Carnaby is the real deal. They have a kind of Savoir Faire—it's an old-fashioned phrase, George, but it fits. They have Bon Ton... It's like the perfect grass at Oxford, George; you can't just grow it in a year. I wonder how they do it. It comes from always living on a certain Scale, George. It's about being born into it."

"She could be a Romney painting come to life!" I said to myself.

"People tell all sorts of wild stories about him," my uncle continued, "but what does it all really mean?"

"Good lord!" I thought to myself. "Why did I forget for so long? Those strange little eyebrows, that mischievous look in her eyes—the way she breaks into a smile!"

"I don't blame him," my uncle said. "Most of it is just imagination. That, and having too much free time, George. When I was a young man, I was kept pretty busy. And so were you. Even back then—!"

What confused me most was the strange trick my memory had played. When I had run into Garvell earlier, I hadn't recalled anything important about Beatrice at all. I had only remembered our boyish rivalry and the fight we had. But now that my senses were full of her again, it seemed impossible that I could ever have forgotten her.

III

"Oh, for heaven's sake!" my aunt said, reading a letter from behind her coffee machine. "Here's a young woman, George!"

We were having breakfast together in the large bay window at Lady Grove, which overlooked the iris beds. My uncle was away in London.

I made a questioning sound and sliced the top off a boiled egg.

"Who is Beatrice Normandy?" my aunt asked. "I've never heard of her."

"Is she the young woman?"

"Yes. She says she knows you. I'm no expert on old-fashioned etiquette, George, but her approach is a bit unusual. She essentially says she's going to make her mother—"

"Wait. Stepmother, right?"

"You seem to know a lot about her. She says 'mother'—Lady Osprey. Anyway, they're coming to call on me next Wednesday at four o'clock, and you have to be here for tea."

"Huh?"

"You—for tea."

"Hmm. She had quite a... strong personality. Back when I knew her."

I noticed my aunt's head leaning out from behind the coffee machine, her wide blue eyes staring at me with intense curiosity. I met her gaze for a second, flinched, blushed, and then laughed.

"I've known her longer than I've known you," I said, and then I explained the whole story to her.

My aunt kept a close eye on me, peering over and around the coffee machine the entire time I spoke. She was deeply interested and asked several questions to clarify things.

"Why didn't you tell me the day you saw her? You've been thinking about her for a week," she said.

"It is weird that I didn't tell you," I admitted.

"You thought I'd take a dislike to her," my aunt said decisively. "That's exactly what you thought." And with that, she went back to opening the rest of her mail.

The two women arrived in a pony-drawn carriage exactly on time, and I got to witness the rare sight of my aunt entertaining guests. We had tea on the terrace under the cedar tree. Old Lady Osprey, being a strict Protestant, had never actually been inside the house before, so we took a sort of guided tour that reminded me of my own first visit. Even though I was entirely focused on Beatrice, I filed away a funny little memory of how totally different the other two women were. My aunt was tall, skinny, and a bit clumsy, wearing a plain blue house dress; she was a huge reader with a genuine, sharp wit. Lady Osprey, on the other hand, was short, chubby, and dressed in overly fussy Victorian clothes. She was the kind of woman whose intellectual life consisted of palm reading and polite romance novels. Her face was flushed pink, and she seemed generally flustered by my aunt's social awkwardness. To cope, she acted like she was doing a bad impression of her own cook trying to act like a queen. One seemed made of wire, the other of dough. My aunt was nervous, partly because Lady Osprey was difficult to handle, and partly because she desperately wanted to watch Beatrice and me. When my aunt got nervous, she tended to make exaggerated, clumsy gestures and use even more of her weird slang than usual, which only made the titled lady more confused and pink. For example, I heard my aunt admit that one of the Stuart Durgan women seemed a bit "crazy in the coconut." She described medieval knights as "galloping around hoping to bump into a dragon." She explained that she was "always messing around in the garden," and instead of offering me a normal biscuit, she asked me in her faint lisp, "Would you like some squashed flies, George?" I was absolutely sure Lady Osprey would describe her as "a most eccentric person" at the very first chance she got—"a most eccentric person." You could practically see her preparing to say it.

Beatrice was dressed very simply in brown, wearing a bold, wide-brimmed hat. She had a surprising new air of being grown-up and responsible. She guided her stepmother through the initial awkward greetings, studied my aunt, and smoothly got us all moving through the house. Then, she turned her attention to me with a quick, slightly confident smile.

"We haven't met," she said, "since—"

"It was in the Warren."

"Of course," she said. "The Warren! I remembered everything except the name... I was only eight."

Her smiling eyes seemed to demand that my memory be just as clear. I looked up and met her gaze directly, a little unsure of what to say.

"I completely betrayed you," she said, studying my face. "And later on, I betrayed Archie, too."

She turned her face away from the others, and her voice dropped just a little.

"They gave him a beating for lying!" she said, sounding as if it were a fond memory. "And when it was all over, I went out to our wigwam. Do you remember the wigwam?"

"Out in the West Wood?"

"Yes—and I cried. I suppose I was crying for all the trouble I'd caused you... I've thought about it often since then."

Lady Osprey paused so we could catch up. "My dear!" she said to Beatrice. "What a beautiful gallery!" Then she stared very hard at me, clearly completely baffled as to who I was and what I was doing there.

"People say the oak staircase is quite nice," my aunt said, leading the way upward.

As Lady Osprey gathered her skirts to climb the stairs, keeping one hand on the railing, she turned and shot a look at Beatrice—a look that was absolutely overflowing with meaning. The main point was obviously a warning about me, but it was just generally loaded with disapproval. I happened to catch Beatrice's reaction in a mirror: she wrinkled her nose and made a fast, incredibly demonic face back at her. Lady Osprey turned a deeper shade of pink and was so offended she was speechless. She clearly decided she was washing her hands of the whole situation as she followed my aunt upstairs.

"It's dark, but it does have a certain dignity," Beatrice said very clearly, looking calmly down the hall and intentionally letting the reluctant footsteps on the stairs get further away from us. She stood one step up, so she was looking slightly down at me and out over the old hallway.

As soon as she was sure her stepmother was out of earshot, she turned to me abruptly.

"But how did you end up here?" she asked.

"Here?"

"All of this." She waved her hand to take in the massive hall, the tall windows, and the sunny terrace, indicating the wealth and leisure it represented. "Weren't you the housekeeper's son?"

"I've been on an adventure. My uncle has become... a major financier. He used to be a small-town pharmacist about twenty miles from Bladesover. Now we're company promoters, corporate amalgamators—we're big players in the new economy."

"I see." She looked at me with intense interest, clearly trying to figure me out.

"And you recognized me?" I asked.

"After a second or two. I saw that you recognized me. I couldn't quite place you, but I knew that I knew you. Then seeing Archie there helped me piece it together."

"I'm glad we met again," I said cautiously. "I never forgot you."

"People don't forget childhood things like that."

We looked at each other for a moment, feeling a strangely easy and confident satisfaction in being reunited. I can't explain why we were instantly so drawn to each other, but we were. We liked each other, and we both knew, without a doubt, that the feeling was mutual. Right from the start, we were completely comfortable together. 

"So picturesque, just so picturesque," a voice floated down from above, followed immediately by: "Bee-atrice!"

"I have a hundred things I want to ask you about," she said with easy familiarity as we started up the winding stairs.

Later, as the four of us sat having tea under the cedar tree on the terrace, she asked me about my aviation work. My aunt chimed in with a detail or two about my broken ribs. Lady Osprey clearly thought flying was a highly inappropriate and distasteful subject—as if we were rudely invading the angels' territory. 

"It's not exactly flying," I clarified. "We can't actually fly yet."

"And you never will," she stated firmly. "You never will."

"Well," I said, "we're doing what we can."

The little lady raised a small, gloved hand and held it about four feet off the ground. "This far," she said. "This far—and no further! No!"

Her face turned a very bright pink. "No," she repeated with finality, and gave a sharp little cough. "Thank you," she then said, accepting her ninth biscuit.

Or her tenth cake. Beatrice burst out laughing, looking at me. I was lying on the grass, which probably confused Lady Osprey even more about the primal curse she was referencing.

"Upon his belly shall he go," she said very clearly, "all the days of his life."

After that, we didn't talk about aeronautics anymore.

Beatrice sat curled up in a chair, watching me with the same intense scrutiny and adventurous aggression that I had faced so long ago at the tea-table in my mother's room. She looked amazingly like the little Princess from my Bladesover memories. The unruly mess of her hair seemed exactly the same, as did her voice—things you would have expected to change completely. She made plans with the same quickness and acted with the same reckless decisiveness.

She stood up suddenly.

"What's past the terrace?" she asked, and I immediately joined her.

I made up a view for her on the spot.

At the far corner away from the cedar tree, she hopped up onto the low wall and made herself comfortable among the mossy stones. "Now," she said, "tell me everything about yourself. I know so many boring men! They all do the exact same things. How did you get... here? All the men I know were just always here. They couldn't have gotten here if they hadn't been here their whole lives. They wouldn't even think it was right. You've climbed up."

"If you can call it climbing," I said.

She changed the subject abruptly. "It's... I don't know if you'll understand... interesting to meet you again. I remembered you. I don't know why, but I did. I've used you as a sort of character model when I've told myself stories. But in my stories, you were always stiff and difficult—some guy in a cheap suit, like a Labour Party politician or a radical atheist. You aren't like that at all. And yet... you are!"

She looked closely at me. "Was it a hard fight? People make it sound like it was."

"I don't know why."

"I was shot up here by accident," I said. "There was no fight at all. Except maybe the fight to stay honest, and I didn't exactly shine in that regard. My uncle and I mixed up a medicine, and it launched us to the top. There's no real merit in it! But you've been here the whole time. First, tell me what you've done."

"Well, there's one thing we didn't do." She thought for a moment.

"What's that?" I asked.

"Produce a little half-brother to inherit Bladesover. So the estate went to the Phillbrick family. And they rented it out! And my stepmother and I... we rented a place, too. Now we live in a little house."

She nodded vaguely over her shoulder and then turned back to me. "Well, let's say it was an accident. But here you are! Now that you're here, what are you going to do? You're young. Are you going into Parliament? I heard some men talking about you the other day, before I realized who you were. They said that's what you ought to do."

She interrogated me about my plans with intense, vivid curiosity. It was exactly like how she had tried to imagine me as a soldier and figure out my place years ago. She made me feel more aimless and accidental than ever. "You want to build a flying machine," she went on, "and what happens when you finally fly? What then? Will you use it for fighting?"

I told her a bit about my experimental work. She had never heard of a soaring airplane, and the idea excited her; she was eager to hear all about it. Until then, she had thought all aviation work was just drawing up impossible machines on paper. She had never heard of pioneers like Pilcher and Lilienthal, or even known that such men existed.

"But that's dangerous!" she said, as if realizing it for the first time.

"Oh!—yes, it's dangerous."

"Bee-atrice!" Lady Osprey called out.

Beatrice hopped down from the wall.

"Where do you do this soaring?"

"Over beyond the high hills. East of Crest Hill and the woods."

"Do you mind if people come and watch?"

"Whenever you like. Just let me know."

"I'll take my chances someday. Someday soon." She looked at me thoughtfully, smiled, and our conversation was over.

IV

All my later work in aeronautics is tied in my memory to Beatrice—to her occasional presence, the things she said and did, and the thoughts I had about her.

By the spring of that year, I had built a flying machine that was perfect in every way except for longitudinal stability. My model would fly like a bird for fifty or a hundred yards, and then it would either dive and smash its nose, or—more often—pitch backward, slide down tail-first, and break its propeller. The rhythm of this pitching baffled me. I felt it must be governed by some laws that hadn't been clearly defined yet. So, I spent some time studying theory and existing literature. I stumbled upon a series of ideas that eventually led to what became known as "Ponderevo's Principle" and earned me a fellowship in the Royal Society. I worked all this out in three long papers. Meanwhile, I built a lot of turntable models and gliders, and I started working on the idea of combining gas balloons with gliders. Ballooning was new to me. I made one or two flights in the Aero Club's balloons before I started building my gas tank and balloon shed, and I sent Cothope off for a couple of months to work with Sir Peter Rumchase. My uncle provided some of the funding for these developments; he was getting competitive and interested in the field because of Lord Boom's prize and all the publicity it would bring. It was at his request that I named my first steerable balloon "Lord Roberts Alpha."

Lord Roberts Alpha very nearly put an end to all my research. The basic idea for this craft—and its more successful and famous successor, Lord Roberts Beta—was to use a collapsible balloon with a rigid, flat bottom. Shaped somewhat like an upside-down boat, the balloon was designed to *almost* lift the weight of the entire apparatus, but not quite. The gas bag used the chambered design typical of long airships, without an internal balloonette. The challenge was making the whole thing collapsible. I tried to solve this by covering it with a fine-meshed silk net that could be rolled up on two long rods running down its sides. Basically, I squeezed my sausage-shaped gas bag by tightening the net around it. The mechanics at the ends were too complicated to explain here, but I had designed them very carefully. Lord Roberts Alpha had a single large propeller in the front and a rudder in the back. The engine was the first one to be mounted directly in line with the gas bag. I lay horizontally right underneath the balloon on a glider-like frame, far away from both the engine and the rudder, controlling them with wire pulls similar to bicycle brakes.

Lord Roberts Alpha has already been extensively diagrammed and described in various aeronautical journals. The fatal flaw that I hadn't foreseen was the poor quality of the silk netting. It ripped near the back as soon as I started to contract the balloon. The last two segments immediately bulged out through the hole, just like an inner tube bulging through a blown tire. The sharp edge of the torn net then sliced into the oiled silk of the swollen last segment along a weak seam, bursting it with a loud bang.

Up until that moment, everything had been going incredibly well. As a steerable balloon, before I contracted it, Lord Roberts Alpha was a complete success. It had cruised out of the shed beautifully at nine or ten miles an hour. Even though there was a mild southwesterly breeze, it had climbed, turned, and faced into the wind as well as any similar craft I've ever seen.

I was lying flat on my stomach in my usual glider position, and because all the machinery was hidden from view, I felt an amazing sensation of independent hovering. Only by looking up and craning my neck back could I see the flat underside of the balloon and hear the rapid *swish, swish, swish* of the propeller blades. I flew in a wide circle over Lady Grove and Duffield, headed out toward Effingham, and successfully returned to my starting point.

Down below in the October sunlight, I could see my sheds and the small crowd that had gathered to watch the launch. They were all looking up, most of them studying my face through binoculars. I could see Lord Carnaby and Beatrice on horseback, along with two girls I didn't recognize. There was Cothope, three or four of my workmen, my aunt, and Mrs. Levinstein, who was staying with her, on foot. The local vet, Dimmock, and a few others were there too. My shadow drifted slightly to the north of them, looking like the shadow of a fish. At Lady Grove, the servants had come out onto the lawn, and the playground at the Duffield school was swarming with kids who were too busy playing to care about my flight. But over toward Crest Hill—which looked incredibly squat and ugly from the air—there were clusters and lines of workmen everywhere, all standing around staring instead of working., Thinking about it now, it might have been their lunch hour; it was close to noon., I hovered for a moment, enjoying the view, then turned to face a clear, open stretch of hills. I pushed the engine to full speed and activated the rollers to tighten the net and compress the gas bags. The reduced drag made the craft speed up instantly...

In the moment right before the bang, I think I must have actually been flying. Just before the net tore, at the exact instant the balloon was fully compressed, I am convinced the entire machine was heavier than air. People have debated that claim, but arguing about who did it first is trivial anyway.

Then, there was a sudden deceleration, immediately followed by the machine tilting downwards in a completely terrifying way. I still remember it with horror. I couldn't see what was happening, and I couldn't even guess. It was a bizarre, inexplicable nosedive. The machine seemed to be kicking its tail up into the air for absolutely no reason. The loud bang followed right after, and I realized I was falling fast.

I was too surprised to immediately figure out what had caused the noise. I don't even know what I thought it was at first. I suppose I was obsessed with every aviator's worst nightmare: a spark between the engine and the balloon. But I clearly wasn't engulfed in flames, so I should have known instantly that it wasn't a fire. Whatever else was going through my mind, I did manage to release the rollers holding the outer net, allowing the balloon to expand again, which undoubtedly helped break my fall. I don't actually remember doing it. All I really remember is the dizzying sight of the landscape spiraling up to meet me, the blur of fields, trees, and cottages rushing past my left shoulder, and the oppressive feeling that the whole machine was pressing down hard on top of my head. I didn't try to stop the propeller. It kept spinning—*swish, swish, swish*—the entire way down.

Cothope actually knows more about the crash than I do. He described my start toward the east, the sudden tilt, and then the back of the balloon swelling up and bursting like a bladder. Then I plunged downward very quickly, though not nearly as steeply as it felt to me. "Fifteen or twenty degrees," Cothope said, "to be exact." He was the one who told me that I had released the nets and slowed the fall. He thinks I was much more in control than I remember.

I don't know why I would have forgotten doing something so smart. He got the impression that I was actively steering and trying to aim for the beech trees at Farthing Down. "You hit the trees," he said, "and the whole thing stood up on its nose among the branches before slowly crumpling. I saw you get jerked out, or so I thought, and I didn't wait around to see anything else. I ran straight for my bicycle."

In reality, landing in the woods was pure luck. I'm pretty sure I had about as much control as a package in the mail. I remember bracing myself and thinking, "Here it comes!" as the trees rushed toward me. If I remember that, I should remember steering. Then the propeller shattered, the machine jerked to a violent halt, and I found myself falling into a canopy of yellowing leaves. It seemed to me as if Lord Roberts Alpha was bouncing back up into the sky.

I felt twigs and branches slapping my face, but I didn't feel hurt at the time. I grabbed at branches that snapped under my weight, tumbling through a blur of green and yellow into a dark world of massive, bark-covered limbs. Thrashing wildly, I managed to get a grip on a thick, solid branch and hung there.

My mind suddenly became incredibly sharp and clear. I hung from the branch for a second, looked around, grabbed another one, and then swung myself into a sturdy fork in the tree. I swung a leg over it and was able to climb down very calmly and deliberately. I dropped the last ten feet from the lowest branch and landed on my feet. "I'm okay," I said aloud. I looked up through the leaves to see the crumpled, deflated remains of what used to be Lord Roberts Alpha draped like a bizarre decoration across the broken branches. "Good lord!" I said. "What a fall!"

I wiped something wet dripping down my face and was shocked to see my hand covered in blood. I looked down at myself and saw...

an astonishing amount of blood pouring down my arm and shoulder. 

I realized my mouth was full of blood. It’s a bizarre moment when you realize you’re hurt—possibly badly hurt—but you still have to figure out exactly how bad it is. I carefully felt my face and discovered an unfamiliar shape on the left side. The jagged end of a broken branch had pierced right through my cheek, damaging the cheek, teeth, and gums, and had left a splinter lodged in my upper jaw like an explorer’s flag marking the farthest point reached. That and a sprained wrist were my only injuries. But I was bleeding as if I had been hacked to pieces, and it felt as though my face had been caved in. I can’t even describe the sheer, horrifying disgust I felt at that moment.

"I have to stop this bleeding somehow," I mumbled groggily.

"I wonder where I can find a spider's web"—a strange leap for my mind to take, referencing an old folk remedy. But it was the only treatment I could think of.

I must have had some idea of getting home without help, because I had walked thirty yards from the tree before I collapsed.

Then, a sort of black disc appeared in the center of my vision, rushed outward to the edges, and blotted everything out. I don't remember falling. I fainted from the shock, the disgust at my injuries, and the loss of blood, and I lay there until Cothope found me.

He was the first to find me, having raced across the hillside grass and taken a wide detour to cut through the narrowest part of the Carnaby estate woods. While he was trying to apply his standard first-aid training to a rather unusual situation, Beatrice came galloping at full speed through the trees. Lord Carnaby was right behind her. She had lost her hat, was covered in mud from a fall, and was as pale as a ghost. "And completely calm, too," Cothope noted later, turning the memory over in his mind as he told me., "Women never seem to completely lose their heads, but they never seem entirely rational either," Cothope said, generalizing about the sex.,

He also witnessed her acting with remarkable decisiveness. The debate was whether I should be taken to her stepmother's current residence at Bedley Corner—the Carnaby estate's dower house—or down to Lord Carnaby's main house at Easting. Beatrice had absolutely no doubts; she fully intended to nurse me herself. Carnaby clearly didn't want that. "She insisted her place wasn't even half as far," Cothope told me. "She completely stared us down....

"I hate being proven wrong, so I actually measured it with a pedometer later. It's exactly forty-three yards further.

"Lord Carnaby looked her right in the eye," Cothope said, finishing the story, "and then he just gave in."

V

But my story has jumped from June to October. During that time, my relationship with Beatrice—and with the countryside that was her home—had developed in many ways. She came and went, moving in circles I knew nothing about, traveling to London, Paris, Wales, and Northampton. Meanwhile, her stepmother followed her own independent schedule, intermittently vanishing and reappearing. When they were at home, they lived under the strict rules of an inflexible old maid named Charlotte, and Beatrice acted as if she owned Lord Carnaby’s extensive stables. Her interest in me was obvious from the very beginning. She found her way to my workshops and, despite Cothope's genuine attempts to discourage her, quickly became an enthusiastic amateur aviation student. She would drop by sometimes in the morning, sometimes in the afternoon. Sometimes she walked over with an Irish terrier; other times she rode her horse. She might visit every day for three or four days, then disappear for a few weeks, only to return.

It wasn’t long before I found myself looking forward to her visits. From the start, I found her incredibly fascinating. As I’ve made clear, my experience with women was extremely limited, and she was a completely new type of woman to me. But she didn't just make me interested in her; she made me interested in myself. She became something that profoundly alters a man's world. How should I put it? She became my audience. Since escaping the emotional turmoil of that whole affair, I’ve thought about it from a hundred different angles, and it seems to me that the way men and women act as audiences for each other is a strangely powerful force in life. For some people, having an audience feels like a vital necessity; they seek attention the way animals seek food. Others, like my uncle, can comfortably perform for an imaginary audience. Personally, I believe I have always lived—and can continue to live—without one. In my youth, I was my own audience and my own judge of character. But putting an audience in your mind means you start playing a part; you become self-conscious and dramatic. For years, I had been completely absorbed in science, forgetting myself entirely. I had lived for my work and objective facts. But then, in Beatrice's eyes, I found scrutiny, applause, and expectation. And so, I began living for the effect I imagined I was having on her. It quickly became the most important thing in my life. I performed for her. I did things just for the look of them. I started daydreaming more and more about dramatic situations, heroic poses, and beautiful moments with her and for her.

I'm writing these things down because they still confuse me. I suppose I was in love with Beatrice, as people generally understand the term. But it was an entirely different feeling from my intense hunger for Marion, or my sharp, physical desire for Effie. Those feelings were selfish and deeply honest—as basic and instinctive as a tiger pouncing. But until things reached a breaking point with Beatrice, my feelings for her were more like a massive, overwhelming surge of imagination. I am writing this down very seriously, even though I know these are probably basic, everyday concepts for most people. The love that grew between Beatrice and me was, I think—and I say this hesitantly and with some curiosity—romantic love. That unfortunate, short-lived affair between my uncle and Mrs. Scrymgeour was actually made of the exact same stuff, even if the tone was a bit different. I have to admit that. In both cases, the need for an audience was the driving force.

Its effect on me was that it turned me back into a teenager in many ways. It made me obsessed with honor, and eager to do grand, splendid things—especially brave things. In that sense, it lifted me up and pushed me forward. But it also pushed me toward cheap, showy behavior. Deep down, it was fake; it turned my life into a stage set. One side faced the audience, looking perfect, while the hidden side was completely hollow. It definitely ruined the patience and quality of my work. Because both she and I were so eager for flashy, impressive flights that looked good from the ground, I started cutting corners on my research. I avoided the harder, longer path.

And it completely stripped away my ability to recognize when I was being ridiculous.

But that wasn't the only thing in our relationship. That primal, elemental instinct was there too. And it hit me very suddenly.

It was a summer day, though without checking my lab notes I couldn't tell you if it was July or August. I was testing a new, more bird-like airplane. I had based the wing curves on studies by aviation pioneers like Lilienthal, Pilcher, and Phillips, hoping it would stabilize the pitching motion better than my previous models. I was gliding down my usual long course, launching from the ramp on the old hill near my sheds and heading toward Tinker’s Corner. The route is mostly wide-open rolling hills, except for a few patches of boxwood and thorn bushes to the right, and a small, bushy ravine with a rabbit warren cutting across from the east. I had just taken off and was intensely focused on the long, sweeping dive this new design always made. Then, completely out of nowhere, Beatrice appeared right in front of me. She had been riding toward Tinker's Corner to intercept me and chat. She glanced over her shoulder, saw me coming, and nudged her horse into a gallop. But the panicked animal bolted directly into my flight path.

There was a terrifying split-second where I thought we were all going to crash into each other. I had to decide instantly: pull up sharply, stall, and risk dropping straight backward—a dangerous move, but one that would protect her—or catch the wind and try to soar right over her head. I chose the latter. By the time I reached her, she had regained control of her horse. She was leaning flat against the animal's neck, and she glanced up just as I swept over her, wings fully spread, every nerve in my body screaming with tension.

I landed soon after and hurried back to where her horse was standing, still shaking with fear.

We didn't say hello. She slid right out of her saddle and into my arms, and for a split second, I held her tight.

"Those massive wings," she whispered. That was all she said.

She went limp in my arms, and for a moment I thought she had fainted.

"That was nearly a fatal accident," Cothope said as he jogged up. He looked at us holding each other with clear disapproval and took the horse by the bridle. "It is incredibly dangerous to cut across our path like that."

Beatrice pulled away from me, stood trembling for a second, and then sank down onto the grass. "I just need to sit for a minute," she said.

"Oh!" she gasped.

She buried her face in her hands. Cothope stared at her, looking somewhere between suspicious and annoyed.

Nobody moved for a long moment. Finally, Cothope muttered that he should probably go get her some water.

As for me, the near-miss, the brief physical contact, and the rush of adrenaline had sparked a shocking new thought in my head: I had to make love to Beatrice and possess her. I have no idea why that specific thought hit me right then, but it did. I don't think I had ever viewed our relationship in that light before. But suddenly, passion just arrived. She was crouched on the ground, I was standing over her, and neither of us spoke. But it felt as loud and clear as a voice shouting from the sky.

Cothope had taken maybe twenty steps when she lowered her hands. "I don't want any water," she said. "Call him back."

VI

After that day, the entire dynamic between us shifted. The easy friendship was gone. She visited less often, and when she did come, she always brought someone with her—usually old Lord Carnaby, who did most of the talking. She was gone for the entire month of September. Whenever we did happen to be alone, there was a strange, heavy awkwardness. We were both just swirling clouds of unspoken feelings; our thoughts felt too massive and overwhelming to put into words.

Then came the crash of Lord Roberts Alpha. I woke up with my face wrapped in bandages, lying in a bedroom at the Bedley Corner dower house. Beatrice was bossing around an incompetent nurse, Lady Osprey was hovering in the background looking deeply flushed and scandalized, and my aunt was jealously trying to take charge.

My injuries looked much worse than they actually were. I could have easily been moved home to Lady Grove the very next day, but Beatrice flat-out refused to allow it. She kept me at Bedley Corner for three full days. On the afternoon of the second day, she suddenly became extremely concerned about the nurse getting some fresh air. She sent the poor woman out into the pouring rain for an hour just so she could sit alone by my bed.

I asked her to marry me.

I have to admit, it was hardly the perfect setting for a romantic speech. I was flat on my back, talking through thick bandages, and struggling a bit because my mouth and tongue were swollen. But I was in pain, running a fever, and the emotional suspense I had been carrying around for so long had finally boiled over into unbearable impatience.

"Are you comfortable?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Shall I read to you?"

"No. I want to talk."

"You shouldn't. I'd better do the talking."

"No," I said. "I want to talk to you."

She stepped up to the bed and looked me right in the eyes. "I don't—I don't want you to talk to me," she said nervously. "I thought you couldn't speak."

"I hardly ever get a chance to be alone with you."

"You really shouldn't talk. Don't say anything right now. Just let me chatter. You need to rest your mouth."

"I don't have much to say," I insisted.

"I'd really rather you didn't."

"I'm not going to be permanently disfigured," I said. "It'll just leave a scar."

"Oh!" she said, looking surprised, as if she had expected me to say something completely different. "Did you think I was worried you were going to turn into a gargoyle?"

"I thought I might look like the 'Man Who Laughs'! I had no idea how bad it was. But it's fine now. Those flowers are lovely!"

"They're Michaelmas daisies," she said. "I'm very glad your face isn't ruined. And those over there are perennial sunflowers. Do you seriously not know any flower names? When I..."

"When I saw you on the ground, I honestly thought you were dead. By all normal rules, you should have been."

She went on to say a few other things, but I was already focused on my next move.

"Are we social equals?" I asked abruptly.

She stared at me. "That's a weird question," she replied.

"But are we?"

"Hmm. Hard to say. But why do you ask? Is the daughter of a courtesy Baron who died—supposedly in disgrace—before his own father...? I give up. Does it even matter?"

"No. My mind is just confused. I want to know if you will marry me."

She went completely pale and said nothing. Suddenly, I felt an overwhelming need to plead with her. "Damn these bandages!" I burst out in useless, feverish anger.

She instantly slipped back into her role as my nurse. "What are you doing? Why are you trying to sit up? Sit down! Don't touch those bandages. I told you not to talk."

She stood there looking helpless for a second, then firmly grabbed my shoulders and pushed me back down onto the pillows. She caught my wrist just as I reached for my face.

"I told you not to talk," she whispered, leaning in close. "I asked you not to talk. Why couldn't you just do what I asked?"

"You've been avoiding me for a month," I said.

"I know. You should have figured it out. Put your hand back—down by your side."

I obeyed. She sat on the edge of the bed. Her cheeks had flushed pink, and her eyes were shining intensely. "I asked you," she repeated, "not to talk."

My eyes questioned her silently.

She rested her hand lightly on my chest. Her eyes were full of anguish.

"How can I possibly answer you now?" she said.

"How can I say anything right now?"

"What do you mean?" I asked.

She didn't answer.

"Do you mean the answer has to be 'No'?"

She nodded.

"But," I started, my whole being suddenly filled with accusation.

"I know," she said. "I can't explain. I just can't. But it has to be 'No!' It just can't happen. It's completely, finally, and forever impossible.... Keep your hands still!"

"But," I insisted, "when we met again—"

"I can't marry. I can't, and I won't."

She stood up. "Why did you have to talk?" she cried out. "Couldn't you *see*?"

It seemed like she was struggling with something impossible to put into words.

She walked over to my bedside table and roughly rearranged the Michaelmas daisies. "Why did you have to speak like that?" she asked in a deeply bitter tone. "To start things like that!"

"But what is it?" I asked. "Is it some external circumstance—like my social position?"

"Oh, *damn* your social position!" she snapped.

She walked to the far window and stood staring out at the rain. We sat in total silence for a long time. Small gusts of wind and rain rattled against the glass. Suddenly, she turned back to me.

"You didn't ask me if I loved you," she pointed out.

"Oh, if it's *that*!" I scoffed.

"It's not that," she said. "But if you really want to know—" She hesitated.

"I do love you," she said.

We just stared at each other.

"I do—with all my heart, if you want to know."

"Then why the hell—?" I demanded.

She didn't reply. Instead, she walked across the room to the piano and started playing—rather loudly and rapidly, with strange bursts of intensity—the shepherd's pipe melody from the final act of *Tristan and Isolde*. Soon she missed a note, made another mistake, dragged her fingers heavily up the keys, and finally slammed her fist down on the high notes with a weak crash. She jumped up and practically ran out of the room....

The nurse eventually found me still wearing my bandage helmet, half-dressed, and stumbling around the room trying to find the rest of my clothes. I was in a state of furious, desperate hunger for Beatrice, and I was too exhausted and feverish to even try to hide my feelings. I was weakly raging about how annoying it was to get dressed, especially struggling with my trousers since I couldn't see my legs. I was staggering around, and at one point I tripped over a chair and knocked over the vase of Michaelmas daisies.

I must have looked absolutely pathetic. "I'll go back to bed," I told her, "if I can just have one word with Miss Beatrice. I have something to say to her. That's the only reason I'm getting dressed."

I eventually got my way, but it took forever. I'm not sure if the whole house heard my ultimatum, or if the nurse just went straight to Beatrice. If it was the former, I can't even imagine what Lady Osprey thought of the whole scene.

Finally, Beatrice came back and stood beside my bed. "Well?" she said.

"All I want to say," I whined, sounding exactly like a misunderstood child, "is that I refuse to accept this as your final answer. I want to see you and talk to you when I'm better. I want to write to you. I can't do any of that now. I'm too weak to argue."

I was suddenly overwhelmed with self-pity and started sniffling. "I can't rest. Can't you see that? I can't do anything."

She sat back down beside me and spoke very softly. "I promise I'll talk everything over with you again. When you're better. I promise I'll meet you somewhere so we can really talk. You just can't talk right now."

"I asked you not to talk. Everything you want to know, I'll tell you... Will that be enough?"

"I just want to know—"

She glanced around to make sure the door was shut, then stood up and walked over to double-check it.

Then she crouched down next to me and started whispering very softly and quickly, her face close to mine.

"My love," she said. "I love you. If marrying me will make you happy, I will marry you. I was just in a mood a minute ago—a stupid, selfish mood. Of course I'll marry you. You are my prince, my king. Women are so moody—otherwise I would have acted differently. We say 'No' when we mean 'Yes,' and we throw tantrums over nothing. So now... Yes, yes, yes. I will. I can't even kiss you right now. Give me your hand so I can kiss that. You need to understand: I am yours. Do you get it? I am yours, just as if we had been married for fifty years. I am your wife, Beatrice. Is that enough? Now—will you finally get some rest?"

"Yes," I said. "But why all this?"

"There are complications. Difficulties. Once you're better, you'll be able to... understand them. But for now, they don't matter. You just need to know that this has to be a secret—for a while. An absolute secret between just the two of us. Can you promise me that?"

"Yes," I replied. "I understand. I wish I could kiss you."

She laid her head down next to mine on the pillow for a second, and then she kissed my hand.

"I don't care what the difficulties are," I said, and closed my eyes.

VII

But I was only just beginning to realize how unpredictable Beatrice could be. For an entire week after I went back to Lady Grove, I didn't hear a single word from her. Then, she finally showed up with Lady Osprey, carrying a massive bouquet of perennial sunflowers and Michaelmas daisies. "Just the same old flowers you had in your room," my aunt pointed out, eyeing me suspiciously. I didn't get a chance to speak with Beatrice alone, and she used the visit to announce that she was going to London for an unspecified number of weeks. I couldn't even get her to promise to write. When she finally did, it was a short, confusing, purely friendly note that didn't mention a single word about what was really going on between us.

I wrote her back a love letter—the first love letter I had ever written—and she completely ignored it for eight days. Then I got a scribbled note: "I'm no good at writing letters. Just wait until we can talk in person. Are you feeling any better?"

I think readers would find it pretty funny if they could see the mess on my desk right now as I try to write all this down—the crumpled pages, the crossed-out notes, the different drafts spread out everywhere, the absolute battlefield of my own thoughts. Writing about my relationship with Beatrice is easily the hardest part of this whole story. I'm naturally a very objective, logical person, and I tend to forget my own moods. But this entire situation was driven purely by moods. And even the emotions I do remember are incredibly hard to put into words. To me, it's about as difficult as trying to describe a specific taste or smell.

The actual events are made up of tiny little details that are hard to put in the right order. And hysterical, passionate love—sometimes soaring, sometimes crashing, sometimes purely spiritual, and sometimes intensely physical—is almost impossible to capture. No one has ever been brave enough to write a completely honest love story, showing all its ups and downs, its comings and goings, its shameful moments, and even its moments of hate. The love stories we read only ever give us the final result, the overall impression....

How can I possibly explain the mystical aura Beatrice had for me back then? My intense craving for her; that overwhelming, irrational, undefined desire? How can I explain how that deep admiration was mixed with a fiery, impatient determination to make her mine, to win her through sheer strength and courage, and to love her in some grand, heroic way? And then there were the doubts—the utter confusion when she kept changing her mind, when she refused to marry me, and when she seemed to actively avoid me even after she finally came back to Bedley Corner.

It drove me crazy and confused me beyond belief.

I felt like I was being betrayed. I tried to come up with every possible explanation, and my highest, most romantic faith in her didn't just alternate with my darkest suspicions—they tangled together.

And into this messy knot of memories comes the figure of Lord Carnaby. He slowly steps out from the background and becomes a major factor, a powerful influence in the web that was keeping us apart. He became a rival. What were the forces pulling her away from me, when it was so incredibly obvious that she loved me? Was she actually considering marrying him? Had I accidentally ruined some long-term plan? It was obvious he didn't like me, and that my existence somehow messed up his world. She came back to Bedley Corner and fluttered around me for weeks, but I never once got to talk to her alone. Whenever she visited my sheds, Carnaby was always right there with her, watching us jealously., Why the hell couldn't she just tell him to get lost and mind his own business?, Days turned into weeks, and my anger just kept building.

All of this was tangled up with the construction of Lord Roberts Beta. I had decided to build it one night while lying awake at Bedley Corner; I had the whole thing planned out before they even took the bandages off my face. I imagined this second steerable balloon on a massive scale. It was going to be another Lord Roberts Alpha, but much bigger. It would be three times the size, large enough to carry three grown men, and it was going to be a total, undeniable triumph that proved I had conquered the air. The frame was going to be hollow, like a bird's bones, and airtight so we could pump air in or out to adjust the weight of the fuel we were carrying. I talked a big game and bragged to Cothope—who I suspected was pretty skeptical about this new design—about everything it was going to do. And the work moved forward, but very slowly. It was slow because I was so restless and unfocused. Sometimes I would run off to London, hoping for a chance encounter with Beatrice. Other times, the only thing that calmed me down was spending the whole day gliding, exhausting myself with dangerous physical risks. And now, everywhere I looked—in the newspapers, in conversations, in the very air around me—a new anxiety was starting to invade my thoughts. Something was starting to go wrong with my uncle's massive business empire. People were starting to have doubts, to ask questions. It was the first tiny tremor in his incredibly unstable foundation, the first wobble of that gigantic financial spinning top he had kept going for so long.

People came and went, and November and December slipped away. I managed to have two very frustrating meetings with Beatrice—meetings that didn't resolve a single thing.
