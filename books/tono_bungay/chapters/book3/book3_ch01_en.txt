# Chapter 1: The Hardingham Hotel, and How We Became Big People

1

Now that I am returning to the main part of my story, it might be good to describe what my uncle looked like during those successful years after he moved from trade into finance. While he was building the Tono-Bungay business, my little uncle gained quite a bit of weight. However, as his first business launch led to even more excitement, he developed stomach problems and started to look a bit flabby, losing some of his plumpness. If you don't mind me describing his most noticeable feature first, his stomach started out nice and round, but later it lost its firmness, although it stayed the same size. He always walked as if he were proud of his stomach, pushing it out as much as possible. Until the end, his movements were quick and sudden. When he walked, his short, strong legs seemed to move briskly and lightly instead of taking normal strides, and it looked like he had flexible joints everywhere instead of regular knees.

I seem to remember that his facial features became sharper over time. His nose grew more distinctive and aggressive, sticking out more into the world, and his crooked mouth seemed to become even more crooked. In my memory, he almost always has a long cigar in his mouth, sometimes pointing jauntily upward from one corner, and sometimes drooping down. That cigar was as expressive as a dog's tail, and he only took it out when he needed to speak very clearly and forcefully. He started wearing a wide black ribbon on his glasses, and he wore them increasingly crooked as time passed. His hair seemed to become stiffer with his success, though it thinned out a lot on top. He brushed it back hard over his ears, where it stuck out wildly. It always stuck out wildly above his forehead, pointing up and forward.

When Tono-Bungay became successful, he started dressing in a very sophisticated city style and almost never changed it. He liked silk top hats with wide brims, which were a bit too big for him by modern standards, and he wore them tilted at different angles. He preferred pants with bold stripes, tailored neatly. He liked his formal frock coats to be long and loose, even though they made him look shorter. He wore several expensive rings. I remember one on his left pinky finger with a large red stone carved with ancient Gnostic religious symbols. "Clever people, those Gnostics, George," he told me. "It means a lot. It brings luck!" He only ever wore a simple black mohair watch chain. In the country, he usually wore grey clothes and a large grey cloth top hat. The only exception was when he was driving his car; then he wore a brown deerstalker cap, like Sherlock Holmes, and an Eskimo-style fur suit with pants that turned into boots at the bottom. In the evenings, he wore white vests with simple gold buttons. He hated diamonds. "Flashy," he called them. "You might as well wear your income tax receipt. They're fine for the rich people in Park Lane, but they look like unsold store inventory. It's not my style. I'm a serious financier, George."

That is enough about how he looked. For a while, his appearance was very well known to everyone. At the peak of his success, he allowed several photographs and at least one pencil drawing of himself to be printed in the cheap popular newspapers.

Over those years, his voice changed from a high tenor to a flat, deep sound that I don't know enough about music to describe properly. The hissing sound he used to make when breathing in became less frequent as he grew older, but it came back when he was excited. Throughout his career, even when he became incredibly wealthy, his personal habits remained as simple as they had been back in our small town of Wimblehurst. He never hired a personal valet. Even at the height of his success, a housemaid folded his pants and brushed the dust off his shoulders when he left the house or hotel. As he got older, he became careful about what he ate for breakfast, and for a while, he talked a lot about Dr. Haig's diets and uric acid. But for other meals, he would eat almost anything. He was something of a food lover, and when he ate something he really liked, he ate it loudly and sweated on his forehead. He drank alcohol very carefully and moderately, except when the excitement of a big public dinner or a special event carried him away. Then he would drink without thinking, his face would turn red, and he would become very talkative—about everything except his business plans.

To complete this picture, you have to imagine sudden, quick bursts of movement, like jumping firecrackers. Whatever pose he was in, you knew he had just rushed into it and would soon rush away. If I were painting his portrait, I would definitely give him a background of a stormy, restless 18th-century sky. In the background, I would paint a large, modern car with a shaking engine, a secretary hurrying with papers, and a watchful driver.

That was the man who created and ran the great Tono-Bungay empire, and who went on to launch even more massive companies and promotions that amazed investors all over the world. I think I already mentioned that long before we sold Tono-Bungay stock to the public, we took over the British rights to sell certain American products. Soon after, we also took over the sales for Moggs' Domestic Soap. My uncle launched a huge "Domestic Convenience Campaign." This campaign, along with his round stomach and bold confidence, earned my uncle the nickname "Napoleon of Commerce."

2

It shows how romantic modern business can be that my uncle met young Mr. Moggs at a formal city dinner—I think it was the Bottle-makers' Company dinner. They met when both of them had already had quite a bit to drink. This young man was the grandson of the original founder of the Moggs company, and he was a perfect example of an educated, cultured, but spoiled rich heir. Just like the famous Ruskin family took young John traveling to teach him, Moggs' family had traveled with him in his youth to encourage his love of history. Because of this, the actual work of running the Moggs soap business had been handed over to his cousin and a junior partner.

Mr. Moggs was a studious and refined man. He had just decided to dedicate his life to studying the history of the Thebaid—an ancient Egyptian desert where early Christian monks lived—because it was a topic that would never remind him of soap. But suddenly, the cousin who ran the business died, and all the responsibilities fell back onto him. In the friendly atmosphere of the dinner, Moggs complained about this unwanted job that had been forced upon him. Right then and there, my uncle offered to take over the burden by forming a partnership. They even agreed on the terms—they were very drunk terms, but they were terms nonetheless.

The two men wrote each other's names and addresses on their shirt cuffs and went their separate ways, feeling like careless brothers. The next morning, neither of them remembered to rescue their shirts from the laundry before it was too late. I was helping my uncle with business that morning, and he struggled painfully to remember the man's name and details.

"He was a tall, blond guy with a face like an aquarium, George. He wore glasses and spoke with a fancy accent," my uncle said.

I was confused. "An aquarium face?"

"You know, the way fish stare at you. I'm almost certain his business was soap. And he had a name... And this deal was the most guaranteed success you've ever seen. I was sober enough to realize that much..."

Finally, with puzzled expressions, we went out and walked to Finsbury, a business district in London. We looked for a nice, well-stocked grocery store. First, we stopped at a pharmacy to get my uncle a hangover cure, and then we found the right store.

"I want," my uncle said, "half a pound of every kind of soap you have. Yes, I'll take them right now. Wait a minute, George.... Now, what is the name of *that* soap?"

After asking three times, the clerk finally said, "Moggs' Domestic."

"Right!" said my uncle. "You don't have to guess again. Come on, George, let's go find a telephone and call Moggs. Oh—what about the order? Yes, I confirm it. Send it all—send all the soap to the Bishop of London. He'll find a good use for it., He's a great man, George, does a lot of charity work,. Put it on my bill. Here is my card—Ponderevo, from Tono-Bungay."

Then we went to see Moggs. We found him lying in a luxurious bed, wearing a camel-hair jacket and drinking fine China tea. By lunchtime, we had figured out the structure of the entire deal, except for the exact numbers.

Young Moggs really opened my eyes. I had never met anyone like him before. He seemed very clean and well-educated, and he promised me that he never read newspapers and never used any kind of soap at all.

"I have delicate skin," he explained.

"Do you have any objections if we advertise your name everywhere?" my uncle asked.

"I draw the line at train stations," Moggs said. "And the cliffs on the south coast, theater programs, books that I write, poetry in general, beautiful scenery—oh!—and the *Mercure de France* literary magazine."

"We will get along just fine," my uncle said.

"As long as you don't bother me," Moggs said, lighting a cigarette, "you can make me as rich as you want."

We certainly didn't make him any poorer. His company was the first one we promoted using a detailed, made-up history. We even published illustrated magazine articles about the quirky past of the Moggs family. We created "Moggsiana" legends. Since our partner was completely uninterested in business, we were free to invent elegant histories for Moggs the First, Moggs the Second, Moggs the Third, and Moggs the Fourth. Unless you are very young, you probably remember some of those stories and our The layout of a Georgian shop window was a marvel. My uncle bought early nineteenth-century memoirs, absorbed the style, and invented stories about the first Moggs, the Duke of Wellington, King George III, and the soap dealer. Soon, we added scented and rich soaps to the original Moggs' Primrose. We created special nursery soaps supposedly used by the Duke of Kent and Queen Victoria in her childhood. We introduced polishing powders and knife cleaners. We partnered with a decent, second-rate black-lead polish company and traced its origins back to ancient times. It was entirely my uncle's idea to link this polish to the Black Prince, the legendary English hero of the Hundred Years' War. He grew obsessed with the history of polish. I remember him cornering the president of the Pepys Society.

"Excuse me, does Samuel Pepys mention black-lead in his diaries? You know, the polish for fireplace grates! Or does he just skip over it as if it’s obvious?"

During those days, he became a nightmare for eminent historians. "I don't care about your military history of drums and trumpets," he would say. "I don't want to know who was someone's mistress, or why so-and-so destroyed a province. That's probably all lies and confusion anyway. It's not my business, and it's nobody's business now. Even the people who did it didn't know what they were doing. What I want to know is, back in the Middle Ages, did they have any cures for housemaid's knee, from scrubbing floors? What did they put in their hot baths after a jousting match? And the Black Prince—was he painted in enamel, or what? Personally, I think he was covered in black-lead polish. But did they really use blacking that long ago?"

While designing and writing those revolutionary soap advertisements, my uncle realized two things. He saw the forgotten history of everyday items, and he recognized a massive opportunity for invention among the dustpans, meat mincers, mousetraps, and carpet sweepers sold in hardware stores. This brought him back to one of his childhood dreams, the Ponderevo Patent Flat, an idea he had long before I joined him at Wimblehurst. "George," he told me, "the home needs to be straightened up. It's a silly muddle! Things just get in the way. We have to organize it."

For a time, he tackled these issues with the passion of a true social reformer.

"We have to bring the home up to date. That's my idea, George. We must take these barbaric relics and turn them into a civilized domestic machine. I'm going to track down inventors and corner the market on household ideas. Everything from string that won't tangle to glue that won't dry out. And after convenience comes beauty! Beauty, George! These basic items should look good. That was your aunt's idea. Beautiful jam jars! We'll hire modern artists to redesign all the ugly things they make now. We'll have green wood patent carpet sweepers, housemaid's toolboxes that are a joy to trip over, and richly colored cleaning cloths. Take buckets, for example. We'll hang them on the walls like decorative warming pans. All the polishes will come in tins so pretty you’ll want to cuddle them, George! Do you see the vision? We'll replace all the silly, ugly things we have now."

We had magnificent visions. They affected me so much that hardware stores began to look like trees in late winter, bursting with the promise of spring leaves and flowers. And honestly, we did a lot to bring that modern brightness to those shops. In the 1880s, they were dingy places compared to the clean, organized displays our efforts have created today.

I won't detail the complicated financial history of Moggs' Limited, which was the first evolution of Moggs and Sons. I also won't dwell on how we expanded into the broader hardware business, becoming agents for one product and partners in another. We would corner specialized manufacturers and secure raw materials, paving the way for our second public company, Domestic Utilities, known in the financial district as "Do Ut." Then came the restructuring of Tono-Bungay, followed by Household Services, and finally, the Boom!

That kind of corporate history doesn't belong in a novel. I've already explained it painfully well elsewhere, in the bankruptcy proceedings with my uncle and in my own statements after his death. Some people know the story completely; some know it entirely too well. But most people don't want the details. It's the story of an imaginative man swimming in numbers. Unless you're ready to cross-reference pounds, shillings, and pence, compare dates, and check the math, it's just confusing. And in the end, you’d find that the early numbers weren't so much fake as they were strained. 

With Moggs and Do Ut, just like with the first Tono-Bungay promotion, we left the courtroom with our reputations legally intact. The massive merger that created Household Services was my uncle's first truly large-scale enterprise and his first use of bolder tactics. To make it happen, we bought back Do Ut and Moggs, which was paying a solid seven percent dividend, and acquired Skinnerton's polishes, the Riffleshaw properties, and Runcorn's meat mincer and coffee mill business. I had very little to do with that merger. I left it to my uncle because I was deeply focused on aviation experiments, building on the work of Lilienthal, Pilcher, and the Wright brothers. I was trying to turn a glider into a powered airplane. I planned to add an engine as soon as I solved a few issues with its front-to-back stability. I had a light enough engine, modified from Bridger's turbine. But I knew that adding power to my airplane right then would be suicide, because it still tended to suddenly jerk its nose up and stall. 

But I will get to that later. My point is that I didn't realize until after the crash just how recklessly my uncle had promised dividends of over eight percent on the massively over-valued shares of Household Services.

I drifted away from business and into my research far more than either of us expected. I disliked finance much more than I disliked organizing the Tono-Bungay factory. This new corporate world was full of bluffing, gambling, and hiding important facts. These things are hateful to a scientific mind. It wasn't exactly fear; it was just a deep discomfort with the inaccuracy. I didn't see the dangers, I just hated the sloppy, loose nature of the work. Eventually, I was constantly making excuses not to visit him in London. Because of this, the later part of his business career falls outside my direct experience. I lived with him to some extent. I talked to him, advised him, and helped him entertain his Sunday crowds at Crest Hill, but I didn't follow him or guide him. From the Do Ut era onward, he shot up through the financial world like a bubble in water, leaving me behind like some busy creature in the deep.

Regardless, he was a massive success. I think the public was drawn to how familiar his products felt. They felt safe investing in companies whose names were printed on their dishcloths and shaving strops. Their loyalty was cemented by his rock-solid, pyramid-like results. After its restructuring, Tono-Bungay paid thirteen percent. Moggs paid seven, Domestic Utilities was a steady nine, and now Household Services paid eight. With a track record like that, he could buy and sell a simple bath salt or antiseptic company in three weeks and walk away with twenty thousand pounds.

I actually believe his products, like Roeburn's antiseptic, were worth the price he charged, at least before he ruined them with excessive advertising. It was a time of expansion and confidence. Money was flowing, and industrial stocks were the trend. Prices were rising everywhere. As he climbed to the unstable peak of his financial greatness, his only job was to, as he put it, "grasp the cosmic oyster, George, while it gaped." In plain English, that meant confidently buying respectable businesses at the seller's price, adding thirty or forty thousand pounds to the valuation, and selling them again. His only real challenge was tactfully managing the massive pile of shares each deal left in his hands. But I cared so little about these later deals that I never understood how dangerous that was until it was too late to help him.

III

When I think of my uncle during the Great Boom, I picture him in his suite at the Hardingham Hotel. I see him sitting at a massive old oak desk, smoking, drinking, and working in a chaotic frenzy. That was his true financial element. Our evenings, mornings, holidays, road trips, Lady Grove, and Crest Hill all belong to a completely different set of memories.

His rooms at the Hardingham were a string of suites along a thick-carpeted corridor. All the doors to the hallway were locked except the first. His bedroom, breakfast room, and private sanctuary were the hardest to reach, accessible only through a side passage that he sometimes used to escape annoying visitors. The most permanent fixture was the main waiting room, which felt very much like an office. It had a couple of uncomfortable sofas, several chairs, a green felt-covered table, and a collection of the finest Moggs and Tono-Bungay posters. The hotel's standard plush carpets had been replaced with practical grey-green linoleum. In that room, I would always find a bizarre mix of people, all watched over by his fiercely loyal and intimidating commissioner, Ropper. They guarded the door that led a step nearer to my uncle. Usually, there would be a clergyman or a widow or two; and hairy, middle-aged gentlemen wearing monocles. Some of these gentlemen looked exactly like unsuccessful versions of Edward Ponderevo, my uncle. There were also young, attractively dressed men, some with papers sticking out of their pockets, others with their papers neatly hidden. And then there were the truly surprising, dirty people you’d stumble upon by chance.

All these people kept up a hopeless siege. Sometimes they waited for weeks on end; they really should have just stayed home. The next room was filled with people who actually had an appointment. Here you would find smartly dressed, sophisticated people: nervous women hiding behind magazines, traditional clergymen and modern religious leaders, and real businessmen. These businessmen, mostly gentlemen in excellent morning suits, would stand proudly and judge my uncle's taste in watercolor paintings, sometimes staring at them for hours. There were also different kinds of young men here: Americans, clerks who had betrayed other companies, and university graduates from Oxford or Cambridge. Most of them looked sharp, determined, and secretive, but they were tense—like a hair-trigger ready to fire—prepared to unleash a flood of persuasive words at any moment.

This room also had a window looking out into the Hardingham Hotel courtyard, with its fern-covered fountains and mosaic floors. The young men would stand by this window and sometimes mutter to themselves. One day, as I walked by, I heard a man repeating in an urgent whisper, "But Mr. Ponderevo, you don't quite see the full advantages, the *full* advantages—" I caught his eye, and he was deeply embarrassed.

Next came a room with a couple of secretaries—without typewriters, because my uncle hated the clattering noise—and a few people sitting around waiting for their projects to be reviewed. Here, and in the inner room closer to the private apartments, my uncle's mail was thoroughly sorted and filtered before it reached him. Finally, there were the two small rooms where my uncle held his meetings. There sat my magic uncle, the man who had captivated the investing public and made everything seem possible. When we walked in, we would usually find him sitting with a cigar in his hand, a look of doubtful bliss on his face, while someone tried to convince him how to get even richer.

"Is that you, George?" he would say. "Come in. Here's something interesting. Tell him—Mister—explain it again. Want a drink, George? No? Smart boy! Listen to this."

I was always ready to listen. All sorts of financial wonders poured out of the Hardingham Hotel, especially during my uncle's final massive stock market manipulation, but they were nothing compared to the wild projects that came in. The room he usually sat in was small, decorated in brown and gold. He had hired Bordingly, a famous interior decorator, to redo it, and hung half a dozen peaceful Sussex countryside paintings by Webster on the walls. Lately, he had started wearing a golden-brown velvet jacket in this room, which I thought made his artistic pretensions too obvious. He also added a few large, crude Chinese bronze statues, which were considered status symbols of wealth.

Throughout that crazy, ambitious time, he was generally a very happy man. He made—and, as I will explain later, spent—massive amounts of money. He was constantly in intense motion, constantly stimulated both mentally and physically, and rarely tired. There was an atmosphere of immense respect around him, and most of his waking hours, as well as his dreams, were filled with a sense of triumph. I doubt he felt any dissatisfaction with himself at all until the crash finally crushed him. Everything must have moved incredibly fast for him. I really think he must have been happy.

As I sit here writing about all these things, jotting down notes and tossing them aside as I try to shape the story of our business promotions, the sheer wonder and ultimate absurdity of it all hits me just as it did the first time. At the peak of his boom, my uncle must have practically owned about two million pounds’ worth of property and credit to offset his massive, vague debts. And from start to finish, he must have controlled the flow of nearly thirty million pounds.

This irrational, chaotic society we live in gave him all that. It paid him that massive amount just to sit in a room, plot, and tell lies. Because he created nothing, he invented nothing, and he saved nothing. I cannot claim that a single one of the massive businesses we built added any real value to human life. Several of them, like our fake cure-all Tono-Bungay, were absolute frauds by any honest standard—just empty products wrapped in advertising to make money. And, I repeat, the things we sent out from the Hardingham were nothing compared to the things that came in. I think of the long line of people who sat before us proposing this and that. One time it was a scheme to sell bread under a fancy name to dodge weight regulations—this was later launched as the Decorticated Health-Bread Company, which eventually clashed with the law. Another time it was a new plan for even louder advertising; then a story about an undiscovered mineral deposit; then a cheap, nasty substitute for a basic necessity; and then a treacherous employee who knew too much and wanted to become our partner. All these ideas were pitched to us carefully or persuasively. Sometimes a large, red-faced, boastful man would try to sweep us away with fake boyish honesty. Other times it was a sickly-looking, whispering man, or an intensely serious, specially dressed young man wearing a monocle with a flower in his lapel. Sometimes it was a sharp, plain-spoken man from Manchester, or a Scotsman eager to explain everything in exhausting detail.

Many came in pairs or threes, often bringing a lawyer to explain the details. Some were pale and intense, while others were overly flustered by the opportunity. Some of them literally begged and prayed for us to fund them. My uncle picked what he wanted and ignored the rest. He became very dictatorial with these applicants.

He felt he could make them successful, and they felt it too. If he just said "No!", their chances vanished. He had become a kind of whirlpool that naturally sucked in wealth. His possessions piled up: his stocks, his leased properties, his mortgages, and his corporate bonds.

Behind his main businesses, my uncle finally found it necessary—and legally approved by all authorities—to set up three general trading companies: the London and African Investment Company, the British Traders’ Loan Company, and Business Organisations Limited. This happened during the peak of the boom, when I was least involved in the business. I don't say that to make excuses; I admit I was a director of all three, and I confess I deliberately chose not to ask questions. At the end of each financial year, these companies stayed afloat by selling massive amounts of shares to each other and using that money to pay dividends. I sat at the boardroom table and agreed to it. That was how we kept our balance at the shining climax of the bubble.

But now you understand the nature of the services for which this crazy society gave him unmanageable wealth, power, and real respect. It was a monstrous payment for bold fiction, a massive reward for providing the one true reality of human life: illusion. We gave them a feeling of hope and profit; we sent a massive wave of confidence into their stranded businesses. "We are minting faith, George," my uncle said one day. "That's what we do. And my God, we have to keep minting it! We've been manufacturing human confidence ever since I popped the first cork of Tono-Bungay."

"Forging" would have been a better word than "minting"! And yet, in a way, he was right. Civilization is only possible through trust, which is why we can put our money in a bank and walk the streets without weapons. The money in a bank vault, or a single policeman keeping order in a restless crowd, are just slightly less arrogant bluffs than my uncle's business proposals. If people actually demanded a quarter of what banks or police guarantee, they would fail instantly. This entire modern commercial civilization is made of the same stuff as dreams. A massive crowd sweats and works, great railway systems grow, cities rise to the sky and spread far and wide, mines are opened, factories hum, foundries roar, ships cross the seas, and countries are settled. And moving through this busy, struggling world are the rich owners, controlling everything, enjoying everything, confident, and creating the trust that pulls us all together into a reluctant, almost unconscious brotherhood. I sit in wonder and design my engines. Flags wave, crowds cheer, and governments meet. Yet it sometimes seems to me that this whole modern commercial civilization is nothing more than my poor uncle's career blown up to a massive scale: an expanding, thinning bubble of false promises. I fear its math is just as broken, its profits just as reckless, and its final goal just as vague and forgotten—and that it might all be drifting toward a massive disaster just like his own.

And so we boomed, and for four and a half years we lived a life made of solid wealth and pure fantasy. Until our own reckless foundation caught up with us, we drove the grandest cars down real highways, showed ourselves off proudly in magnificent mansions, ate lavishly, and had a constant stream of cash flowing into our pockets. Hundreds of thousands of men and women respected us, saluted us, and gave us their hard work and honor. I asked, and my factories were built; my airplanes soared out of nowhere to scare the birds in the hills. My uncle waved his hand, and Lady Grove—a grand country estate with all its history of knights and ancient peace—was his. He waved again, and architects scrambled to design Crest Hill, the massive palace he never finished. An army of workers gathered at his command; blue marble arrived from Canada, and timber from New Zealand. But underneath it all, as you know, there was nothing but fake value, as fleeting as a pot of gold at the end of a rainbow.

IV

Every now and then, I walk past the Hardingham Hotel. I glance through the great archway at the fountain and the ferns, and I think of those faded days when I was so close to the center of our whirlpool of greed and ambition. I see my uncle’s pale, focused face again, and I hear him talking. I hear him making what he thought were Napoleonic decisions: telling us to "grasp the nettle" and face the danger, to put his "finger on the spot," to "bluff," or to say "snap" and grab the deal. He became particularly addicted to that last phrase. Toward the end, every possible action became a "snap." It was a way of saying "snap!"

And oh, the strange outsiders who came to us! Among them was Gordon-Nasmyth. He was a bizarre mix of romantic dreamer and lawbreaker. Later, he would pull me into the most absurd adventure of my life—the Mordet Island affair—and leave me with blood on my hands, as people say. It is remarkable how little my conscience bothers me over the life I took, yet how much it still sparks my imagination. The story of Mordet Island was told in a British government report, but they got it all wrong. Even though there are still good reasons to leave parts of that official record uncorrected, I cannot simply leave the incident out of my story, no matter what my common sense tells me.

I still vividly remember Gordon-Nasmyth walking into the private back room of our office. He was a skinny, sun-baked man wearing a tweed suit. He had a sharp, tanned face like a hatchet and one faded blue eye—the other eye was sunken and closed shut. With a stiff, fake relaxed manner, he told us an unbelievable story. He spoke of a massive pile of a substance called "quap," lying abandoned behind the beach of Mordet Island, hidden among dead white mangrove trees and salty, sticky mud.

"What exactly is quap?" my uncle asked, after hearing the word for the fourth time.

"The locals call it quap, or quab, or quabb," Gordon-Nasmyth replied. "But we were not friendly enough with the natives to get the exact pronunciation....

But the stuff is just sitting there, waiting to be taken. They don’t know about it. Nobody knows. I went down to that cursed place all alone in a canoe. My workers refused to come with me. I just pretended I was collecting plant samples."

Right from the start, Gordon-Nasmyth was quite theatrical.

"Look here," he said when he first came in, carefully closing the door behind him. "Are you two men willing to put up six thousand pounds—a fortune—for a guaranteed chance to make a fifteen-hundred percent profit in just one year? Yes or no?"

"We get offers like that all the time," my uncle said. He chewed his cigar, wiped his glasses, and leaned back in his chair, unimpressed. "We prefer a safe twenty percent."

Gordon-Nasmyth had a quick temper, and his body grew a little stiff.

"Don't listen to my uncle," I said, standing up before he could argue. "You are different, and I’ve read the books you've written. We are very glad you came to us. Oh, come on, Uncle! This is Mr. Gordon-Nasmyth! Please sit down. What is this stuff? Some kind of mineral?"

"Quap," Gordon-Nasmyth said, staring right at me. "Piles of it."

"Piles," my uncle repeated softly, looking at him sideways through his glasses.

"You really belong in a grocery store," Gordon-Nasmyth said with disgust. He sat down and helped himself to one of my uncle's cigars. "I regret coming here. But since I am already here... First, let me explain quap. Quap, sir, is the most radioactive material in the world! That’s what it is. It is a bubbling mass of rare earth dirt, heavy metals, polonium, radium, thorium, and completely unknown elements. There’s something inside it we temporarily call Xk. It is all mixed together in a kind of rotting sand. I have no idea exactly what it is or how it formed. It looks like a young God was playing around in the dirt there. It sits in two heaps—one small, one massive. The land for miles around it is scorched and dead, like a bomb hit it. You can have it if you just go get it. All you have to do is take it!"

"That sounds interesting," I said. "Do you have any samples?"

"Well—should I? You can have any amount, up to two ounces."

"Where is this place?"

His blue eye smiled as he studied me. He puffed on his cigar and gave vague answers for a while, dodging my questions. Then, slowly, his story started to come together. He painted a magical picture of a forgotten, twisted corner of the world's coast. He described winding water channels spreading out, dumping mud and dirt just inside the loud, crashing waves of the Atlantic Ocean. He talked about thick, tangled jungle vines crawling into the shimmering water. He made us feel the extreme heat and smell the endless rot of dead plants. Finally, he said, there is an open clearing in the jungle. It is a round space surrounded by bone-white dead trees. You can see the hard blue line of the ocean past the bright waves, and a wide, empty stretch of bleached, dirty gravel and mud. A little further away, standing among burnt weeds, is an abandoned dock. It was abandoned because anyone who stayed there for more than two months died a mysterious, rotting death, like a leper. The dock still has broken sheds and worm-eaten wooden posts leaning over the water. It looks dangerous, but you could still use it.

And right in the middle, sticking out from under a rocky ridge, are two huge heaps shaped like the backs of pigs. One small, one large. That is the quap!

"There it is," said Gordon-Nasmyth. "It is worth at least three pounds an ounce, if it's worth anything. Two giant piles of soft, rotting dirt. You just shovel it into a wheelbarrow. You could take it away by the ton!"

"How did it get there?"

"God only knows! It’s just sitting there for the taking! In a country where trading is illegal. In a place where a greedy corporation waits for kind people to find riches, just so they can steal it from them. But there it is—completely abandoned."

"Can't you make a deal with someone?"

"They are too stupid for that. You just have to go in and take it. That’s all."

"They might catch you."

"They might, of course. But they aren't very good at catching people."

We talked about the details of that problem. "They wouldn't catch me, because I would just sink my boat first. Just give me a yacht," Gordon-Nasmyth said. "That is all I need."

"But if you do get caught," my uncle added.

I think Gordon-Nasmyth really believed we would write him a check for six thousand pounds right then, just based on his exciting story. It was a great story, but we didn't give him the money. I insisted he give us samples of the material so we could analyze it, and he reluctantly agreed.

Honestly, I think he was hoping I wouldn't test the samples. He reached toward his pocket, which made us totally sure he had the quap on him, but at the last second, he decided it was too soon to show it.

He clearly loved being secretive. He didn't want to hand over the samples, and he refused to tell us where Mordet Island was within a three-hundred-mile radius. He knew he had a hugely valuable secret, but he had no idea how much he should share with actual businessmen. So, to stall for time, he started talking about other things. He was a fantastic speaker. He talked about the Dutch East Indies and the Congo, about Portuguese East Africa and Paraguay. He spoke of Malays, rich Chinese merchants, African tribes, and the spread of Islam in modern Africa. All the while, he was watching us, trying to decide if we were smart and brave enough to trust with his adventure. As he painted pictures of dark betrayals in exotic ports, illegal trades, strange customs, unpunished murders, and strange mixings of people in unmapped rivers, our cozy little office suddenly felt very small. All our daily business deals seemed cold and dead compared to his wild life.

My uncle and I had never traveled abroad, except for a few cheap tourist trips to Paris. Our entire world was England. The foreign countries where our raw materials came from felt as distant and imaginary to us as fairyland. But Gordon-Nasmyth made those places feel so real and close to us that afternoon—at least to me. It felt like remembering a dream I had long forgotten.

Finally, he pulled out his sample. It was a little lump of brown-speckled mud inside a glass bottle. The bottle was wrapped in lead and red flannel cloth—red flannel, which people back then believed magically doubled the fabric's protective powers.

"Don't carry this in your pocket," Gordon-Nasmyth warned. "It will burn sores into your skin."

I took the material to a scientist named Thorold. Thorold went through the agonizing but thrilling process of discovering two brand-new elements during his top-secret tests. He later named them and published his findings. But at the time, Gordon-Nasmyth flew into a violent rage and screamed at me for showing the dirt to Thorold. He absolutely refused to let us publish anything about it. "I thought you were going to test it yourself!" he yelled. Like many normal people, he foolishly believed that a scientist knew everything about every single branch of science.

I did some business research, and it turned out Gordon-Nasmyth was right about how much the dirt was worth. This was before the invention of the Capern filament, but the cerium and thorium inside the quap were incredibly valuable for making gas lanterns, which were very popular then. However, there were questions. Actually, there were a lot of doubts. How big was the market for gas lanterns? How much thorium could the factories actually buy at once? If the supply was too large, what would happen? By the time we had enough reason to launch a ship, doubts arose from another quarter. Were the heaps of minerals really worth as much as the sample? Were they as massive as Gordon-Nasmyth claimed? Was it possible that his imagination had exaggerated everything? And even if the value was real, could we actually get our hands on the material? It did not belong to us. Besides, it was located in a restricted, forbidden area. As you can see, all sorts and levels of suspicions lurked along the path of this adventure.

Nevertheless, we continued to discuss his plan quite deeply, though I think we may have tested his patience. Then one day, he suddenly vanished from London, and I did not see him again for a year and a half.

My uncle said he had expected as much. And when Gordon-Nasmyth finally reappeared and casually mentioned that he had been to Paraguay on personal affairs—which we guessed involved a romantic entanglement—the "quap" expedition project had to be started all over again from the beginning. My uncle showed a completely skeptical attitude, but I was not so resolute. Perhaps I was drawn to the vividly picturesque aspects of the plan. But neither of us even thought about seriously undertaking the project until Capern's discovery.

Nasmyth's story had captivated my imagination, much like a small, intense painting of tropical sunshine hung on a wall filled with gray, mundane business affairs. I kept the story alive during Gordon-Nasmyth's intermittent appearances in England. Occasionally, he and I would meet and reinforce the impact of the story. We would have lunch together in London, or he would come to see my gliders at Crest Hill. Sometimes together, sometimes on his own, we would hatch new plans to approach those mineral heaps again.

At times, it felt like a kind of fairy tale or an exercise of imagination for us. Then, Capern discovered what he called the "ideal filament," which greatly reduced the uncertainty surrounding the business side of quap. Creating that ideal filament required 5 percent canadium. At the time, canadium was only known to the world as a newly isolated component from a rare mineral variety called rutile. But to Thorold, it was better known as an element in the mysterious sample I had brought him, and to me, it was known as one of the components of quap. I informed my uncle of this fact, and we immediately jumped into the operation. We learned that Gordon-Nasmyth, completely unaware of the material's changed value and still thinking only of the experimental price of radium and the rarity value of cerium, had brought in a cousin named Pollack. He was making a bizarre transaction involving his life insurance policy and was purchasing a two-masted sailing ship, or brig. We stepped in, invested three thousand pounds, and instantly, the life insurance deal and the financial scheme involving Pollack vanished into thin air. Regrettably, Pollack was left behind like dregs in that sailing ship and the secret—at least as far as canadium and the filament were concerned. We seriously debated whether to charter a steamboat or continue using the sailing ship, but we ultimately decided on the sailing ship as a less conspicuous means for what was, frankly, nothing more than a thieving enterprise.

However, that was one of our final ventures before our massive crisis struck, and I will tell that story in detail when the time comes.

This was how quap entered our business—starting as a fairy tale and turning into reality. It took on an increasingly realistic shape until it finally became completely real. At last, I saw with my own eyes the heaps I had imagined for so long. Between my fingers, I once again felt the half-rough, half-soft texture of quap. It felt like brown sugar mixed with sand and mud, with something seemingly squirming inside it.

You have to feel it yourself to understand.

V

All sorts of business opportunities flooded into Hardingham and were proposed to my uncle. Gordon-Nasmyth merely stood out because he eventually played a role in the crisis of our destiny. So many offers came our way that, at times, it seemed as if the entire human world was ready to sell itself to our real or imagined millions. Looking back now, I am still dazzled and in disbelief at the sheer scale of the opportunities we were given.

We engaged in the most extraordinary endeavors; it seemed absurd to me to leave such things to any ordinary person with wealth and a sense of adventure who might be willing to do them. I gained a startling insight into how capital can control modern thought and the facts presented to the public mind. Among the various ventures my uncle proposed, he tried very hard to acquire the *British Medical Journal* and the *Lancet*, aiming to run them in what he called a modern way. When they refused him, he spoke very forcefully for a while about organizing a rival enterprise. In its own way, it was a magnificent idea. It would have provided a massive advantage in handling numerous medical specialties, and it is hard to even guess how tightly it would have placed the medical field in our grasp. The fact that such a thing is possible in this modern nation still astounds me—and I will remain astounded until the day I die. Even though my uncle failed to accomplish it, someone else might succeed. But even if he had acquired both weekly journals, I doubt his peculiar style would have suited them. The shift in purpose would have been obvious, and he would have struggled to maintain their dignity.

One day, my uncle snapped up an important critical magazine called *The Sacred Grove* for 800 pounds. He certainly failed to maintain its dignity. He bought it "lock, stock, and barrel"—meaning completely, including the editor. Even at that price, it was not profitable. If you are a literary person, you will remember the bright new cover he gave to that representative journal of British intellectual culture. You will also remember how harshly his sound business instincts clashed with the lofty pretensions of a fading era. One old cover I recently found reads like this:

"THE SACRED GROVE"

A Weekly Magazine of Art, Philosophy, Science, and Belles Lettres.

DO YOU HAVE A BAD TASTE IN YOUR MOUTH?
IT IS YOUR LIVER.

YOU NEED A 1-2-3 PILL., JUST ONE WILL DO.,
NOT A DRUG, BUT A LIVING AMERICAN REMEDY.

THE WORLD'S BEST PILL FOR AN IRREGULAR LIVER

The reason this combination of literature and pills feels so mismatched is probably due to the lingering attachment I have to the traditions of the Bladesover estate. Furthermore, the idea that a country which leaves its medical or literary criticism—or indeed any vitally important criticism—entirely in the hands of private enterprise, completely vulnerable to the whims of any buyer, must be in a hopeless state, stems from my ineradicable boyish imagination and traces of Plutarch's *Lives*. These are my own ideal concepts.

In truth, nothing illustrates the relationship between learning, thought, and the economic situation of the modern world more naturally and representatively than this cover of *The Sacred Grove*. The quiet conservatism of one element was buried under the aggressive flashiness of the other—a striking contrast between bold physiological experimentation and extreme mental stagnation.

VI

Among my memories at Hardingham, an impression of a drizzling November day also comes to mind, when we looked out the window at a procession of London's unemployed.

It was like peering down a well and catching a brief glimpse of the underworld. Thousands of destitute and helpless men had gathered to drag their lifeless misery across the West End—London's wealthy district. Their plea was, in its own way, a fragile and insubstantial threat: "We need work, not charity."

Half-phantom-like in the fog, there they were—an endless, silent, gray procession shuffling their feet. They carried wet, dirty banners and shook collection boxes for pennies. These were the men who had failed to grab an opportunity at the right moment, the men who had pounced too eagerly, the men who had never grabbed one at all, and the men who had never even had a chance to try. They formed a stumbling, shameful stream that oozed along the street—the gutter waste produced by a competitive society. And we stood high above it all, like gods from another world, in a beautifully lit, properly heated room filled with expensive furniture.

"There," I thought, "but for the grace of God, walk George and Edward Ponderevo."

My uncle's thoughts, however, flowed in an entirely different direction. He used that scene as a testing ground for a lively but inconclusive speech on Tariff Reform.
