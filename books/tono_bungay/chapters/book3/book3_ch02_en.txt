CHAPTER THE SECOND
OUR PROGRESS FROM CAMDEN TOWN TO CREST HILL

I
So far, my account of my aunt and uncle has focused mainly on his industrial and financial exploits. But alongside that history of expanding a tiny venture into a massive empire, there was another progression: the year-by-year change from their shabby, impoverished lodging in the working-class area of Camden Town to the lavish wealth of their Crest Hill mansion, complete with a grand marble staircase and my aunt's golden bed, which was an exact replica of one from the French Palace of Fontainebleau. Strangely enough, as I approach this more recent part of my story, I find it much harder to tell than the clear, distinct memories of those early days. The impressions crowd and overlap one another. Around that time, I was soon to fall in love again, seized by a passion to which I still faintly respond and which still clouds my mind. I traveled back and forth between Ealing, in London's western suburbs, and where my aunt and uncle lived; and soon between my wife Effie and the exclusive gentlemen's clubs of London; and then between my business duties and a life of scientific research. That research became far more continuous and memorable than any of those other experiences. As a result, I didn't witness their social ascent as a steady, regular progression. From my perspective, my aunt and uncle seemed to move up in the world with the jerky, flickering jumps of an early cinema projector.

When I recall this side of our life, the image of my round-eyed, button-nosed, pink-and-white Aunt Susan always takes the center stage. While we drove and maintained the motorcar, she sat in it wearing a magnificent array of hats balanced on her delicate neck. And always, with that faint lisp that no misspelling can capture, she commented on and explained our new surroundings.

I have already briefly described the little home behind the Wimblehurst pharmacy, the lodging near the statue of the radical politician Richard Cobden, and the apartments in central London on Gower Street. From there, my aunt and uncle moved into a flat in Redgauntlet Mansions. They were living there when I got married. It was a compact flat with very little housework for a woman to do. In those days, I think my aunt found she had too much time on her hands, so she took up reading and eventually started attending afternoon lectures. I began to find unexpected books on her table: sociological works, travel books, and plays by George Bernard Shaw, the famous playwright and social critic. 

"Hullo!" I said, spotting one of Shaw's volumes.

"I am cultivating a mind, George," she explained.

"Eh?"

"Cultivating a mind. I've never cared for dogs. It was a toss-up between cultivating a mind and cultivating a soul. It is quite fortunate for him and you that I chose a mind. I've joined the London Library, the largest lending library in the country, and I'm going to attend every single lecture at the Royal Institution of science next winter. You had better watch out." ...

I remember her coming in late one evening with a notebook in her hand.

"Where have you been, Susan?" my uncle asked.

"Birkbeck College—studying physiology. I am making progress." Birkbeck was well-known as a night school for working people. She sat down and took off her gloves. "You are completely transparent to me," she sighed, and then added with a tone of serious reproach: "You old fool! I had no idea! The things you have kept hidden from me!", She was likely referring to facts about the human body she had just learned in physiology.,

Before long, they bought a house in Beckenham, a quiet suburban town in southeast London, and my aunt temporarily paused her intellectual pursuits. By the standards of the early days of our Tono-Bungay business, the Beckenham house was quite a major undertaking for them. It was a large, somewhat bare villa featuring a greenhouse, a shrubbery, a tennis lawn, a fairly large vegetable garden, and a small, unused carriage house. I caught a few glimpses of the excitement during their move-in, but not many, because of the ongoing estrangement between my aunt and my wife, Marion.

My aunt moved into that house with great enthusiasm, while my uncle distinguished himself by completely overhauling the painting and plumbing. He tore up all the drainage pipes and most of the garden with them, standing like a commander on top of dirt heaps to direct the workers and hand out whisky. I found him there one day, looking remarkably like Napoleon on a little dirt island of Elba, surrounded by an indescribable smell. I also recall that he chose what he considered cheerful color contrasts for the exterior woodwork. This exasperated my aunt extremely—she earnestly called him a "pestilential old splosher"—and he further provoked her into new fits of name-calling by naming each bedroom after a favorite historical hero, such as Clive, Napoleon, or Caesar. He even painted these names in gold letters on black labels on the doors. The name "Martin Luther," after the religious reformer, was reserved for my room. My aunt claimed that only her respect for household discipline prevented her from retaliating by labeling the housemaid's cupboard "Old Pondo," referring to an African tribe or ape.

My uncle also went out and ordered one of the most complete sets of garden tools I have ever seen—and had them all painted a harsh, bright blue. In response, my aunt bought large tins of a softer-colored enamel and secretly repainted everything. Once this was done, she found great joy in the garden, becoming a passionate grower of roses and flower borders, leaving her intellectual pursuits for damp evenings and winter months. When I think of her at Beckenham, my first memory is always of her dressed in her favorite blue cotton outfit, wearing huge gauntlet gloves, holding a trowel in one hand and a limp, very young-looking, timid little annual plant in the other.

Almost as soon as the lawn was replanted, the local community of Beckenham—represented by a clergyman, a doctor's wife, and a large, arrogant lady named Hogberry—came to formally "call" on my uncle and aunt. Afterwards, my aunt became friends with a quiet, polite gentlewoman next door over an overhanging cherry tree and the need to repair the shared fence. In this way, she regained her place in society, which she had lost after our financial disaster back in Wimblehurst. She made a somewhat joking study of the etiquette required for her new status, had calling cards printed, and returned the visits. Soon she received an invitation to one of Mrs. Hogberry's "At Home" tea parties, hosted an old-fashioned garden party of her own, participated in a charity bazaar and craft sale, and was becoming quite cheerfully entangled in Beckenham society. Then, suddenly, my uncle uprooted her once again and moved them to Chislehurst, an even wealthier and more exclusive suburb.

"The Great Trek, George," she told me concisely, "Onward and Up," when I found her supervising the loading of two large moving vans. "Go upstairs and say goodbye to 'Martin Luther,' and then we'll see how you can help me."

II
When I look into my jumbled memories of that middle period, Beckenham seems like a very brief phase. But in reality, they lived there for several years—through nearly all of my married life, in fact, which was far longer than the year or so we lived together in Wimblehurst. Yet the time in Wimblehurst is far richer in my memory than the Beckenham period. I can still vividly recall the atmosphere of my aunt's garden party and a small social blunder I committed that day. It feels like a fragment from another life. My memories of it are tied to a physical sensation: the uncomfortable feeling of wearing poorly tailored city clothes—a frock coat, grey trousers, and a high, stiff collar and tie—while standing among flowers in the hot sunshine. I still have a very clear memory of the little trapezoid-shaped lawn and the people gathered there, especially the women's hats and feathers, the parlor maid with the blue teacups, and the overwhelming presence of Mrs. Hogberry and her loud, resonant voice. Her voice would have been more suited to a much larger garden party; it easily carried into the neighboring yards and even reached the gardener working far away in the vegetable patch, who was technically not part of the event. The only other men present were my aunt's doctor, two pleasantly contrasting clergymen, and Mrs. Hogberry's barely grown son, a young man whose neck seemed to be bursting out of his collar. The rest were women, except for a young girl or two who sat in silent, perfect behavior. Marion was also there.

Marion and I had arrived feeling somewhat alienated from each other, and I remember her as a quiet shadow across all that sunlit, empty socializing. We had embittered each other with one of those miserable, petty arguments that seemed so unavoidable between us. With her friend Smithie's help, she had dressed quite elaborately for the occasion. When she saw me ready to go in a grey suit, she insisted that a silk top hat and a formal frock coat were absolutely necessary. I resisted, but she showed me a magazine illustration of a garden party attended by the King. Finally, I gave in—though, out of bad habit, I did so resentfully. Oh, dear! How pitiful and trivial those old arguments were! And how sad they are to recall! I think they become even sadder as I grow older, because all the small, emotional reasons for our mutual anger gradually fade from my memory.

The impression the Beckenham crowd left on me was one of modest fakery; they were all keeping up a facade of unspecified social importance while hiding their true financial realities. Most of the husbands were "in business" behind the scenes, and it would have been highly offensive to ask what that business actually was. Meanwhile, the wives spent their energy trying to recreate a moralized, suburban version of aristocratic afternoon life, taking their cues from novels and illustrated magazines. These women lacked the intellectual or moral drive of true upper-class women; they had no interest in politics, held no real opinions on anything, and consequently, I remember them being extremely difficult to talk to. They all sat around the summerhouse and in garden chairs, covered in hats, ruffles, and parasols. Three ladies and the junior clergyman played croquet—a traditional lawn game—with immense seriousness, interrupted only by the clergyman's occasional loud, fake cries of distress. "Oh! Hitting me again! Augh!"

The dominant social figure that afternoon was Mrs. Hogberry. She took a commanding position over the croquet game and rambled on, as my aunt whispered to me, "like an old roundabout." She talked about how Beckenham society was becoming too mixed, and then switched to a touching letter she had recently received from her old nurse in Little Gossdean. This was followed by a loud, boastful account of Little Gossdean and how much she and her eight sisters had been respected there. "My poor mother was practically a little Queen there," she claimed. "And the common people were so nice! People say country laborers are becoming disrespectful nowadays. That isn't true—not if they are treated properly. Of course, here in Beckenham, it is different. I refuse to call the people here 'the Poor'—they certainly aren't the proper sort of Poor. They are just the Masses. I always tell Mr. Bugshoot that they are the Masses and ought to be treated as such." ...

Vague memories of the arrogant Mrs. Mackridge floated through my mind as I listened to her.

I was spun around on this conversational roundabout for a while before I luckily fell off into a one-on-one chat with a lady my aunt introduced as Mrs. Mumble. To be fair, my aunt introduced everyone to me as "Mumble" that afternoon, either as a joke or because she genuinely forgot their names.

That must have been one of my earliest attempts at polite small talk. I recall starting by criticizing the local train service. By my third sentence, Mrs. Mumble cheerfully and encouragingly remarked that she was afraid I was a very "frivolous" person.

Looking back, I have no idea what I said that could have been considered frivolous.

I don't remember how that conversation ended, or if it ever really did. I recall talking rather awkwardly for a time with one of the clergymen, who gave me a sort of geographical history of Beckenham. He assured me over and over that it was "Quite an old place. Quite an old place," treating me with patient firmness as if I had foolishly called it a new town. Then our conversation hit a dead stop, and my aunt rescued me. "George," she whispered confidentially, "keep the pot boiling." Then she added loudly, "I say, will you both trot about with some tea for a bit?"

"Only too delighted to trot for you, Mrs. Ponderevo," said the clergyman, falling into his element with fearful expertise. "Only too delighted."

I suddenly realized we were standing near a rustic table, and the housemaid was positioned perfectly behind us to catch us on the rebound with the tea tray.

"Trot!" the clergyman repeated to me, highly amused. "Excellent expression!" I barely stopped him from backing into the maid's tray as he turned around.

We handed out tea for a while.

"Give them cakes," my aunt instructed, looking flushed but still in control. "It helps them talk, George. People always talk better after a little nourishment. It's like throwing a chunk of turf down an old geyser."

She surveyed the gathering with her commanding blue eyes and poured herself some tea.

"They keep freezing up," she muttered under her breath. "I've done my best."

"It's been a huge success," I said encouragingly.

"That boy has sat with his legs crossed in that exact position without speaking for ten minutes. He is getting stiffer and stiffer. He looks brittle. Now he's starting a dry cough—that's always a bad sign, George. Should I force them to walk around? Or rub snow on their noses?"

Thankfully, she didn't do either. I ended up talking with the gentlewoman from next door, a thoughtful, tired-looking little woman with a soft voice. Our topic was Cats and Dogs, and which one we preferred.

"I always feel," the thoughtful little woman said, "that there is something special about a dog. Something a cat just doesn't have."

"Yes," I found myself agreeing with great enthusiasm, "there definitely is something. And yet again—"

"Oh! I know there is something about a cat, too. But it isn't the same."

"Not quite the same," I admitted, "but still, it is something."

"Ah! But it's such a different kind of something!"

"More sinuous and flexible."

"Much more."

"Ever so much more."

"It makes all the difference, don't you think?"

"Yes," I said, "it makes all the difference."

She looked at me seriously and let out a long, deeply emotional sigh. "Yes."

A long pause followed.

The conversation felt like a complete stalemate. Fear and deep confusion crept into my heart.

"The—er—roses," I stammered, feeling like a drowning man grasping for a lifeline. "Those roses—don't you think they are very beautiful flowers?"

"Aren't they!" she agreed softly. "There seems to be something in roses... something... I don't know how to express it."

"Something," I offered helpfully.

"Yes," she said, "something. Isn't there?"

"So few people see it," I noted. "It's a real pity!"

She sighed and whispered very softly once more, "Yes..."

There was another long, agonizing pause. I looked at her, and she was lost in a dreamy trance. The drowning sensation returned, bringing fear and helplessness. Driven by a sudden flash of inspiration, I noticed that her teacup was empty.

"Let me take your cup," I said abruptly. Snatching it up, I made a beeline for the table by the summerhouse. At that moment, I had no intention of abandoning my aunt. But right nearby, the large French windows of the drawing room stood wide open, yawning invitingly. I can still feel the pull of that temptation, especially with the irritating provocation of my stiff collar. In an instant, I was lost. I told myself I would just go inside for a moment!

I dashed inside, slammed the cup down on the keys of the grand piano, and sprinted upstairs—silently and swiftly, taking three steps at a time—to the safe haven of my uncle's cozy study. I arrived out of breath, completely convinced there was no going back for me. I felt a mix of intense relief, shame, and desperation. Using a pocketknife, I pried open his cigar cabinet, pulled a chair to the window, stripped off my coat, collar, and tie, and sat there smoking in guilty rebellion. I peered through the blinds at the gathering on the lawn until every last guest had finally left.

The clergymen, I thought to myself, were truly remarkable.

III
A few vivid pictures like that stand out from our early days at Beckenham, and then my memories shift to Chislehurst. The Chislehurst mansion didn't just have a garden; it had expansive "grounds," complete with a gardener's cottage and a small gatekeeper's lodge at the entrance. The upward trajectory of their wealth and social climbing was far more obvious there than it had been in Beckenham. The speed of their rise was accelerating rapidly.

One particular night stands out as a classic example, marking the end of an era in its own way. I was visiting them—probably regarding some advertising work or other business matter—and my uncle and aunt had just returned in a hired carriage from a dinner party at the Runcorns' house., Even at that social event, my uncle had been dropping hints to Runcorn, nurturing the idea of our great corporate amalgamation in his mind., I think I arrived downstairs around eleven o'clock. I found the two of them sitting in the study. My aunt was perched on the arm of a chair with an amused but thoughtful look on her face, watching my uncle. He, looking very round and stretched out, was lounging in a low armchair pulled right up to the fireplace fender.

"Look here, George," my uncle said after we exchanged greetings. "I was just saying: We aren't Oh Fay!"

"Eh?"

"Not Oh Fay! Socially speaking!"

"He means 'au fait,' George—it's French!" my aunt clarified.

"Oh! I didn't think of French. One never knows what to expect with him. What went wrong tonight?"

"I've been thinking," he replied. "It isn't any one particular thing. I just ate too much of that..."

At first, we were served fishy food like salted caviar, sturgeon eggs, that looked like frog spawn, which confused me, and I was also bewildered when olives were served. And honestly, I had no idea which wine was which. I had to say so every time. When you do that, it completely breaks the flow of the conversation. Moreover, my aunt did not wear an evening gown like the other women. We cannot continue this way, George. This is not a proper way to advertise ourselves.

"I am not sure taking a carriage was a good idea," I said.

My uncle replied, "We must do all this better. We have to act with style. We must become smart business, smart people. Your aunt tries to brush it off as something humorous..." My aunt grimaced. "...but it is not humorous at all! Look! We are on an upward trend now, fair and square. We are going to be big shots. We are not going to be laughed at by people calling us 'Poovenoos'—a mispronunciation of the French word *parvenus*, meaning *nouveau riche*, do you understand!"

"No one laughed at you," my aunt said. "You old blowhard!"

"No one is going to laugh at me," my uncle said, glancing down at his bulging stomach and suddenly sitting up straight.

My aunt slightly raised her eyebrows, swung her foot, and said nothing.

"We are not keeping pace with our own progress, George. We must keep up. We are encountering new people, and they act like upper-class gentlemen and ladies. Formal dinners and all the rest of it. They put on airs and expect us to feel awkward, like fish out of water. We are not going to let that happen. They think we have no style. Well, we showed them style through our advertisements, and now we will show them style in every aspect... You do not need to be born into the upper class to dance to the tune of the merchants on Bond Street, London's luxury shopping district. Do you see?"

I handed him the cigar box.

"Your old employer Runcorn could not smoke cigars like these," he said, carefully snipping the end of his cigar. "We have beaten him in cigars. We will beat him in everything."

My aunt and I looked at him with worried eyes.

"I have a good idea," he muttered darkly toward his cigar, deepening our anxiety.

He put his cigar cutter in his pocket and spoke again.

"First, we must learn all the rules of this rotten little game. For example, we must get samples of every wine in the world and study them. Rene Smoor wine, French Burgundy wine, all of them! Susan, you drank Smoor wine tonight. And when you first tasted it, you grimaced. I saw you. You were surprised by the taste of the wine. You wrinkled your nose. We have to get used to wine so we do not do that. We also have to get used to wearing evening gowns. You too, Susan."

"I have always had a tendency to burst out of my clothes," my aunt said. "But... who cares?" She shrugged her shoulders.

I had never seen my uncle look so immensely serious.

"We must grasp the tricks of etiquette," he continued toward the fireplace. "Even riding a horse. Practice everything. Eat dinner every night wearing evening dress... Buy something like a brougham, a covered luxury carriage drawn by a horse. Learn things like golf and tennis. Act like a country gentleman. Oh, my. It is not just about escaping *gaucherie*, which means clumsiness."

"Pardon?" I asked.

"Oh! If you insist, *Gawshery*—the incorrect pronunciation of *gaucherie*!"

"That is French, George," my aunt said. "But I am no old clumsy fool. I just made that face for fun."

"It is not just about escaping clumsiness. We must have style. Look! Style! Doing everything right and then going one step further. That is what I call style. We can do it, and we will."

He mumbled his cigar, smoked for a moment, and leaned forward to peer into the fire.

"What is it, after all?" he asked. "What is it? It is the trick to eating, the trick to drinking. Clothes. How to control yourself, and avoiding those few little things they know for sure are wrong... things like a *shibboleth*, which is a pronunciation or word used exclusively by a particular group."

He fell silent again, and as the confidence in his mouth grew, his cigar tilted up from the horizontal toward the ceiling.

"Learn all these tricks within six months," he said more cheerfully. "Ah, Susan? Let's do this! George, you especially need to figure this out. You should get into a good social club and the like."

"Always ready to learn!" I said. "Ever since you gave me the chance to learn Latin. So far, however, we do not seem to have found a class of the population that speaks Latin."

"Well, we have reached French anyway," my aunt said.

"French is a very useful language," my uncle noted. "It clarifies situations. Tsk tsk. As for accent, no Englishman has an accent. English people cannot pronounce French properly. Do not argue with me about it. It is a bluff. Everything is a bluff. Life is practically a bluff. That is why it is so important for us to pay attention to style, Susan. *Le style c'est l'homme.* Style, it is the man himself. What are you laughing at, Susan? George, you are not smoking. These cigars are good for the mind... What do you think of all this? We must adapt ourselves. We have done so thus far... We cannot be defeated by these silly things."

IV

"What do you think of it, George?" he insisted.

I do not remember now how I replied regarding what I thought of it. However, I have a very distinct impression of catching my aunt's inscrutable gaze for a moment. In any case, with his usual energy, he charged into the mysteries of the "costly life" and began to become the calmest master of that world. Overall, I think he did it thoroughly. My head is full of complex, tangled memories of his experimental stages and experimental processes. Sometimes it is hard to say which memory comes first. Overall, I remember him presenting a series of small surprises, unexpectedly becoming a little more confident, a little more polished, a little richer and more graceful, and slightly more aware of the positions and values of things and people, time and time again.

There was a time—it must have been very early on—when I saw him deeply impressed by the splendor of the dining room at the National Liberal Club, a famous social club for politicians and the upper class in London. Only heaven knows who our host was, or what that particular little dinner was about! All that remains in my memory is the impression of six or seven guests straggling in, and my uncle looking around at the numerous tables with red lampshades, the exotic plants in large Italian Majolica ceramic jars, the gleaming ceramic pillars and pilasters, the impressive portraits of Liberal politicians and heroes, and everything else that contributed to the harmony of that grand spectacle. He whispered to me, "This is truly amazing, George!" That simple remark feels almost unbelievable to me even as I write it now. Because before long, a time came when not even the clubs of New York could intimidate my uncle, and he could walk past the bowing grandeur of the Royal Grand Hotel on a beautifully aggressive riverside balcony, strolling to his chosen table as relaxed and calm as one of the most legitimate kings on earth.

The two of them learned the new game quickly and brilliantly. They experimented outside, and they experimented at home. In Chiselhurst, Kent, with the help of a new, very expensive, but highly instructive chef, they tried everything that piqued their curiosity and was rumored to be difficult to cook, from asparagus to plover eggs. Then they hired a gardener who could wait tables, and he brought the food to the dining table. After that, a butler arrived.

I vividly remember my aunt's first evening dinner dress. She stood before the fireplace in the living room, summoning all her courage to bare her beautiful arms—which she had never imagined showing before—and looked at herself over her shoulder in the mirror.

"This must be how a ham feels," she pondered. "Just a ham wearing a necklace."...

I think I was about to offer a conventional compliment.

My uncle appeared in the doorway wearing a white vest and with his hands in his trouser pockets. He stopped and scrutinized her critically.

"One could not tell you apart from a duchess, Susan," he said. "I would like to have a painting of you standing in front of the fire like that. I would commission Sargent, the greatest portrait artist of our time, to do it! Somehow, you look elegant. Good lord! If only those damn merchants in Wimblehurst could see you."...

They spent a lot of time at hotels every weekend, and sometimes I went down with them. We seemed to have fallen into a massive, wandering crowd learning high society. I do not know if it was simply due to my changed circumstances, but over the last twenty years, the population that frequents hotels and uses restaurants seems to have grown tremendously and disproportionately. It is not just that there are more people in an economic upswing like us; it must be that the entire masses of the prosperous class have changed their habits, abandoning high-tea for luxurious dinners, wearing evening gowns, and using weekend hotels as training grounds for these new social skills. I am convinced that since I turned twenty-one, a rapid and systematic transition toward the upper class has been underway across the entire commercial upper-middle class. The personal characteristics of the people seen in this invasion were strangely mixed. There were conscientiously cultured people who lowered their voices and exuded a proud shyness, and there were aggressively sophisticated people who called each other by pet names loudly, seeking new opportunities to be witty and rude. There were awkward couples who secretly argued over manners and felt uncomfortable under the winter gaze, and there were cheerful, amiable, and often mismatched couples who liked inconspicuous corners, as well as jovial people feigning unaffected comfort. There were plump, happy ladies who laughed too loudly, and gentlemen in evening dress who would later "pull out their pipes" to smoke. And everyone knew that no matter how expensively they dressed or what room they booked, none of them were anyone important.

I now look back with a strange sense of distance at those crowded dining rooms with scattered tables, the inevitable red lampshades, and the unfriendly, inexperienced waiters asking, "Thick soup or clear soup, sir?" I have not eaten in that way, in that kind of place, for five years now. It has been almost five years since my life became so specialized and narrow.

My uncle's early automobile days intertwine with these memories, and the bright image of the lobby at the Magnificent Hotel in Bexhill-on-Sea comes to mind. People were dressed in evening gowns, sitting among crimson furniture and glossy white woodwork, waiting for the gong to summon them. And my aunt was there, wrapped in a duster coat and a cage-like veil, alongside highly agile hotel porters, under-porters, and an obsequious manager. A tall young lady in black clothes from the office could not hide her admiration, and right in the middle of the picture was my uncle. He made his first appearance wearing clothes like the traditional Esquimaux costume I have already mentioned—a small, solid, massive body wearing large goggles and a long brown rubber trunk, proboscis, with a motoring cap as flat as a plain on top of his head.

V

Thus, as new invaders into the upper echelons of the social system, we recognized our new needs and very consciously set out to acquire style and social skills, *Savoir Faire*,. We became part of what is now a quite important element in the confusion of our world: the multitudes of economically rising people who are learning how to spend money. These people consist of financiers, business owners who devour their competitors, and people who, like us, have invented new sources of wealth, encompassing almost all the Americans seen on the European stage. They are a diverse crowd with only this one thing in common. All of them, especially their wives and daughters, are moving from conditions of limited means, few possessions, and simple customs toward limitless consumption and the allure of Bond Street in London, Fifth Avenue in New York, and Paris. Their overall effect is like a progressive revolution, an indulgence of boundless freedom.

They suddenly discover luxuries that their moral codes never anticipated and had no provisions for—decorations and possessions they could not have imagined even in their fantasies. With immense, astonishing passion, they begin to "shop," systematically adapting to a crowded and glamorous new life filled with shopped items, jewelry, maids, butlers, coachmen, electric carriages, and rented city and country mansions. They dive into it as if jumping into a career. As a class, they talk, think, and dream about possessions. Their literature and their press are entirely focused on it. Unprecedentedly majestic and massive illustrated weekly magazines guide them on domestic architecture, the art of owning a garden, the acquisition of top-tier luxury cars, elaborate sports equipment, the purchase and management of estates, and travel and colossal hotels. Once they start moving, they go far and fast. Ownership becomes the essence of their lives. They discover a world organized to satisfy that passion. In just a year or two, they become experts. They join in the plunder of the eighteenth century, buying rare old books, fine old paintings, and good old furniture. Their initial crude concepts of newly perfected, dazzling furniture suites are replaced almost from the start by a jackdaw-like desire to hoard expensive, mismatched old things.

I remember my uncle taking up shopping very suddenly. During his Beckenham days and early Chiselhurst days, he was primarily interested in making money, and except for his aggressive renovation of the Beckenham house, he cared very little about his personal environment and possessions. I have now forgotten when the change occurred and he began to spend money. Some chance event must have revealed this new source of power to him, or some subtle change must have occurred in his brain tissue. He began to spend money and "shop." As soon as he started shopping, he shopped fiercely. He began buying paintings, and strangely enough, he bought grandfather clocks. For the Chiselhurst house, he bought nearly a dozen grandfather clocks and three copper bed warmers. After that, he bought a lot of furniture. Then he jumped into art patronage, commissioning paintings and giving gifts to churches and institutions. His purchases increased with a steady acceleration. That development was part of the mental changes that came over him amid the wild excitement of the final four years of his ascent. By the time he reached the climax, he was a furious squanderer. He bought large, unexpected items, shopping like a mind searching for expression, shopping to surprise and astonish people. He shopped ever more loudly, *crescendo*, with very intense and rich expression, *fortissimo, con molto espressione*, until the immense financial ruin of Crest Hill eroded his shopping forever. It was always he who shopped. My aunt did not shine as a buyer. I do not know what fine trait in her character caused this, but it is strange that my aunt never placed great value on possessions. As she passed through the crowded bazaar, much like Vanity Fair, during those feverish years, she undoubtedly spent freely and heavily, but she spent with a detached, humorous contempt for the things money could buy, even the "old" things. One afternoon, as she headed toward the Hardingham—sitting stiffly in her electric carriage, as she always did, looking out at the glittering world with interestingly, ironically innocent blue eyes from under a hat brim that defied criticism—I suddenly realized just how detached she was. "No one would sit so isolated like that if she didn't have dreams," I thought. "If so, what are her dreams?"

I had never thought about it before.

I also remember the contemptuous description she poured out after having lunch with a group of women at the Imperial Cosmic Club, a social club for upper-class women. She stopped by my room on the chance I might be there, and I served her tea. Saying she was tired and annoyed, she plopped down in my chair...

"George," she cried. "Women really are! Do I reek of money?"

"Did you have lunch?" I asked.

She nodded.

"With rich ladies?"

"Yes."

"An Oriental atmosphere?"

"Oh! Like a crowded *hareem*, the women's quarters in a palace,!... Bragging about their possessions... They touch you. George, they run their hands over your clothes to see if they are good quality!"

I calmed her down as best I could. "Those clothes really are good quality, aren't they?" I said.

"It is the instinct of the old pawnshop flowing in their blood," she said, drinking her tea. And then, with infinite disgust, she added, "They run their hands over your clothes. They grope you."

I briefly wondered if she had perhaps been caught possessing an unsuspected forgery. I do not know. After that incident, my eyes quickened, and I began to see for myself women running their hands over other women's furs, closely inspecting their lace, and even demanding to handle jewelry—evaluating, envying, and testing. They have a sort of etiquette. The woman who touches says, "What beautiful sable fur!" or "What lovely lace!" The woman being touched proudly admits, "You know, it is real," or she modestly and hastily denies the pretense, saying, "It is not real." They peer closely at paintings in each other's houses, feel the edges of draperies, and inspect the bottoms of ceramics...

I wonder if it really is the instinct of the old pawnshop flowing in their blood.

I doubt if Lady Drew or those Olympian gods, the noble aristocracy, did such things, but here I may just be clinging to another one of my old illusions about the aristocracy and the state. Perhaps possessions have always been plunder, and nowhere in the world have houses and furniture ever been native and natural to the men and women who used them...

VI

For me, at least, it marked an epoch in my uncle's career. One day, I learned that he had "shopped" for Lady Grove, a massive mansion. I realized he had taken a new, broad, and unprecedented step. He surprised me by suddenly scaling up from portable possessions like jewelry or cars to a vast countryside estate. The transaction was swift and decisive, like Napoleon. He heard about the place and said, "Snap." There was no preliminary desire or searching. Then he came home and told us what he had done. Even my aunt was visibly awestruck by this feat of purchasing for a day or two, and we both went down with him to see the house in a state bordering on consternation. It struck us then as a truly grand lord's manor. I remember the three of us standing on the west-facing terrace, looking at the house's windows reflecting the sky, and that feeling of unwarranted intrusion comes back to me.

As you know, Lady Grove is a very quiet, elegant place, and a truly beautiful house. Its long seclusion was effectively broken only by the honking of the arriving automobile. An old Catholic family had died out there over the centuries, and now it was completely extinct. Parts of the building date back to the thirteenth century, and its last architectural revision took place during the Tudor dynasty. The interior is mostly dark and chilly, with the exception of two or three favored rooms and a long gallery with a high ceiling and large windows.

It was an oak-galleried hall. The terrace was undoubtedly the estate's most magnificent feature. It was a very wide lawn bordered by a low stone battlement, and in one corner stood a massive cedar of Lebanon tree with flat, outspread branches. From under that tree, one could look out across the blue distances of the Weald, a wooded region in southern England. The blue landscape appeared remarkably Italian, thanks to the dark silhouette created by that single tree. Because the terrace was situated so high, looking southward allowed one to see the tops of wayfaring trees and spruces, while to the west, one looked down upon a steep beechwood slope where the road wound its way up. Turning back toward the quiet, ancient house, one saw a grey, lichen-covered facade featuring a beautifully arched entrance. It was warmed by the afternoon sunlight, with a touch of color added by a few neglected roses and the red berries of a pyracanthus shrub. I thought to myself that the most fitting modern owner for such a serene and splendid place would be some bearded, scholarly man in a black cassock, a Catholic priest's robe, with a gentle voice and pale hands, or perhaps a soft-spoken, grey-haired lady in delicate clothing. Instead, there was my uncle, holding his driving goggles in a sealskin glove, wiping his glasses with a pocket handkerchief, and asking my aunt if Lady Grove wasn't an "absolutely splendid" place.

My aunt did not answer him.

"The man who built this place must have worn armor and carried a sword," I speculated.

"There is still some of that stuff inside," my uncle said.

We went inside. A white-haired old woman was in charge of taking care of the place, and she groveled blatantly to her new master. To her, my uncle clearly seemed like a strange and terrifying apparition, and she was dreadfully afraid of him. However, while the surviving present might bow down to us, the past certainly did not. We stood before the long, dark portraits of the extinct family line—one of which was painted by the famous artist Holbein—and met their sidelong glances. They looked right back at us. All of us, I know, felt the enigmatic presence they exuded. Even my uncle, I think, was momentarily embarrassed by their invincibly self-satisfied expressions. It was as if, despite the fact that he had bought them and entirely replaced them, they secretly knew better and could afford to smile down at him.

The spirit of this place was similar to Bladesover, the estate where I spent my childhood, but it held something much older and more remote. The armor standing around might have been used in jousting tournaments, if not in actual battle, and this family must have sent its blood and treasure to Palestine time and time again during the Crusades, the most romantic military expeditions in history. Dreams, loyalties, status, and honor—how thoroughly they had all evaporated, leaving behind only these curiously painted smiles, these triumphant smiles of completion, as the ultimate expression of their spirit! In truth, those qualities had evaporated long before the last Lord Durgan passed away. In his old age, he had cluttered the place with Early Victorian cushions, carpets, tapestry tablecloths, and invalid appliances that seemed even more obsolete to us than the Crusades themselves... Yes, this place was distinctly different from Bladesover.

"It's a bit stuffy, George," my uncle said. "They didn't have much of a concept of ventilation when this place was built."

One of the wood-paneled rooms was half-filled with wardrobes and a four-poster bed. "This might be the ghost room," my uncle remarked, but it seemed unlikely to me that a family as withdrawn and completely exhausted as the Durgans would bother haunting anyone. What living thing today had any connection to their honor, their judgments, or their good and evil deeds? Ghosts and witchcraft were a much later innovation—a trend that came down from Scotland during the Stuart dynasty.

Later, while wandering around looking for epitaphs, we discovered a marble crusader with a broken nose. He was lying beneath a weathered stone canopy, just outside the restricted boundaries of the current Duffield church, half-buried in nettles.

"Ichabod," my uncle said, using the biblical term meaning the glory has departed. "Eh? We will all be like that someday, Susan... I am going to clean him up a bit and put up a railing to keep the children away."

"Saved at the eleventh hour, just like the old man," my aunt noted, quoting one of the less successful advertisements for our Tono-Bungay medicine.

But I do not think my uncle heard her.

The local vicar found us while we were standing by our newly acquired crusader statue. He came around the corner briskly, slightly out of breath. He looked as if he had been running after us ever since the first honk of our car horn had announced our presence to the village. He was an Oxford University graduate, clean-shaven, with a pale complexion, a cautious but respectful demeanor, an educated accent, and a general air of adaptability to the new order of things. These Oxford men are like the educated Greek slaves who served the Roman Empire, but for our modern plutocracy. He was a Tory, a conservative, at heart, but one might call him an adapted Tory who yielded to the pressure of circumstances; that is, he was no longer a strict royalist and was prepared to accept new landlords in place of the old ones. He knew we were pill-sellers and no doubt believed our souls to be terribly vulgar. Still, his new patron could have been a polygamous Indian rajah who would severely test a good man's tact, or perhaps a Jewish man with an inherited look of disdain. At any rate, we were English, and we were neither religious Dissenters nor Socialists, so he was cheerfully prepared to do whatever he could to turn both of us into proper gentlemen. For several reasons, he might have preferred Americans; they are not so obviously plucked from one part of the social class system and dropped into another like we were, and they are easier to teach. But in this world, we cannot always be choosers. Therefore, he was very bright and pleasant with us, showed us the church, and gossiped informatively about our countryside neighbors—Tux the banker, Lord Boom the magazine and newspaper proprietor, Lord Carnaby the great sportsman, and old Lady Osprey. Finally, he led us down a village lane, where three children bowed convulsively with terror-stricken eyes at the sight of my uncle, through a meticulously kept garden, and into a large, untidy vicarage. There, his faded, Victorian-style wife served us tea and introduced us to their large, confusing family scattered among worn-out wicker chairs on the edge of a well-used tennis lawn.

These people interested me. They were a common type, no doubt, but they were a new class to me. There were two tall, lanky sons who had been playing singles tennis—youths with red ears and budding black mustaches, wearing deliberately untidy tweed clothes and unbuttoned, unbelted Norfolk hunting jackets. There were several daughters who looked somewhat undernourished, dressed in practical and economical clothing. The younger ones still showed their legs in long brown stockings, while the eldest daughter present, we later learned there were one or two more hidden away, displayed a large gold cross and other assertive Christian symbols on her chest. Furthermore, there were two or three fox terriers, a retriever mix, and an old, bloodshot-eyed, terrible-smelling St. Bernard dog. There was also a jackdaw bird. Additionally, there was an ambiguous, silent woman whom my aunt later concluded must be a very deaf paying boarder. Two or three other people had hidden themselves when we arrived, leaving their unfinished tea behind. Rugs and cushions were scattered among the chairs, and I noticed that two of the cushions were covered with Union Jack flags.

The vicar introduced us rather sketchily, and his faded Victorian wife looked at my aunt with a mixture of customary snobbery and groveling respect. She spoke to my aunt in a sluggish, persistent voice about people in the neighborhood whom my aunt could not possibly know.

My aunt cheerfully tolerated these personal anecdotes, her blue eyes darting from place to place but repeatedly returning to the daughters' pale faces and the large cross on the eldest's chest. Encouraged by my aunt's demeanor, the vicar's wife became increasingly patronizing and overly friendly, making it clear that she could do a great deal to bridge the social gap between us and the established aristocratic families around us.

I only caught fragments of their conversation. "Mrs. Merridew brought him quite a lot of money. I heard her father was in the Spanish wine trade. She is quite a lady, though. After that, he fell off his horse and fractured his skull, so he took up fishing and farming. I'm sure you will like them. He is a very amusing man... Their daughter suffered a broken heart and went to China as a missionary, where she got caught up in a massacre."...

"The beautiful silks and items she brought back are truly unbelievable!"

"Yes, the Chinese gave them to her to appease her. You see, they didn't understand the difference made by Christian teachings; they thought that because they were massacring people, they would be massacred in return."...

"Their family has produced seven bishops!"

"She married a Catholic and cut ties with her family completely."...

"He failed some dreadful exam and had to join the militia."...

"So she bit his leg as hard as she could, and only then did he let go."...

"He had to have four of his ribs amputated."...

"He caught meningitis and passed away within a week."

"He had to have a large silver tube inserted into his throat, and when he wants to speak, he places his finger over it. I think it makes him so interesting. Somehow, you feel he is sincere. He is a most charming man in every way."

"Fortunately, they were preserved in alcohol and are now kept in his study, though, of course, he doesn't show them to just anyone."

The silent lady, completely unfazed by these seemingly exciting topics, stared at my aunt's outfit with a strange intensity. She was visibly moved when my aunt unbuttoned her dust cloak and threw it open. Meanwhile, we men conversed. One of the more energetic daughters listened brightly, while the young men lay on the grass at our feet. My uncle offered them cigars, but they both declined—out of shyness, it seemed to me—whereas the vicar tactfully accepted. Whenever we were not looking directly at them, these young men would secretly kick each other.

Fueled by my uncle's cigar smoke, the vicar's thoughts soared beyond the confines of the local district. "This Socialism," he said, "seems to be making immense headway."

My uncle shook his head. "We are too individualistic in this country for that sort of nonsense," he stated. "When something is everybody's business, it becomes nobody's business. That is where they go wrong."

"I am told they have some intelligent people in their ranks," the vicar noted, "writers and such. My eldest daughter was telling me about a quite distinguished playwright—I forget his name.

Milly, dear! Oh! She is not here. They have painters, too. This Socialism, it seems to me, is simply part of the general unrest of our age... But, as you say, the spirit of the public is against it. At least in the countryside. The people down here are far too sturdily independent in their own small lives—and entirely too sensible for that."...

"It is a great thing for Duffield to have Lady Grove occupied again," he was saying as my wandering attention returned from a fascinating disaster story in his wife's conversation. "People have always looked up to the estate. And considering everything, old Mr. Durgan was a tremendously good man—tremendously good. I hope you plan to spend a good deal of your time here."

"I intend to do my duty for the Parish," my uncle declared.

"I am sincerely glad to hear it—sincerely. We have missed the influence of the manor house. An English village isn't complete without it... The people get out of control. Life becomes dull. The young people drift away to London."

He took a careful puff of his cigar for a moment.

"We will look to you to liven things up," he said. Poor man!

My uncle tilted his cigar upward and then removed it from his mouth.

"What do you think the village needs?" my uncle asked.

He did not wait for an answer. "I have been thinking while you were talking about things we could do. Cricket—a fine English game. We could build a pavilion for the men to play. And every village ought to have a miniature rifle shooting range."

"Ye-ees," the vicar replied hesitantly. "Provided, of course, there isn't a constant popping noise."...

"I will manage that all right," my uncle assured him. "It would be a sort of long shed. We will paint it red, the British color. Then we need a Union Jack flag for the church and the village school. Perhaps we should paint the school red, too. There isn't enough color around here right now. It's too grey. And we should put up a maypole."

"I wonder how well our villagers would take to that sort of thing..." the vicar began.

"I am entirely in favor of bringing back that good old English spirit," my uncle said. "Festivals. Young lads and lasses dancing on the village green. Harvest celebrations, fairs, burning the Yule Log at Christmas—all of that."

In the brief pause that followed, one of the sons asked, "How would old Sally Glue do as a May Queen?"

"Or Annie Glassbound?" the other son added, letting out the loud, booming laugh of a young man whose voice had only recently deepened.

"Sally Glue is eighty-five years old," the vicar explained, "and Annie Glassbound is, well... a young woman of extremely large proportions. And she is not quite right in the head, you know." He tapped his forehead.

"Extremely large proportions!" the eldest son repeated, and the loud laughter resumed.

"You see," the vicar said, "all the more energetic girls go into domestic service in or near London. The exciting life attracts them. And no doubt the higher wages have something to do with it. Along with the freedom to wear fancy clothes, and a general freedom from restraint. So, it might be a little difficult to find a May Queen here right now who is truly young and... er... pretty. Of course, I couldn't possibly consider using any of my own daughters for something like that."

"We have to attract them back," my uncle insisted. "That is how I feel about it. We have to energize the countryside. The English countryside is still a going concern, a functioning enterprise; just as the Established Church of England is a going concern, if you'll excuse me for saying so. Just like Oxford or Cambridge Universities. Or any of those fine old traditions. It just needs fresh capital, fresh ideas, and new methods. Light railways, for example—or the scientific use of drainage systems, wire-fencing machinery, and all that."

The vicar's face betrayed a moment of dismay. Perhaps he was thinking about his peaceful country walks among the hawthorn and honeysuckle bushes.

"There are great things to be done on modern lines," my uncle continued, "with village jams and pickles—boiled right here in the country."

I think it was the echo of that last sentence in my mind that sharpened my sentimental sympathy as we drove through the scattered village street and across the neat green lawn on our way back to London. That afternoon, the village seemed like the most tranquil and idyllic collection of vine-covered homes you could ever imagine. Thatch roofs still remained on one or two whitewashed cottages; pyracanthus, agrimony, and daffodils grew in abundance; and above an unorganized orchard, the trees were white with blossoms, while colorful bulb flowers bloomed below. I noticed a row of traditional straw beehives—the dome-shaped kind that progressive thinkers had long ago condemned as inefficient. In the doctor's one-acre pasture, a flock of exactly two sheep was grazing; no doubt he had accepted them from a patient in lieu of payment. Two men and an old woman bowed to us with gestures of abject servitude, and my uncle replied with a lordly wave of his massive motoring glove.

"England is full of beautiful little places like this," my uncle said, leaning over the front seat and looking back with immense satisfaction. The dark lenses of his goggles rested for a while on the receding towers of Lady Grove as they peeked over the treetops.

"I think I shall have a flagstaff erected," he considered. "Then I can raise a flag to show when I am in residence. The villagers will like to know."...

I pondered this. "They will," I agreed. "They are used to liking to know."...

My aunt had been unusually quiet. Suddenly, she spoke up. "He says 'Snap,' and just like that, he buys the place," she remarked. "And what a lovely old housekeeping job he dumps on me! He struts through the village puffing his chest out like an old turkey. But who has to deal with the butler? Me! Who has to forget everything she ever knew and start all over again? Me! Who has to pack up and move from Chiselhurst to play the part of a grand lady? Me! You old nuisance! Just when I was finally settling down and starting to feel at home."

My uncle turned his goggled face toward her. "Ah! But this time it is our true home, Susan... We have finally made it to the top."

VII

Looking back, it seems like only a short step from the purchase of Lady Grove to the beginning of the Crest Hill estate—from the days when the former was considered a stupendous achievement to the days when it became entirely too small, dark, and inconvenient for a great financier's needs. For me, that era marked a period of increasing detachment from our business and the glamorous high society of London. I saw the world more and more in fragmented glimpses, and sometimes I would work continuously in my little research pavilion above Lady Grove for two weeks straight. Even when I did travel up to London, it was usually solely to attend a meeting of the aeronautical society or other learned societies, to consult academic literature, to hire researchers, or for some similar specialized business. For my uncle, however, it was a period of stupendous inflation. Every time I met him, I found him more confident, broader in his thinking, and far more conscious of being a major factor in great affairs. Soon, he was no longer merely an associate of business people; he had become big enough to attract the attention of even greater powers.

I grew accustomed to discovering personal news items about him in my evening newspaper, or seeing full-page portraits of him in sixpenny magazines. Usually, the news highlighted some generous charitable act, a romantic purchase or donation, or fresh rumors regarding corporate restructuring. You will recall that he saved the Parbury Reynolds, a famous estate or artwork, for the nation. Other times, there would be an interview, or my uncle's written contribution to a symposium on the "Secret of Success" or similar topics. There were endless, wonderful tales about his capacity for work, his incredible organizational skills for getting things done, his instant decision-making, and his remarkable ability to judge his fellow men. People frequently repeated his famous quote: "An eight-hour workday? I want eighty hours!"

He became modestly but resolutely a "public figure." Vanity Fair magazine published a caricature of him. One year, a portrait of my aunt—looking like a very graceful, slender lady indeed—was hung opposite the King's portrait in the great hall at Burlington House, the Royal Academy of Arts,. The following year, a medallion of my uncle crafted by Ewart was displayed on the walls of the New Gallery; though he looked perhaps a little too convex and bulging, he stared out at the world with a proud and imperial gaze.

I shared in his social experiences only intermittently. It is true that people knew of me, and many tried to use me as a sort of flank attack to gain access to him. There was also a legendary rumor—due partly, and unreasonably, to my growing scientific reputation, and partly to my reserved manner—that I played a much larger role in planning his business operations than was actually the case. This occasionally led to my inclusion in one or two very intimate private dinners...

Although I declined most of them, people invited me to weekend house parties or offered to introduce me and help me out in various ways. Among the people who approached me was Archie Garvell, a soldier,. He was now a stylish, cash-strapped soldier with no particular distinction. As far as I can recall, he seemed quite ready to willingly develop any sporting instincts I might have had. Furthermore, he was completely unaware of the fact that we had met before. He was always recommending winning racehorses to me. Of course, this behavior likely stemmed from his desire to receive a genuinely good tip in advance as a reward, something that would allow us to gain something for nothing through our sure and scientific methods.

Despite being deeply absorbed in my experiments and work, when I search through my memories now, I realize that I saw quite a lot of the great world during those eventful years. I closely observed the machinery that ran this astounding British Empire. I rubbed shoulders and shared experiences with bishops and politicians, women involved in politics and those who were not, doctors and soldiers, artists and writers, directors of major newspapers, philanthropists, and all sorts of prominent and important people. I saw politicians without their medals and bishops inhaling cigar smoke instead of incense, draped only in a bit of purple silk rather than elaborate religious garments. The reason I was able to observe them so well was that most of them were not looking at me, but at my uncle. Consciously or unconsciously, they were calculating how to use him and assimilate him into their system. That system was a plutocracy, a system ruled by the wealthy,—the most meticulous, successful, and aimlessly overarching system that had ever weighed down the destiny of humanity. As far as I could see, until disaster struck him, not a single person resented my uncle's lies, his almost blatantly dishonest methods, or the chaotic disruptions he caused in various industries through his impulsive business operations. I can still see them around my uncle now, polite yet watchful and diverse. My uncle, with his stiff and solid build, was always the center of attention. I remember his wiry hair, short nose, and lower lip that seemed electrified with self-confidence. If you wandered around gatherings of famous people, you could hear whispers like this: "Is that Mr. Ponderevo?"

"You mean that little man?"

"Yes, that short, vulgar man wearing glasses."

"People say he made a fortune..."

Sometimes I also saw my uncle standing next to my aunt's loud hat on some spectacular platform. It was a place filled with titles and lavish outfits, and, to borrow my uncle's phrase, he was "playing his part" perfectly. He donated huge sums to well-known charities and even gave short, passionate speeches for good causes in front of the most high-ranking audiences. When the applause died down, he would begin with, "Mr. Chairman, Your Royal Highness, ladies and gentlemen," adjusting his stubborn-looking glasses, throwing back the tails of his frock coat, a man's formal coat, and placing both hands on his hips to deliver his speech. Throughout his speech, he would make his characteristic "Zzzz" sound. While speaking, his hands moved restlessly, fiddling with his glasses or rummaging through his vest pockets. When a sentence sprang out suddenly like a coiled snake, he would slowly rise on his toes, and when the sentence ended, he would drop heavily back onto his heels. These were the exact same gestures he had shown when we first met, standing before the empty fireplace in our small, curtained living room, talking to my mother about my future.

On every endlessly long, hot afternoon spent in the little shop in Wimblehurst, he had talked and dreamed of the romance of modern commerce, the myth of success,. And now, his romance had clearly become a reality.

VIII

People say my uncle lost his mind when his wealth reached its peak. But to tell a little truth about a man I once loved, he didn't have much of a mind to lose in the first place. He was always imaginative, erratic, inconsistent, and recklessly inaccurate. The massive influx of wealth simply provided an opportunity for these tendencies of his to be fully unleashed. Of course, it is true that by the time he reached his peak, he occasionally became extremely irritable and could not tolerate being contradicted. However, I believe that was due to the anxiety of trying to maintain his sanity, rather than any mental illness. But evaluating him or fully conveying how he changed to the readers is a truly difficult task. I saw too much of him, and my memory is packed with his unorganized moods and appearances. Sometimes he was inflated with megalomania, other times he was deflated, and at other times he was belligerent or impenetrably absorbed in self-satisfaction. But he was always impulsive, unpredictable, fragmented, and full of energy. And in a very fundamental, though hard-to-define way, he was an absurd man.

I remember a conversation we had on the veranda of a small annex near the workshop behind Crest Hill, where I kept my airplanes and airships. Perhaps it stands out because the surrounding scenery was so tranquil and beautiful. It was just one of countless similar conversations we had, and I don't know why that particular one stuck in my memory. It just happened that way. He came to consult me about a certain chalice issue after drinking coffee after a meal. In a moment of ostentation, driven by a countess's pleas, he had decided to donate a chalice to a fine church in the East End, a slum area in London,. With equal rashness, I had recommended my artist friend Ewart as the right person for the job. Ewart immediately drew up a wonderful sketch of a sacred cup surrounded by a wreath of Millies with spread arms and wings, and he received an advance of fifty pounds based on it. However, after that, frustrating delays continued. The chalice gradually lost its commercial nature and took on the ambiguous quality of the elusive, legendary Holy Grail, the cup said to have been used by Jesus Christ at the Last Supper, and eventually, even the sketch faded away.

My uncle began to get restless. "Look here, George. People are going to start asking for that damn thing!"

"What damn thing?"

"That chalice, damn it! People have started asking questions. This isn't business, George."

"This is art and religion," I protested.

"That's all well and good. But George, making a promise and failing to deliver the goods isn't a good advertisement for us. We'll just have to write off your friend Ewart as a bad debt and give the job to a proper company."

We sat side by side on deck chairs on the annex veranda, smoking cigars and drinking whiskey, and once the chalice issue was settled, we fell into meditation. His temporary irritation had passed. It was a truly wonderful summer night following a very hot, languid day. The bright moonlight dimly illuminated the ridges of the receding hills, looking like layers of rolling waves. In the distance, the lights of Leatherhead looked like pinpricks, and in the foreground, the small stage where I used to launch my gliders shone like wet steel. It must have been the height of June because I remember the enchanting song of the nightingale coming from the woods that obscured the window lights of the Lady Grove mansion.

Breaking a long silence, my uncle said, "George, we've made it here. Didn't I tell you?"

"When did you say that?" I asked.

"In that small, shabby place on To'nem Court Road. We fought a really fierce head-on battle, and finally, we're here!"

I nodded.

"Do you remember when I told you about Tono-Bungay?... Well... I thought of the name that very afternoon!"

"I did wonder about that sometimes," I admitted.

"George, it's a truly amazing world these days. It's a world that gives a fair chance to anyone who grabs the opportunity. You could call it a career open to talent, career ouvert to the Talons,. Tono-Bungay. Think about it! It's a truly great and constantly growing world. I'm glad we are part of this world and are on the road to success. George, we're becoming big shots. Big things are rolling into our laps. Like that Palestine business."

He fell deep into thought for a moment and then made a soft "Zzzz" sound. Then he went quiet.

A cricket in the grass picked up his story and chirped, waiting until my uncle was ready to continue. The cricket, too, seemed to think it had arrived here through some plan of its own. "Chirrrrup, chirrrrup," the cricket sang.

"My god, that shop in Wimblehurst was really something!" my uncle blurted out. "George, if we ever get a day off, let's drive there and run over that dog sleeping on the High Street, the main street,. There was always a dog sleeping there. Always... I'd like to see that old shop again. Old man Ruck is probably still standing by the door among the sheep, grinning with all his teeth showing, and that idiot Marbel would come out wearing a white apron with a pencil tucked behind his ear, pretending to be awake... I wonder if they would recognize me. Somehow, I wish they'd recognize that it's me."

"They've probably taken a big hit from the expansion of large corporations like the International Tea Company," I said. "And that dog has been on the sidewalk for six years now. Poor thing, he probably can't even sleep properly there anymore because of the car horns and his ruined nerves."

"Things are changing everywhere," my uncle said. "You're probably right... George, we're living in a tremendous era. It's an era of massive progress and the coming empire. This Palestine business... it's a really bold plan. George, this is one big process. And we're holding it. George, we're sitting here controlling it. The world has entrusted it to us."

"It's unusually quiet tonight. But if only we could see and hear." He waved his cigar-holding hand towards Leatherhead and London.

"They're out there. Millions of people, George. Just imagine what those ten million people did today. You can't even grasp it. It's like what old Whitman, the American poet Walt Whitman, said... What was it he said? Anyway, it's like Whitman's poetry. A great man, Whitman! A fine old fellow! Strangely, his words are hard to quote exactly... And these millions are nothing. Across the sea, there are hundreds of millions more. China, Morocco, all of Africa, America... Well, now we've become these chosen people with power and leisure. Because we were full of energy, because we seized opportunities, and because we made the world buzz while others were just waiting for it to run. See? We're here. Holding the control. We're big shots. People growing massively. In a sense, we are the 'Forces.'"

He paused for a moment. "George, it's really amazing," he said.

"Anglo-Saxon energy," I said softly to the night sky.

"That's exactly it, George. Energy. It's put everything in our grasp. The threads and lines stretching from our little office reach endlessly into West Africa, Egypt, India, and in all directions, north, south, east, and west. In fact, we are running the world. Making it run faster and faster. Creatively. Look at that Palestine canal plan. It's a brilliant idea! Imagine if we take on that job, if we and others draw water from the Mediterranean and flow it into the Dead Sea valley. Think about how much the world will change! All the deserts will bloom like roses, Jericho, the biblical city, will disappear forever, and all the holy sites will be submerged under water... It'll probably destroy Christianity."

He was lost in thought for a moment. "Digging a canal..." my uncle muttered. "Boring tunnels... new countries... new centers... Zzzz... Finance... It's not just Palestine.

"George, where will we be by the time all this is done? We're undertaking numerous massive projects. We've secured investors solidly and surely. There's no reason why we shouldn't ultimately become incredibly huge figures. There will be difficulties, but I have the ability to handle them. Our bones are still a bit soft, but they'll harden soon... George, if I settled everything and stepped away right now, my wealth would probably be around a million pounds. George, this is a great era. A truly amazing era!"

I stole a sideways glance at his bulging stomach in the twilight, and to be honest, he didn't look like a man of any particularly great worth.

"We have control of the situation, George. Us big shots. We need to stick together, George. We need to run this show. We join hands with the old order, like the mill-wheel in writer Rudyard Kipling's work., George, that's the best thing he ever wrote. I just read it again. That book is why I bought the Lady Grove estate., Anyway, George, we have to lead this country. This country is ours. We turn the country into a scientifically organized enterprise. We inject ideas and electrify it. We run the press and push all sorts of development projects. All sorts of development. I've even spoken with Lord Boom, a media magnate, and I've talked to all kinds of people. This is great work. Progress. A world running on business principles. This is just the beginning."

He fell into deep meditation.

He made the "Zzzz" sound for a while and then stopped.

Finally, in the tone of someone who had found the ultimate answer to profound problems, he said, "Yes."

After an appropriate pause, I asked, "What is it?"

My uncle hesitated for a moment, and it felt as tense as if the fate of a nation were hanging in the balance. Then he spoke with a voice that seemed to come from the very bottom of his heart. No, it must have truly been from his heart.

"George, I'd like to drop into the Eastry Arms, a pub, for a bit. Just when guys like Ruck and Marbel in the parlor are all gathered around sitting down to play whist, a card game,. I want to go and pour out my true feelings for 10 minutes. Straight from the shoulder, without beating around the bush. I want to tell them exactly what I think of them. It's a small thing, but I'd like to do it just once before I die."

He dwelled on that thought for a while, making his "Zzzz" sound.

Then he changed the subject with the tone of a detached critic.

"Look at Boom," he mused.

"George, this old British system is truly amazing. It's calm and stable, yet there is room for new people to enter. We show up and take our places. It's almost expected. We participate. That is where our democracy differs from America. Over there, if a man succeeds, all he gets is money. But here, there is a system that is practically open to everyone... Even people like Boom... come from humble backgrounds."

His voice stopped. I thought about the meaning of his words. Then suddenly, I kicked my feet in the air, rolled over on the deck chair, dropped my legs, and sat up straight.

"You can't be serious!" I said.

"About what, George?"

"Donating to party funds. A mutually beneficial deal. Do we really have to stoop to that?"

"What are you getting at, George?"

"You know. They would never allow it, Uncle!"

"Allow what?" he said weakly. "And why shouldn't they?"

"They wouldn't even give you a baronetcy. Never!... But, of course, there is someone like Boom! And Collingshead and Gorver. Those people were in the beer business, and they sold scraps of newspapers! Come to think of it, the Tono-Bungay business... it's not like being a horse racing betting agent!... Although, of course, there have been some very dignified betting agents. It's not like a foolish scientist who can't even make money!"

My uncle grumbled in dissatisfaction. We had clashed over this issue before.

A malicious humor seized me. "What would people call you?" I imagined. "The vicar would like the name Duffield. That sounds too much like Duffer, idiot,! Titles are tricky things." I ran through various possibilities in my head.

"Why not get an idea from a socialist pamphlet I happened to read yesterday? The author said we are all getting 'delocalized.' It's a beautiful word, delocalized! Why not be the first delocalized peer? 'Tono-Bungay' is perfect! You know, there is an actual place called 'Bungay.' How about 'Lord Tono of Bungay, in bottles everywhere'? Hmm?"

To my surprise, my uncle got angry.

"Damn it. George, you don't seem to realize I'm serious! You're always mocking Tono-Bungay! As if it were some kind of scam. This is a perfectly legitimate trade, perfectly legitimate. It has sufficient value and is a good product... When I come up here to tell you my plans and share ideas... you mock me. You do. You just don't get it. This is an incredibly huge deal. A massive enterprise. You need to get used to the new environment. You need to face the future that lies before us. Drop that tone."

IX

My uncle was not completely consumed by business and ambition. He maintained communication with modern thought as well. For instance, as far as I knew, he was heavily swayed by what he called "Nietzsche's, the philosopher, Overman idea... stuff like that."

He blended comforting implications about powerful and exceptional human beings who were freed from the petty limitations of integrity with the legend of Napoleon, the French Emperor,. It provided a considerable outlet for his imagination. That damn Napoleonic legend! The real harm caused by Napoleon's highly disastrous and chance-driven life only began after he died, when romantically inclined people felt free to embellish his character. I firmly believe that if there hadn't been the Napoleonic legend to mislead him, my uncle would not have met such an absurd downfall. In many ways, he was a better and far kinder person than his actions suggested. But whenever he was torn between decent conduct and a base advantage, that cult exerted a stronger and stronger influence. "Think of Napoleon. Think how the inflexibly willful Napoleon would have handled such pangs of conscience." That became the rule, and the result was always a new step into dishonor.

In a rather unsystematic way, my uncle was a collector of Napoleonic relics. The thicker the book about his hero, the more easily he bought it. He bought letters, shiny ornaments, and weapons that had even the slightest connection to the "Man of Destiny," and he even bought an old carriage in Geneva that Bonaparte, Napoleon, might have ridden in, though he never brought it home,. He filled the quiet walls of the Lady Grove estate with engravings and statues of Napoleon. According to my aunt, he preferred the bulging portraits of him in a white vest or small statues with his hands behind his back and his body leaning forward. The Durgan family watched this whole process cynically and sneeringly.

And sometimes, after breakfast, my uncle would stand a little apart under the window light at Lady Grove, stick two fingers of one hand between his vest buttons, sink his chin down, and sink into thought. He looked like the most ridiculous short, fat man in the world. My aunt would see him and say, "He looks like an old Field Marshal. It completely floors me, George!"

Perhaps this bias toward Napoleon made him smoke cigars a little less often than before. But I can't be sure. What is certain is that after he read a book called "Napoleon and the Fair Sex," it caused my aunt considerable irritation. Because for a time, that book awakened my uncle to another side of life that he had largely forgotten while caught up in commercial concerns. Suggestion plays such a big role in this area. My uncle seized the next opportunity and had an "affair"!

It was not a particularly passionate affair, and naturally, the exact details never reached me. It was purely by chance that I learned anything about it at all. One evening, I went to an "At Home", a reception, at the apartment of Robbert, the Royal Academician, R.A., who had painted my aunt's portrait. I was surprised to stumble upon my uncle in a crowd of bohemians and stylish people. He was standing a little apart in a recess in the wall, quietly conversing with—or rather, being talked to by—a plump, small, blonde woman named Helen Scrymgeour, who wrote novels...

I was helping with the launch of a weekly magazine. I bumped elbows with a large lady who was talking about the magazine, but I didn't even need to hear her words to figure out the relationship between her and my uncle. It was as obvious as a giant billboard on the street. In fact, it was surprising that the entire gathering hadn't noticed it. Perhaps they already knew. She was wearing a diamond necklace that was far too flashy and expensive for someone in journalism. And she was looking at my uncle with a peculiar gaze common in such affairs—a gaze of unspoken ownership and an intense, tightly leashed intimacy ready to snap at any moment. It felt far more explicit than an ordinary marriage. What turned my suspicion into absolute certainty was my uncle's eyes when he finally spotted me. His eyes held a mixture of embarrassment, a strange pride, and a defiant look that said he didn't care what others thought. The next day, my uncle made a point to briefly praise how intelligent the lady was, seemingly to ensure I hadn't missed the underlying message of what was happening at the party.

Later, I heard some gossip from a friend of the lady. My curiosity was so strong that I couldn't help but listen quietly. I had never in my life imagined my uncle falling romantically in love with anyone. According to the rumor, she called him her "God in the Car"—referencing the title of a novel by the famous English adventure writer Anthony Hope, describing a powerful man ruthlessly driving toward success. The unspoken rule of their relationship was that whenever business called, my uncle could leave her behind heartlessly, and situations were usually arranged so that this would happen. It was a mutual understanding between them that to him, women were merely a passing incident, and his ambition for success was his greatest passion. A massive world and a noble hunger for power were calling him. I could never know how sincere Mrs. Scrymgeour was about all this, but it is highly likely that she was captivated by the immense charm of my uncle's massive wealth, and that she too harbored truly romantic feelings for their encounters. There must have been some extraordinary moments between them....

When I realized my uncle was having an affair, I was deeply worried and heartbroken for my aunt. I thought she would suffer a terrible humiliation. I suspected she was just putting on a brave face to hide her pain over losing my uncle's affection. But I had completely underestimated her. She remained unaware of it for a while, and when she finally found out, she acted with immense anger and explosive energy. She didn't spend a single second agonizing over the sentimental romance. She concluded that my uncle "needed a good smacking." She dressed herself up with an unexpected new hat, went straight to the Hardingham Hotel where my uncle was staying, and unleashed an unimaginable torrent of scolding upon him. Then she came over to me and "blew up," demanding to know why I hadn't told her sooner....

I tried to remind her of the conventional ways society views such affairs. But my aunt's unique way of thinking was too steadfast to be broken. "Men don't tattle on each other when it comes to passionate love affairs," I protested, offering worldly excuses.

"Women!" she retorted with great indignation, "and men! This isn't about women and men, George. This is between him and me! Why don't you talk sense?"

"Old-fashioned passion is fine in its own way, George. And I'm the last person to be jealous. But this is just tired, foolish nonsense.... I'm not going to let him show off to other women what a silly old lobster he is.... I'm going to mark every single piece of his underwear with red letters: 'Ponderevo - Private'—every last scrap."

"Running around having love affairs at his age—while wearing an abdominal corset!"

I cannot imagine what was said between my aunt and uncle. But I have no doubt that, for once, her usual lighthearted banter was cast aside. I don't know exactly how they conversed, because even knowing them as well as I did, I had never heard them engage in such deep, private conversations. At any rate, the man I had to deal with over the next few days was a worried, deeply troubled "God in the Car." He made that trembling "Zzzz" sound with his lips far more than usual and constantly made nervous, impatient gestures that had nothing to do with what we were talking about. It was also obvious that he was having a much harder time than usual explaining things to people in every aspect of his life.

The private moments of this affair were hidden from me, but in the end, it was my aunt who triumphed. My uncle didn't just break up with Mrs. Scrymgeour; he practically yanked her away and discarded her. And rather than turning the breakup into a beautiful novel, the lady grieved loudly, pouring out buckets of sorrow from her fragile, impure feminine soul. My aunt did not make even the slightest appearance during this breakup process. So it is highly doubtful that Mrs. Scrymgeour ever knew the real reason she was dumped. Acting like Napoleon, my uncle had essentially pretended to be unmarried, and just as the real Napoleon abandoned Josephine to form a great alliance, my uncle threw away his lover.

It was a victory for my aunt, but it came at a price. For some time, it was clear that the atmosphere between them was strained. My uncle gave up the lady, but he deeply resented having to do so. She had meant far more in his imagination than people realized. For a long time, he refused to "come around" to his usual self. He acted irritable, impatient, and secretive toward my aunt. From what I observed, after a few major clashes, my aunt also stopped the cheerful scolding that had been a long-standing tradition and a great source of vitality in their lives. With the loss of that banter, both of them became poorer in spirit and less happy. My aunt devoted herself more and more to the joys and complexities of managing Lady Grove, their newly acquired mansion. The servants took a strong liking to her, and during her time managing the estate, she became the godmother to three children all named Susan—the coachman's daughter, the gardener's daughter, and the upland gamekeeper's daughter. She gathered a library of old household management books that perfectly suited the atmosphere of the mansion. She revived the still-room—the room for preparing preserves and cosmetics—and became a master artist at making jellies and wines from elderberries and cowslip flowers.

X

While I neglected my own finances due to my scientific research and my struggles with the challenges of inventing airplanes, and while I left my uncle's financial affairs unattended, his business schemes grew increasingly vast and dangerous, and his spending became far more reckless and extravagant. I believe the main reason he grew increasingly irritable and secretive toward my aunt and me during this peak period was that he was painfully aware of—and haunted by—the fact that his financial foundation was becoming severely unstable. I think he dreaded having to explain the situation to us, fearing that our lighthearted jokes might accidentally pierce through to the truth. He wouldn't even face the truth in his own private thoughts. He kept piling up worthless, uncashable securities in his safes, which hung precariously like a potential avalanche ready to bury the entire economic world. Yet his spending spread like a fever, and his restless desire to maintain the illusion that he was successfully marching toward limitless wealth gnawed deeper and deeper into his mind. A bizarre trait of his during this time was his habit of buying similar items over and over again. His ideas seemed to run in series. In less than a year, he bought five new automobiles, each faster and more powerful than the last. If his head chauffeur hadn't promptly threatened to resign at every moment of danger, my uncle would have driven the cars himself. He used the cars more and more, developing a massive obsession with the sheer act of moving around.

Then, irritated by a joke he overheard at a dinner party, he began to chafe and complain about Lady Grove. "This mansion, George," he said. "It doesn't suit me. It's so cramped I can't breathe. It's choked full of old memories. And I can't stand the portraits of these damn Durgans—the old family that used to own this place!

"That guy in the corner, George. No! The other corner! The man in the cherry-colored coat. He's watching you! He'd look pretty silly if I shoved a fire poker down his throat!"

"I think he'd look exactly the same as he does now," I replied. "Like he's highly amused."

Excitedly, he put his glasses back on—they had fallen off in his agitation—and glared at his enemies on the wall. "What are they? What is the whole lot of them? They're completely dead meat! They just stayed stuck in the mud. They didn't even survive into the Reformation era—that old, outdated Reformation! You have to move with the times! They moved against the times.

"They're just a family of failures. They didn't even try!

"They are exactly the opposite of what I am, George. The exact opposite. This doesn't suit me.... All this living in the past.

"And I need a bigger place, George. I need air, sunlight, room to move freely, and more service. A house where you can actually get things moving! Zzzz. Come on! Even putting a telephone in here feels discordant and out of place.... There is nothing, absolutely nothing of value in this house except the terrace. It's all dark, old, dried up, and full of outdated things—musty old ideas. It's a place more suited for a silverfish bug than a modern man.... I don't know how I ever ended up here."

He aired another grievance. "That damn vicar," he complained. "He thinks I should consider myself lucky to have gotten this estate! Every time I meet him, I can see him thinking it.... One of these days, George, I'll show him what a truly modern house looks like!"

And he actually did.

I remember the day he declared, as the Americans say, his plans for Crest Hill. I was just beginning to experiment with auxiliary collapsible balloons, and he had come up the hill to see my new gas equipment. But behind his glasses, his eyes kept wandering toward the open downs in the distance. "Let's walk back toward Lady Grove over the hill," he said. "There's something I want to show you. Something magnificent!"

That summer evening, it was a sunlit, empty space. The sky and the earth were warmly tinted with the sunset, and the occasional cry of a lapwing only emphasized the peaceful stillness of a long, clear day drawing to a close. It was a truly beautiful peace, destined to be destroyed forever. And there stood my uncle—the modern man of power, in a grey top hat, a grey suit, and black-ribboned glasses, with his short, thin legs and large stomach—pointing and gesturing, threatening this tranquility.

He started with a wide sweep of his arm. "That's the place, George," he said. "See it?"

"Eh!" I startled, as I had been lost in other thoughts.

"I got it."

"Got what?"

"Land for a house! A twentieth-century mansion! That's the perfect spot for it!"

One of his trademark catchphrases was born right then and there.

"Open to the winds of heaven on all four sides, George!" he said. "Eh? Four-square to the winds of heaven!"

"You'll certainly get strong winds up here," I said.

"It needs to be a mammoth house, George—to match these hills."

"Quite so," I said.

"Great galleries and structures extending from here all the way to there. See? I've been thinking about it constantly, George! Looking out across this entire Weald—the rolling countryside. With its back turned to Lady Grove."

"And catching the morning sun right in its eyes."

"Like an eagle, George. Like an eagle!"

And so he revealed to me the Crest Hill project, which quickly became the greatest obsession of his peak years. By now, the whole world has heard of that extravagant mansion, which grew out of control like a salted snail as plans constantly changed during construction, swelling and bulging endlessly. I cannot know what kind of mad fantasy of towering peaks, terraces, arcades, and corridors finally sparkled on the hills of his mind. Even though its expansion was abruptly halted by our business collapse, the place is awe-inspiring enough exactly as it stands today—a massive, empty structure built by a childless man driven by instinct to fill a void. His chief architect was a young man named Westminster. My uncle had handpicked him at the Royal Academy's architecture exhibition because he recognized a bold, grandiose quality in his work. However, my uncle also brought in an army of other experts to work alongside him: stonemasons, sanitary engineers, painters, sculptors, calligraphers, metalworkers, woodcarvers, furniture designers, ceramics specialists, landscape gardeners, and even the man who designed the layouts and ventilation for the new animal enclosures at the London Zoo. On top of all this, my uncle added his own ideas. The project occupied his mind constantly, but from Friday night to Monday morning, it entirely consumed him. On Friday nights, he would come down to Lady Grove in a car so packed with architects you could barely breathe. But he didn't limit his invitations to architects; anyone could be invited for the weekend to view Crest Hill. Many eager business promoters, unaware of how perfectly my uncle compartmentalized the different sectors of his mind like Napoleon, tried to curry favor with him by pitching tiles, ventilators, or new electrical fittings. Every Sunday morning, unless the weather was absolutely terrible, my uncle would finish breakfast, dismiss his secretaries, and visit the construction site with a large entourage. There, he would alter and develop the blueprints, make modifications, and issue massive new orders verbally while making his signature "Zzzz" sound. As Westminster and the contractors eventually realized, this was a highly unsatisfactory and chaotic way to work.

In my memory, he stands there as the symbol of this era, the embodiment of luck and self-promotion, the current master of the world. He stood on the broad terrace sweeping out in front of the colossal main entrance. Compared to that forty-foot-tall archway, he looked ridiculously small and disproportionate. Behind him sat a granite astronomical globe bound in brass, symbolizing the world, featuring a small, adjustable lens tube on a gun-metal arm designed to focus on the exact point on earth where the sun was shining vertically at that moment. He stood there grouped like Napoleon with his entourage: men in tweed jackets and golf suits, a small lawyer whose name escapes me wearing grey trousers and a black jacket, and Westminster, dressed in Jaeger thermal underwear, a flamboyant floral tie, and his own peculiar brown cloth suit.

The breeze rolling off the downs fluttered my uncle's coat tails and messed up his stiff hair. As he pointed out various features of the landscape to his attentive collaborator, his face and figure fully displayed his unbridled, endless appetites.

Hundreds of feet below lay wheeling-planks for wheelbarrows, trenches, excavated pits, mounds of earth, and piles of garden stones hauled from the Weald ridges. On both sides rose the walls of this random, meaningless palace he was building. At one point, he had over three thousand laborers working on the site, a workforce so massive it disrupted the economic balance of the entire surrounding countryside....

And so he poses for the picture in my memory, standing amidst a raw construction site that would never be finished. He did the most bizarre things at that site—actions that became increasingly detached from any sense of financial scale and further removed from grounded humanity. It seemed as though he finally believed he had broken free from all such limitations. He moved an entire sizable hill and relocated sixty fully grown trees about two hundred feet to the south just to open up his eastern view. Another time, inspired by a restaurant in the city, he built a billiard room with a thick glass roof underneath the waters of his ornamental lake. He furnished one wing of the house while its roof was still uncompleted. He built a swimming pool measuring thirty feet square right next to his upstairs bedroom. And to top it all off, he began constructing a massive wall to enclose his entire domain and protect it from the invasion of common people. It was a ten-foot-tall wall topped with glass, and had it been finished as he planned, it would have stretched for nearly eleven miles. Some of the sections built toward the end were constructed so dishonestly that their foundations collapsed within a year, but a few miles of the wall still stand today. Whenever I think of that wall now, I can't help but think of the hundreds of eager, poor investors who followed his "star"—investors whose hopes, lives, wives' security, and children's futures are all hopelessly mixed into that crumbling mortar....

It is strange how many of these modern financiers, who build their success on luck and bluffing, end their careers by building actual structures. It wasn't just my uncle. Sooner or later, they all seem to test their luck by trying to make it real, attempting to solidify their liquid wealth into tangible bricks and mortar, and trying to tie their moonshine fantasies to an actual weekly payroll. And then, the entire structure built on confidence and imagination begins to shake... and down they all come....

When I think of that ruined hillside, that colossal wasteland of bricks, mortar, crude roads, scaffolding, and temporary sheds—a general act of blind violence against the peace of nature—I am reminded of a conversation I had with the vicar one bleak day after he had watched my airplane glide. I had just landed and was standing next to my machine in a sweater and shorts while he talked to me about aeronautics. His pale, gaunt face couldn't hide a deep sense of desolation that seemed to possess him.

"You have almost convinced my mind, sir," he said, walking up to me. "Against my will, no less.... A truly marvelous invention! But it will take you a very long time before you can ever emulate that perfect piece of machinery—the wing of a bird."

He looked over at my hangars.

"You've changed the look of this valley, too," he noted.

"Just temporary defilements," I remarked, guessing the thoughts troubling his mind.

"Of course. Things come and go. Things come and go. But... hmm. I've just been over the hill to look at Mr. Edward Ponderevo's new house. That... that is something much more permanent. A magnificent place! In many ways. Truly imposing. Somehow, I hadn't brought myself to walk that way until now. The construction has advanced greatly.... We find the massive influx of strangers brought into the nearby villages by these operations—mostly workingmen—to be a little embarrassing. It unsettled us. They bring a new spirit into the village: gambling, new ideas, all sorts of strange notions. Our pub owners love it, of course. But they sleep in people's outhouses... and they make the town a bit unsafe at night. The other morning, I couldn't sleep due to slight indigestion, and I looked out my window. I was stunned to see people riding by on bicycles. A silent procession. I counted ninety-seven of them at the crack of dawn. All heading up the new road toward Crest Hill. I thought it was remarkable. So, I went up there to see exactly what they were doing."

"Thirty years ago, that would have been far more than remarkable," I said.

"Yes, indeed. The world changes. We think nothing of it now... comparatively speaking. And that massive house..."

He raised his eyebrows. "Truly stupendous! Stupendous.

"The entire hillside—the old, ancient turf—cut to ribbons!"

His eyes searched my face. "We had grown so accustomed to looking up at Lady Grove," he said, smiling as if seeking sympathy. "This is shifting the center of gravity of our town."

"Things will readjust themselves and find their balance," I lied.

He eagerly seized on the phrase. "Of course," he said.

"They will find their balance... and settle down again. They must. Back to the old ways. It's bound to make things right again. What a comforting thought. Yes. After all, Lady Grove itself had to be built once upon a time... in the beginning, it too was artificial."

His eyes returned to my airplane. He tried to dismiss his heavier worries. "I'd think twice before trusting my body to that contraption," he remarked. "But I suppose a person gets used to the motion."

He bid me good morning and went on his way, his shoulders bowed, lost in thought....

He had kept the truth out of his mind for a long time, but that morning, the truth had forced its way into him in a form that could not be denied. This time, it wasn't just that changes were coming to his world. It was the realization that his entire world lay completely open, defenseless, conquered, and surrendered—doomed to change from root to branch, in both scale and form, as far as the eye could see.
