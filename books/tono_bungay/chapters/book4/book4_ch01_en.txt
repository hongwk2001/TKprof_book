# Chapter 1: The Crash of Tono-Bungay

I

That evening, I talked with my uncle in the Hardingham (a luxury hotel in London) for the last time. The atmosphere of the place had changed terribly. Instead of the usual crowd of persistent suitors demanding favors, there were only half a dozen unpleasant reporters waiting for an interview. Ropper, the big head porter, was still there, but now he was protecting my uncle from more than just time-wasting intruders. I found my uncle sitting alone in the inner office. He was pretending to work but was actually lost in deep worry. His face looked yellow and exhausted.

"Good lord!" my uncle said as soon as he saw me. "You've gotten so thin, George. It makes your scar look even clearer."

We looked at each other seriously for a while.

"The Quap (the radioactive mineral we were looking for)," I said, "is at the bottom of the Atlantic Ocean. There are unpaid bills... We have to pay the workers."

"Did you read the newspapers?"

"I read them all on the train."

"I am cornered," my uncle said. "I have been cornered for a whole week.... They are barking all around me.... And I am facing the music. I feel a bit tired."

He exhaled and wiped his glasses.

"My stomach is not what it used to be," he explained. "You realize during times like this that your stomach goes bad. How did all this happen, George? Because of the Marconi telegram (wireless telegram) you sent, I felt like I had been punched in the gut."

I briefly explained the situation to him. He nodded at my story, and when I finished, he poured something sticky from a medicine bottle into a small wine glass and drank it. I realized there were drugs present. Three or four small medicine bottles were sitting among the messy piles of papers, and a faintly familiar smell was floating in the room.

"Yes," my uncle said, wiping his lips and putting the cork back in the bottle. "You did your best, George. We just had bad luck."

He fell into thought while holding the bottle. "Sometimes luck is with you, and sometimes it isn't. Sometimes you have no luck. Then what happens? You burn up like grass in an oven! Whether you fight or not."

He asked a few questions, and then his mind returned to his own urgent problems. I tried to get a complete explanation of his current situation, but he wouldn't answer.

"Oh, I wish you had been by my side. I wish you had been here, George. I had too much on my hands. Your mind is very clear sometimes."

"What happened?"

"Oh! The stock market crashed! It’s a hellish situation."

"Yes, but how did it happen? Please remember I just got back from the sea."

"I am too worried to tell you right now. It is tangled up like a messy knot."

My uncle muttered to himself and thought darkly. Then he gathered himself and said,

"Besides... it is better for you to stay out of this. The situation is getting tight. Let them talk. Go down to Crest Hill (the huge mansion my uncle was building) and fly your airplane. That is your job."

For a while, his attitude brought back a strange feeling of anxiety in my mind.

I confess that my nightmare from Mordet Island (an island in Africa) came back to me. As I watched him, his hand reached for the medicine bottle again. "George, it's because of my stomach," he said.

"I have been fighting through the stomach pain. Everyone fights and endures something. Something is bound to break down, whether it's the head, the heart, or the liver. Tsk tsk. Something breaks down. Napoleon (the Emperor of France) also ended up like that. Throughout the Battle of Waterloo (Napoleon's final battle where he was defeated), his stomach was not normal! It was much worse than mine, it never ended."

As the medicine took effect, his depressed mood disappeared. His eyes sparkled. He started bragging. He tried to dress up the situation for me and make up for what he had just admitted. He compared this situation to the retreat from Russia (the fatal retreat that caused Napoleon's downfall). He said there was still a chance for Leipzig (a large battle where Napoleon tried to counterattack).

"This is a battle, George. A giant fight. We are fighting with millions of pounds on the line. I still have a chance. I still have a few cards left to play. I cannot tell you all my plans. It is like speaking at the exact moment the bell rings."

"You could tell me," I started to say.

"No, George. That is like asking to see an unborn baby. You have to wait. I know. In some ways, I know. But to put it into words... No! You have been away for too long. And everything has become complicated."

As his mood improved, my understanding of our disastrous situation grew deeper. It was clear that if I forced questions and explanations on him, I would only tie him tighter into the net trapping his mind. My thoughts jumped in another direction. "How is Aunt Susan?" I asked.

I had to repeat the question. My uncle's busy whispering lips stopped for a moment, and he answered in a tone like someone reciting an official script.

"Susan would want to join me in this battle. She would want to be here in London. But there are corners I have to turn alone." His eyes rested for a moment on the small medicine bottle next to him. "And a lot of things have happened."

"Go down now and talk to Susan," he said in a more direct voice. "I plan to go down tomorrow night."

He looked up as if he hoped our conversation would end there.

"For the weekend?" I asked.

"For the weekend. Thank God for weekends, George!"

II

My return to the house at Lady Grove (the old estate my uncle bought) was completely different from what I had imagined when I set out to sea full of radioactive mineral, dreaming of acquiring the perfect filament (the core part of a lightbulb). As I walked up the hill in the evening light, the summer silence felt like the silence of something freshly dead. There were no more workers hanging around, and no bicyclists riding on the main road.

There were clear signs of things being abandoned everywhere. From what my aunt told me, when construction on the Crest Hill mansion was stopped and the workers received their final pay, there was a touching and voluntary protest. They cheered for my uncle and booed the contractors and Lord Boom (the media tycoon who attacked my uncle's business).

I cannot remember exactly how my aunt and I greeted each other. I must have been very tired, but whatever impression I had disappeared from my memory. However, I clearly remember that we sat at the small round table near the large window overlooking the terrace, eating dinner and talking. I remember her talking about my uncle.

She asked how he was doing and if he looked healthy. "I wish I could help," she said. "But I have never helped him much, not even once. The way he worked was never my way. And... ever since he started getting very rich... he hid many things from me. In the old days... it was different...."

"He is over there... and I do not know what he is doing. He does not want to keep me close...."

"He hides more from me than anyone else. Even the servants won't tell me anything. They try to stop the worst newspapers, I mean Lord Boom's newspapers, from coming upstairs.... I guess they have backed him into a corner, George. Poor old Teddy! We are truly a poor old Adam and Eve! The official receivers (government officials who seize property) with flaming swords are driving us out of our Garden of Eden! I had hoped we would never have to wander again. Well... either way, it won't be at the Crest Hill mansion.... But it is harsh for Teddy. It must be a complete mess up there for him. Poor old man. I don't think we can help him. We would only make him worry. Eat some more soup while there is still some left, George...."

The next day was one of those days that leaves a strong, clear memory when normal daily life becomes a blur. Even now, I remember waking up in the large, familiar room that was always kept ready for me. I remember staring blankly at the chairs covered in chintz (floral print fabric), the widely spaced fine furniture, and the cedar trees outside the window, thinking that all of this had to end.

I had never been greedy for money and I never wanted to be rich, but now I felt a massive sense of coming loss. After breakfast, I read the newspapers with my aunt, and then I went up to see what Cothope had done with 'Lord Roberts Beta' (the airplane I was inventing). More sharply than ever before, I felt the rich brightness of the Lady Grove gardens and the dignity and vast peace of everything around me. It was a warm morning in late May, having gained all the glory of summer without losing the cheerful delicacy of spring. The bushes were bright with laburnum and lilac, the flowerbeds were blooming with daffodils and narcissus, and the shade was full of lilies of the valley.

I walked along the well-kept paths full of rhododendrons and passed through the private gate leading into the woods, where bluebells and common orchids were blooming beautifully. I had never perfectly tasted this sense of privilege and ownership before. And I told myself that all this had to end, all this had to end.

Neither my uncle nor I had made any preparations for a disaster. Everything we owned was tied up in a dangerous gamble, and now I had no doubt that our ruin would be complete. For the first time in my life since my uncle sent me that wonderful telegram, I had to worry about mankind's common anxiety: 'Employment.' I had to step off my magic carpet and walk into the real world once again.

And suddenly, I found myself standing at the crossroads where I had seen Beatrice for the first time in many years. It is strange, but as far as I can remember, I had not thought about her even once since landing in Plymouth. Without a doubt, she had filled the background of my mind, but I could not recall a single clear thought of her. I had been completely focused on my uncle and the financial collapse.

Now it hit me like a punch in the face: all of that had to end too!

Suddenly, I was filled with thoughts of her and a huge longing for her. What would she do when she realized our massive disaster? What would she do? How would she accept it? Realizing how little I knew filled me with deep shock....


I wondered if I might soon happen to run into her.

I walked on through the farm areas and out onto the open hills. There, I saw Cothope, my flight assistant, riding a new glider he had designed himself. He was gliding down with the wind toward my familiar old landing spot. Judging by its smooth, long flight pattern, it was a very excellent glider. "He's got some nerve," I thought to myself, "continuing the research like this. I wonder if he is keeping notes.... But all of this will have to come to an end soon."

He was genuinely happy to see me. "It's been a very strange situation," he said.

He had been staying there for a whole month without receiving any pay, like a man completely forgotten amidst the rush of our bankruptcy crisis.

"I just stayed behind and did what I could with the equipment. I had a little bit of my own money. So I said to myself, 'Well, you have the gear here, and there is no one around to boss you about. You will never get an opportunity like this again in your life. Why not see what you can achieve with it?'"

"How is the airship, Lord Roberts beta?" I asked.

Cothope raised his eyebrows. "I have refrained from flying it," he replied. "But it looks very impressive."

"My God!" I said. "I would love to fly it just once before we are completely ruined. Have you read the newspapers? Do you know that our company is going bankrupt?"

"Oh! I have read the papers. It is an absolute disgrace, sir, that scientific work like ours should depend on financial problems like that. With all due respect, sir, you and I ought to be funded by the State."

"You don't need to apologize," I said. "In theory, I have always been a Socialist of sorts myself. Let us go and take a look at the airship. How is it? Has it lost its gas?"

"It is only about a quarter full. That final oil coating you applied holds the gas beautifully. It hasn't lost even a cubic meter a week."

As we walked toward the hangars, Cothope returned to the topic of Socialism.

"I am glad to hear you are a Socialist, sir," he said. "It is the only truly civilized state of society. I have been a Socialist for some years now, ever since reading the 'Clarion' magazine. This world is a rotten, chaotic scramble. We scientists invent and build things, and then the business world plays silly games with them. We scientific people should take control and put an end to all this nonsense with finance and advertising. It is entirely foolish. It is a nuisance. Just look at what has happened to us!"

Even in its partially deflated state inside the hangar, the airship Lord Roberts beta was a magnificent sight to look up at. I stood side by side with Cothope looking at it, and I felt more painfully than ever that all of this had to end. I had a strong urge, like a boy wanting to do something forbidden, to use up what I had before the creditors arrived to take it all away. I also remember having a strange fantasy that if I could fly up into the air, it would serve as an announcement to my lover, Beatrice, that I had returned.

"Let's fill the airship with gas," I said decisively.

"Everything is ready," Cothope said, and then added as an afterthought, "unless they have cut off the gas supply."

I worked with Cothope all morning and found it very interesting, which helped me forget my other troubles for a while. However, thoughts of Beatrice slowly but steadily overwhelmed me. It turned into an irrational, sick longing to see her. I felt I could not wait for the airship to be filled; I had to go find her and see her immediately. I rushed everything forward, had lunch with Cothope, and then left him making a flimsy excuse so I could wander down through the woods toward Bedley Corner. I became gripped by miserable hesitation and a lack of confidence. Asking myself if I ought to approach her now, I reviewed all the social humiliations of my early years. Finally, around five o'clock, I visited the Dower House. I was greeted by their maid, Charlotte, with a forbidding glare and cold astonishment.

Both Beatrice and Lady Osprey were out.

A lingering dream of accidentally running into her entered my mind. I walked along the lane toward Woking, the exact lane where we had walked together in the wind and rain five months ago.

I wandered aimlessly for a time in our old footsteps, then swore to myself and turned back across the fields. Suddenly feeling a distaste for Cothope, I walked down the hill. At last, I found myself looking down at the massive, abandoned ruins of the unfinished Crest Hill mansion.

That sight twisted my thoughts into a new direction. The thought of my uncle rose to the surface again. Under the evening sunlight, the desolation of that failed enterprise—its vulgar, crude attempt at magnificence and its total absurdity—looked strangely and sadly empty. It was as idiotic as building the pyramids. I sat down on the steps, staring at the forest of scaffolding poles, the wasteland of brick walls, plaster, and carved stone, and the wilderness of torn-up soil, cart tracks, and rubbish dumps, as if I had never seen them before. It struck me suddenly as the most compact image and prime example of everything that passes for 'Progress': the wasteful spending driven by advertising, the aimless building up and tearing down, and the grand enterprises and empty promises of our era. This was our ultimate fruit; this was what my uncle and I had accomplished, following the fashion of our time. We were the leaders and representatives of this system, and this ruin was its most flourishing product. It seemed the solemn scroll of history had unfolded simply to reveal this futility, for an era defined by such meaninglessness....

"Great God!" I cried out. "Is this what Life is?"

Was it for this that armies trained, the law was enforced, and prisons did their duty? Was it for this that millions of people worked themselves to death in suffering? Just so that a few of us could build palaces we would never finish, construct billiard rooms beneath ponds, build foolish walls around irrational estates, speed around the world in motor-cars, invent flying machines, play golf and dozens of other silly ball games, crowd into noisy dinner parties, gamble, and turn our lives into one massive, depressing spectacle of mindless waste? That was how it struck me then, and for a while, I could think of no other way to interpret it. This was Life! It came to me like a revelation—both unbelievable and undeniable—showing the abysmal foolishness of our human existence.

III

I was startled out of these thoughts by the sound of footsteps behind me.

I turned around, half-hopeful—because a lover's imagination is foolish like that—and stopped in pure amazement. It was my uncle. His face was as deathly pale as I had seen it in my nightmares.

"Hello!" I said, staring at him. "Why aren't you in London?"

"It is all over," he said....

"Have you been officially declared bankrupt?"

"No!"

I stared at him for a moment and then stepped down from the stairs.

We swayed on our feet, and then he moved forward with a weak motion of his arms, like a man who cannot see clearly, and caught hold of the stairs to lean on them. For a moment, we were completely still. He made a clumsy gesture toward the massive, futile ruin below us and choked. I realized that his face was wet with tears and that his wet glasses were blinding him. He raised his little, fat hand and clumsily clawed his glasses off, fumbling ineffectively in his pockets for a handkerchief. And then, to my horror, as he clung to me, this small, world-weary old swindler began to weep out loud. He wasn't just sobbing or shedding a few tears; he was crying like a child. Oh, it was terrible!

"It is so cruel," he blubbered at last. "They kept asking me questions. They just kept asking me questions, George."

He struggled to speak and spluttered.

"Those merciless bullies!" he shouted. "Those bloody bullies!"

He stopped crying and suddenly started explaining things very rapidly.

"It is not a fair game, George. They deliberately exhaust you. And I am not well. My stomach is completely ruined. And I have caught a cold. I've always been prone to colds, and this one has settled on my chest. And then they demand that you speak up loudly. They bait you, and they bait you, and they bait you. It is sheer torture. The mental strain of it! You can't remember what you previously said. You are bound to contradict yourself. It is like being interrogated in Russia, George.... It isn't fair play.... I am a prominent man. I have sat next to that lawyer, Neal, at dinner parties. I've told him jokes! And now he is so bitter! He has set out to completely ruin me. He doesn't ask a civil question—he just bellows at me." He broke down crying again. "I have been bellowed at, I have been bullied, I have been treated like a dog. They are dirty scoundrels! Dirty scoundrels! I would rather be a street-corner card cheat than a barrister; I would rather sell cheap cat meat in the streets.

"They sprang things on me this morning, things I didn't expect at all. They rushed me! I thought I had everything under control, and then I was ambushed. By Neal! I gave Neal insider stock tips! I've helped Neal in the past....

"I couldn't swallow a single mouthful of food during the lunch hour. I simply couldn't face going back in. It's true, George—I couldn't face it. I told them I was going to get some fresh air. I slipped out, went down to the river Embankment, and took a boat to Richmond. God knows why I had that idea. When I got there, I rented a rowboat and rowed around on the river for a while. A bunch of young men and girls on the bank laughed at me for rowing in my shirtsleeves and a top hat. I suppose they thought I was on a pleasure trip. A fat lot of pleasure it was! I rowed around a bit and came back to shore. Then I came here, heading towards Windsor. And right now, they are sitting in London doing whatever they please with my case.... I don't care anymore!"

"But," I said, looking down at him in perplexity.

"That means you are absconding from the law. They will issue a warrant for your arrest."

"I don't understand," I said.

"It's all over, George—it's all over and finished.

"And to think I believed I would live in that grand place, George, and die as a lord! It really is a magnificent place. It would be an imperial estate if only someone had the sense to buy it and finish building it. That terrace—"

I stood there, looking at him and thinking over the situation.

"Look here!" I said. "What is all this about an arrest warrant? Are you sure they will get a warrant? I am sorry, uncle, but what exactly have you done?"

"Haven't I told you?"

"Yes, but they won't punish you too severely just for leaving. They will probably only bring you back to finish the rest of your examination."


My uncle remained silent for a while. Finally, he spoke with difficulty.
"The situation is worse than we thought. I have done something terrible. People are bound to find out. In fact, they already have."
"What happened?"
"I wrote some things down. I forged some documents."
I believe it was the first time in his life that my uncle genuinely felt and showed shame. Seeing him suffer like that broke my heart.
"We all make mistakes," I said. "It is just part of the cruel game the world forces us to play. If they are trying to arrest you, and you have no defense ready, we absolutely cannot let them take you."
"You are right. That is part of the reason I went to Richmond. But I never dreamed things would turn out this way."
His small, bloodshot eyes stared toward Crest Hill.
"That man Whitaker Wright (a businessman famous for financial fraud) made sure he had an escape plan ready. But I prepared nothing. George, now you understand the terrible trap I am in."

IV

I can still vividly remember the image of my uncle standing at the door. I can even recall the complex thoughts flowing through my mind as he spoke. I remember feeling a surge of pity and affection for him in his misery, and making a firm resolve to help him at any cost.
However, my memories after that become blurry again. I began to take action. I am certain I persuaded my uncle to leave everything to me, and I immediately made a plan and put it into motion. It seems that humans record the fewest memories when they are actively taking action. Once our impulses translate into concrete plans and movements, they are no longer recorded as memories.
I resolved to help my uncle escape immediately, using my airship named 'Lord Roberts β'. It was obvious my uncle would soon be a hunted man, and trying to escape using the standard routes to the European continent already seemed too dangerous. So, I had to quickly devise a way to escape across the sea as inconspicuously as possible. My long-standing desire to take at least one flight in the airship perfectly aligned with this situation. If we crossed the sea under the cover of night, abandoned the airship, and appeared as ordinary tourists walking through Normandy or Brittany, we could escape safely. At least, that was my main plan.

To keep my assistant Cothope from getting involved, I sent him on a fake errand to Woking. Then, I took my uncle to the airship hangar. I went to my aunt and honestly told her about our current situation. She handled it with surprising composure. We went to my uncle's dressing room and ruthlessly broke the locks. I gathered a pair of brown boots, a tweed suit, and a hat to create a plausible walking outfit, along with a small hunting bag for his belongings. Additionally, I packed a large motoring coat and several extra blankets from the hangar. I also found a flask of brandy, and my aunt made some sandwiches. I do not remember any servants appearing, nor do I remember where she got the ingredients for the sandwiches. We talked while we prepared. Looking back, it is surprising how much confidence we had in each other as we spoke.

"What did he do?" my aunt asked.
"Are you sure you want to know?"
"Thankfully, I have no conscience left!"
"I believe it is forgery."

A brief silence followed. "Can you carry these bundles?" she asked.
I lifted the bags.

"No woman has ever truly respected the law, not a single one," my aunt said. "The law is too foolish. It allows people to do all sorts of things... but when it matters, it restricts you like a crazy nurse overprotecting a child."

My aunt helped carry some blankets for me through the dusky shrubbery.

"People will just think we are taking a moonlight stroll," she said, gesturing toward the house. "I wonder what they would think if they knew we were criminals."
As if answering her, a loud, booming sound startled us both.
"Oh my!" she said. "It's the dinner gong!... But George, I wish I could help poor Teddy (my uncle). It is awful to think of him sitting there with dry, bloodshot eyes. And seeing me will only hurt him more, because of the things I have said... If I had known it would come to this, I would have kicked out everyone who criticized him. I hurt him too much. He probably did not think I meant it... Anyway, I will help as much as I can."

Something in her voice made me turn around, and I saw tears streaming down her face in the moonlight.

"Could that woman have helped?" she asked suddenly.
"That woman?"
"His mistress."
"My God!" I cried. "Help? People like that are of no help at all!"

"Tell me again what I need to do," my aunt said after a moment of silence.

I explained my communication plan again and what I needed her to do. I also gave her the contact information for a lawyer she could trust.

"But you must use your own judgment and act for yourself," I emphasized.
"To put it simply, it is a scramble. You must secure whatever you can for us and follow us as quickly as possible."

My aunt nodded.
She came right up to the front of the hangar, hesitated cautiously for a moment, and then left.

When I returned to the living room, my uncle was sitting in an armchair with his feet near the gas heater. He had turned it on and was slightly drunk on the whiskey I had left him. Perhaps because he was physically and mentally exhausted, he was acting quite cowardly.

"I left my medicine behind," he said.

He changed his clothes very slowly and reluctantly. I practically had to force him, pushing him into the airship and laying him down on the wicker floor before covering him with blankets. Taking off alone was quite clumsy. The airship scraped the roof of the hangar, bending one of the propeller blades. For a while, I was left hanging below the airship, and my uncle did not even reach out a hand to help me climb up. If it had not been for Cothope's 'slip anchor'—a device that slid along a rail—we never would have been able to take off.

V

The events of that night's escape on the 'Lord Roberts β' do not come to mind in order. Remembering that adventure is like flipping randomly through a photo album. One scene comes to mind, then another. We were both lying flat on a wicker floor because this airship had no elegant passenger amenities like a hot air balloon. I lay in the front, and my uncle lay behind me, in a position where he could barely see our flight. We were kept from falling out of the airship only by netting stretched between the steel supports. Standing up was completely impossible; we had to either crawl or lie flat on the basket floor. In the center of the airship were storage lockers made of a light, sturdy material called 'Aulite'. I laid my uncle down between these lockers and wrapped him in blankets. I wore sealskin motoring boots and gloves, with a fur coat over my tweed suit. I controlled the engine from the front using Bowden wires and levers.

The early part of the night was warm, and the moonlit landscapes of Surrey and Sussex were beautiful. We successfully flew south at a fast pace, repeatedly raising and lowering our altitude. Because the body of the airship was directly above me, I could not observe the clouds or the stars to gauge the weather. However, I could clearly tell that the wind had shifted from north to northeast and was growing stronger. After successfully testing the expansion and contraction of the airship's gas chamber a few times, I became confident in the flight capabilities of the 'Lord Roberts β'. So, to save fuel, I turned off the engine and let the airship drift with the wind, checking our progress by looking at the dim landscape below. My uncle lay very quietly behind me, staring blankly ahead and saying almost nothing. This allowed me to immerse myself in my own thoughts and feelings.

Whatever I was thinking back then has long faded from memory, but the sensations of that night remain as one continuous landscape. The countryside looked as if it were covered in snow. I could see blurry square patches, roads that looked like white ghosts, pitch-black pools and crevices, and houses lit up like glowing jewels. I remember seeing a train speeding across the landscape like a hastening, fiery caterpillar, and I could hear its clattering very clearly. Every town and street was buttoned up with streetlights. We came very close to the South Downs hills near Lewes. All the lights in the houses were out, and the people had gone to bed. We left the land slightly east of Brighton. By that time, Brighton was fast asleep, and the brightly lit seafront was completely empty. Then, I fully inflated the gas chamber to increase our altitude. I prefer flying high above the water.

I do not know exactly what happened during the night. I must have dozed off, and my uncle probably fell asleep as well. I remember hearing him mutter urgently to himself, or perhaps to an imaginary court, once or twice. But there is no doubt that the wind completely shifted to the east, and we were pushed far down the English Channel by a massive force. I remember the foolish bewilderment I felt when dawn broke, revealing an endless grey sea below, and I realized something was terribly wrong. I was so foolish that it was only after sunrise that I noticed the direction of the whitecaps on the sea and realized we were caught in a severe gale with strong easterly winds. Even then, instead of turning the airship southeast, I turned the engine back on and continued heading south. That course was destined to either crash us into the island of Ushant or blow us over the Bay of Biscay. I mistakenly believed we were east of Cherbourg, even though we had been pushed far to the west. Because of that belief, I kept turning the engine off and on again. It was not until late afternoon that I spotted the coast of Brittany to the southeast, and I finally woke up to how serious our situation was. I had been looking for the coast in the southwest, but found it in the southeast by accident. I turned the airship east and tried to fight the wind for a while, but I soon realized we had no chance against it head-on. So, I tried to climb higher where the wind seemed less intense and set a course for the southeast. It was only then that I realized what a massive storm we were in. We were flying west, or perhaps even northwest in the gusts, at a speed of fifty or sixty miles an hour.

Then began what is commonly called a desperate fight against the east wind.


The wind blew fiercely. People often call flying a "fight" with nature, but in reality, it was as far from a fight as simple sewing. The wind tried to push us to the west, and I tried my best to go to the east. The wind shook and hit us irregularly, but for about 12 hours, it was not unbearable. My only hope was for the wind to die down, and until then, to stay in the sky east of Cape Finisterre (a rocky peninsula on the west coast of Spain). The biggest danger was running out of gasoline. The time was long, anxious, and almost made me fall into deep thought. We were quite warm and only slowly getting hungry. Except for my uncle complaining a bit, sharing some philosophical thoughts, and making a fuss about having a fever, we hardly spoke. I was tired, irritated, and mainly worried about the engine. I had to resist the urge to crawl back and check the engine. I didn't want to risk shrinking the gas chamber because I was afraid of losing gas. In popular magazines, situations like this are described as people acting crazy and hysterical. They describe captains saving ships and generals leading battles while foaming at the mouth and shouting technical words in excitement. That might excite readers, but it is not true to life. In my experience, even in the most urgent moments, people handle things calmly. Neither my uncle nor I yelled or made jokes. We just sat there stiffly.

My uncle sat in his place and complained that his stomach hurt. Sometimes he would ramble on about his money situation or curse at Neal. Our basket kept creaking, and as the night got deeper, we became terribly cold.

I must have fallen asleep briefly. When I woke up suddenly, it was still dark. We were far south of a lighthouse that stood in front of the lights of a big city. What woke me up was the sound of the engine stopping. We were being pushed back to the west. At that moment, I felt a terrifying thrill of life. I crawled forward, pulled the cord for the gas release valve, and made my uncle crawl forward too. We let out the gas as we fell like a clumsy glider toward the blurry gray land.

I think I saw the lights of Bordeaux (a port city in southwestern France) when it was very dark. But we definitely crashed in the cold, uncertain light of early morning. Mimizan, the town near where we landed, was 50 miles (about 80 kilometers) away from Bordeaux. When we landed, a strong gust of wind caught the airship, and I tried to hold on to it but let go. The airship flew quickly over the sand dunes and probably fell into the sea and sank. There was never any news of anyone seeing the airship again.

In the early morning, on the cold sand dunes, I had to take care of my uncle. He was shivering badly and had a fever. We went to a nearby farmhouse, pretended to be lost travelers, and got some warm coffee. Then we took a train to a small border town and stayed in a small inn. My uncle was very sick with a fever and pneumonia. He was delirious, constantly counting numbers or cursing at Neal. I called a doctor and hired a nurse to take care of him. In this small, quiet country town in France, my uncle, who was once rich and full of confidence, was now lying on his sickbed. He was sweating and his skin had turned yellow under the shadow of death.


His face looked unfamiliar because of his new beard, and his nose was pinched and thin. He had never looked so small. In a strained whisper, he talked to me about important things—about what his life had been and where he was going. Poor little man! That final phase of his life felt completely disconnected from everything else. It was as if he had crawled out of the ruins of his career to look around before he died.

During the breaks in his delirium, his mind was quite clear. He knew he was almost certainly dying. In a way, this lifted a huge burden off his shoulders. He didn’t have to face Neal anymore. There were no more escapes, no more hiding, and no punishments waiting for him.

"It has been a great career, George," he said, "but I shall be glad to rest. Glad to rest!... Glad to rest."

He mostly thought about his career, and I'm glad to say he usually felt satisfied and proud of it. During his delirious moments, he would often exaggerate this pride and talk about his greatness. He would pull at his bedsheets, stare straight ahead, and whisper broken sentences that I could barely hear.

"What is this great place? These high towers touching the clouds... these peaks?... Ilion. Reaching to the sky... Ilion House, the home of one of our great merchant princes... Terrace above terrace. Reaching the heavens... Kingdoms that Caesar never knew... A great poet, George. Zzzz. Kingdoms Caesar never knew... Under entirely new management.

"Greatness... Millions... Universities... He stands on the terrace—on the upper terrace—directing—directing—by the globe—directing—the trade."

At times, it was hard to tell when he stopped making sense and when his delirium began. His secret motivations and empty fantasies were all laid bare. I sometimes think that human life is messy and careless behind closed doors, only cleaning itself up to look proper when interacting with others. I suspect that all the unspoken thoughts in our minds are somewhat like delirium and madness. Certainly, nothing but dreams and broken fantasies came from his dry, trembling lips.

Sometimes he raved and threatened Neal. "What investments has he made?" he said. "Does he think he can escape me?... If I went after him... Ruin. Ruin... You'd think *I* had stolen his money."

Other times, his mind went back to our airship flight. "It's too long, George, too long and too cold. I'm too old for this... You know you're not saving me—you're killing me."

Towards the end, it became clear that people had found out who we were. I learned that the press, especially the newspapers owned by Boom, had been aggressively hunting for us. Although no reporters reached us before my uncle died, I could feel the storm of attention building up. The story even made it into the popular French newspapers. The people around us started looking at us curiously, and new faces showed up outside the tiny, stuffy room where my uncle was struggling to stay alive. The young doctor insisted on holding medical consultations, a car arrived from Biarritz, and suddenly, strangers with questioning eyes began offering help. Even though no one said anything directly, I knew we were no longer seen as just ordinary middle-class tourists. I could feel the heavy reputation of big finance and criminal infamy following me around. Wealthy, important locals came to the inn to ask questions, the local priest offered to help, people watched our windows, and they stared at me whenever I went out. Then, we were ambushed by a little English clergyman and his capable wife. Dressed in strict black church clothes, they swooped down on us like determined vultures from the nearby village of Saint Jean de Pollack.

The clergyman was a strange man who seemed to bounce between small English country towns and running church services at hotels abroad. He was a nervous, stubborn little man with patchy facial hair, glasses, a red nose, and old black clothes. He was clearly amazed by my uncle's immense wealth and suspected he knew who we really were. He was overly eager to help and tried hard to be polite. Since I was busy reviewing the massive financial ruin using papers I got from Biarritz, I accepted his help with watching my uncle. I had been away from traditional religion for so long that I didn't realize he might try to push his beliefs on my dying uncle. However, I quickly noticed this when the clergyman got into a polite but intense argument with our Basque landlady. She wanted to hang a cheap crucifix over the bed where my uncle could see it—and I noticed he was indeed looking at it.

"Good Lord!" I cried. "Is that still going on?"

That night, the little clergyman was watching my uncle. In the early morning hours, he caused a huge scene by falsely claiming my uncle was dying. He woke up the whole house. I don't think I'll ever forget that scene. It started with a knock on my bedroom door just after I had fallen asleep, followed by his voice:

"If you want to see your uncle before he goes, you must come now."

When I reached the stuffy little room, it was crowded and lit only by three flickering candles. It felt like I had stepped back into the eighteenth century. My poor uncle lay there in a messy pile of blankets, looking incredibly exhausted from life, tired and mumbling. The little clergyman was trying to hold his hand and get his attention, repeating over and over:

"Mr. Ponderevo, Mr. Ponderevo, it is all right. It is all right.

"Only Believe! 'Believe on me, and ye shall be saved'!"

Nearby stood the young doctor holding one of those cruel and pointless modern injection needles, keeping my uncle barely alive for no real reason. The nun hovered sleepily in the background, having missed the time to give a dose of medicine. On top of that, the landlady had brought in her old mother and her mentally impaired husband. There was also a bulky man in a grey suit who looked important—I have no idea who he was or why he was there. The doctor might have explained it to me in French, but I didn't understand. They were all there, tired from being awake in the middle of the night, dressed carelessly, and entirely focused on my uncle's fading life. It was a bizarre public spectacle of death, with strange shadows cast by the candles. Everyone seemed eager to watch him die. The doctor stood, while the others sat on chairs the landlady had brought in for them.

But my uncle ruined their grand finale—he didn't die.

I made the clergyman move from the chair next to the bed, and he began pacing around the room.

"I think," he whispered to me mysteriously, "I believe—it is well with his soul."

I heard him trying to translate his typical Protestant religious phrases into French for the bulky man in the grey suit. Then, he knocked a glass off the table and scrambled on the floor to pick up the broken pieces. From the start, I doubted my uncle was actually dying right then. I quickly whispered with the doctor, then turned to get some champagne, nearly tripping over the clergyman. He had kneeled at a chair the landlady brought and was praying out loud, "Oh, Heavenly Father, have mercy on this thy Child..." I quickly shoved him up and out of the way. A minute later, he was kneeling at another chair, praying again, and blocking the nun who was bringing me a corkscrew. His behavior reminded me of a famous quote about the pathetic cries of a drowning kitten. When he found a third empty chair, it almost felt like he was playing a game.

"Good Heavens!" I said. "We have to get these people out of here," and I forcefully pushed them out.

I momentarily forgot all my French, so I used hand gestures to drive them out. I then opened the window, which shocked everyone. I let them know the death scene was delayed. In fact, my uncle didn't die until the following night.

I didn't let the little clergyman near him again, and I watched closely to see if he had disturbed my uncle's peace of mind. But my uncle seemed fine. He only mentioned "that parson chap" once.

"Didn't bother you?" I asked.

"Wanted something," he said.

I stayed quiet, listening closely to his mumbling. I thought I heard him say, "They wanted too much." His face wrinkled up like a child about to cry. "You can't get a safe six per cent.," he said. For a moment, I had a wild suspicion that the clergyman had been asking him about money rather than his soul, but I think that was unfair. The little clergyman was genuinely honest. My uncle was just talking about that class of people in general.

However, maybe those intense conversations triggered something deep inside my uncle's brain—thoughts that his obsession with worldly things had hidden for a long time. Near the very end, he suddenly became clear-headed. He was very weak, and his voice was faint, but clear.

"George," he said.

"I'm here," I said, "right beside you."

"George. You have always been the one who understood science. George. You know better than I do. Is—is it proven?"

"What is proven?"

"Either way?"

"I don't understand."

"Does death end everything? After so much—after such splendid beginnings. Is there somewhere else? Something else?"

I stared at him in shock. His sunken eyes looked incredibly serious.


"What do you expect?" I said in surprise.

He did not answer. "Aspirations," he whispered. He started talking to himself, ignoring me. "Trailing clouds of glory," he quoted from Wordsworth's poetry. "A first-rate poet, first-rate... George was always cold. Always."

Silence fell for a long time.

Then he gestured that he wanted to speak.

"I think, George."

As I bowed my head, he tried to reach out and put his hand on my shoulder. I propped him up a little against his pillows and listened.

"George, I think there is always... something in me that will never die."

He looked at me as if the decision depended entirely on me.

"I think," he said, "...there is something."

Then his mind wandered for a moment. "It's just a tiny little link," he whispered almost pleadingly, and lay very still, but soon became anxious again.

"Some other world..."

"Perhaps," I said. "Who knows?"

"There must be some other world."

"You wouldn't have the same opportunities to start a business there," I said.

"No, I suppose not."

He fell silent. I sat leaning toward him, lost in my own thoughts, while the nursing sister began struggling with the window latch again. For a while he gasped for air... It seemed so absurd that he had to suffer like this. Poor, foolish little man!

"George," he whispered, and his weak, small hand reached out. "Perhaps..."

He didn't say anything more, but I saw in his eyes that he felt he had asked me a question.

"Yes, I think so too," I said firmly.

"Are you sure?"

"Oh, practically sure," I said, and he seemed to try to squeeze my hand. I sat there, holding his hand tightly, trying to think of what seed of immortality might be found in his whole being, what kind of soul was in him to step out into that bleak, vast universe. Strange thoughts came to my mind... Except for a brief struggle to breathe, he lay still for a long time, and I kept wiping his mouth and lips.

I fell into a deep pit of thought. At first, I didn't notice the change slowly spreading across his face. He leaned back on his pillow, made a faint wheezing sound, and then stopped. Soon, very quietly, he passed away, greatly comforted by my reassurance. I don't know exactly when he died. Before I knew it, his hand had gone limp. Suddenly, with a start, I realized his mouth had fallen open and he was dead...

Chapter 8

It was a dark night when I left his deathbed and walked back to my inn along the winding street of Luzon.

That walk back to the inn remains a completely separate experience in my memory. Inside the room, there had been the hushed bustling of women, flickering lights, and the petty post-mortem chores performed on that strangely exhausted body that had once been my energetic, passionate little uncle. To me, those chores felt annoying and disrespectful. I slammed the door and stepped out into the warm, foggy drizzle of the village street, where blurry lights dotted the vast darkness, and not a single soul was in sight. That warm veil of fog created a feeling of being completely cut off from the world. Even the houses along the road looked as if they belonged to another world through the fog. The occasional distant barking of dogs broke the silence of the night. Everyone here kept dogs because they lived close to the border.

Death!

It was a rare moment of release, feeling as if I were briefly walking along the outer edges of life. I felt similar to how I sometimes feel after a play has ended. My uncle's entire life looked like something familiar and already finished to me. Like a play I had walked away from or a book I had closed, it was over. I thought about the ambition and promotions, the noise of London, the crowded and diverse people our lives had passed through, the public rallies, the excitements, the dinners, and the arguments, and suddenly it felt as if none of these things were real.

The realization that none of this existed came to me like a new discovery.

I have thought of and called life a fleeting illusion before and since, but I had never felt that truth as deeply as I did that night... We had parted. The two of us, who had been together for so long, were now separated. But I knew there was no end for him or for me. He had died a dream death and ended his dream. His painful dream was over. To me, it felt as though I had died as well. If pain, desire, the beginning, and the end were all just an illusion, what did it matter? There was no reality other than this lonely road, this extremely solitary road, which I had to walk down somewhat confused and quite exhausted...

Part of the fog turned into a large mastiff dog that came toward me. The dog stopped, growled, prowled around me, barked roughly, and then quickly dissolved back into the fog.

My mind drifted back to the ancient beliefs and fears of humanity. My doubts and disbelief slipped away from me like loose clothing. I purely wondered what dogs were barking along the path of that other walker in the dark, and what shapes and lights might vaguely appear around him as he walked his true path forever, leaving behind our last encounter in this world.

Chapter 9

The last person to arrive among those gathered around my uncle's deathbed was my aunt. When there was absolutely no hope of my uncle surviving, I cast aside all remaining secrets and telegraphed her directly. But she arrived too late to see him alive. She saw him strangely different from his usual talkative and lively self, looking unfamiliar, stiff, peaceful, and still.

"It doesn't look like him," she whispered, awestruck by this strange dignity.

I mostly remember her standing, talking, and crying on the bridge below the old castle. We had shaken off a few amateur reporters from Biarritz and walked together through Port Luzon under the hot morning sun. There, for a while, we stood leaning against the bridge railing, looking at the distant peaks and deep blue ridges of the Pyrenees. We didn't say anything for a long time, and then she began to speak.

"Life is a cruel joke, George!" she began. "When I used to mend your socks in Wimblehurst, who would have thought this would be the end of the story? It feels so far away now. That little pharmacy, which was his and my first home. The glow of the glass bottles, those huge, colorful bottles! Do you remember how the light shone on the mahogany drawers? Those little gold letters! Labels like 'Ol Amjig' and 'S'nap'! I remember it all. It was bright and sparkling like a Dutch painting. It was so real! Like it happened yesterday. And yet, here we are in a dream. You've grown into a man, and I've become an old woman, George. And poor little Teddy, who was always rushing about and chattering, always making so much noise... Oh!"

She choked up, and tears flowed endlessly. She wept aloud, and I was glad to see her cry.

She stood leaning against the bridge, holding a tear-soaked handkerchief in her clenched hand.

"If only we could spend just one hour in that old pharmacy again. Listening to him talk. Before all this happened. Before business and other things swallowed him up. And made a fool out of him.

"People shouldn't be so tempted by business and things like that...

"They didn't hurt him, did they, George?" she asked suddenly.

For a moment, I was taken aback.

"I mean, here," she said.

"No," I lied firmly, suppressing the memory of the foolish syringe needle I had seen the young doctor using.

"George, I wonder if they'll let him talk as much as he wants in Heaven..."

She looked at me. "Oh! George, my dear George, my heart hurts so much I don't know what I'm saying or doing. Let me borrow your arm to lean on. It's so good to have you here, to be able to lean on you... Yes, I know you care for me. That's why I'm talking like this. We've always loved each other, but we never spoke of it. You understand, and I understand. But this whole thing has shattered my heart and torn it to pieces, and everything I've kept inside is pouring out. It's true that in the end he wasn't a great husband to me. But he was my child, George. He was my child, and all my children. My foolish child. Life threw him around on my behalf, and I couldn't say a single word about it. Not one word. Right before my eyes, life blew him up like an old bag and then smashed him. I was smart enough to see it happening, but not smart enough to stop it. So all I could do was mock him. I just had to do my best to endure it. Like most people. Like most of us... But it wasn't fair, George. It really wasn't fair. Life and Death—these huge, serious things—why couldn't they just leave him alone, along with his lies and his ways? If only we could see the lightness of it all...

"Why couldn't they just leave him alone?" she repeated in a whisper as we walked toward the inn.
