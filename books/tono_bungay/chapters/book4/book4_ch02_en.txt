Book 4 Chapter 2, LOVE AMONG THE WRECKAGE,

I

When I returned to London, the fact that I was implicated in my uncle's flight and death—an event symbolizing the collapse of a corrupt financial system—made me both infamous and rather famous for a time. For two weeks, I stayed in London taking responsibility, "facing the music" as my uncle would have said in his lifetime, and making things easier for my aunt. The consideration the world showed me is astonishing even when I think about it now. At that time, it had become glaringly obvious that my uncle and I were merely specimens of a modern band of thieves who had wasted the public's savings out of a reckless greed to build an enterprise. In a way, however, my uncle's death seemed to stir public sympathy for me, and the details of the escape I had undertaken—my reckless adventure to steal radioactive mud from Mordet Island—seemed to capture the public's imagination. People considered my escape a far more daring and difficult feat than it actually was, and I did not feel the need to write to the newspapers to state my personal assessment and correct the truth. There is no doubt that people much prefer a bold and enterprising appearance to simple honesty. The public did not disbelieve that I was a core conspirator who led my uncle's financial fraud, yet they still treated me favorably. I even received permission from the bankruptcy trustee to use my chalet, which served as my laboratory, for two weeks. There, I was able to organize the massive pile of documents, calculations, work notes, and airplane blueprints that I had left in disarray when I left for my impulsive raid on the radioactive mud heaps of Mordet.

I was there alone. Cothope, my airplane building assistant, had been arranged to work for the Ilchesters, a traditional aristocratic family. I am currently building destroyers for that family. They wanted Cothope immediately, and because he was short on money, I let him go and stayed alone, maintaining a very philosophical attitude.

However, it was difficult to focus on aeronautics and flight research. I had been away from my research for over half a year, and that half year was packed with chaotic and intense events. For a while, my brain completely rejected the delicate flight problems of balance and adjustment. My mind only wanted to think about my uncle dying with his jaw hanging slack, the reluctant tears my aunt shed, dead Black men and pestilence-ridden swamps, and the glaring realities of cruelty, pain, life, and death. Furthermore, I was exhausted by the immense amount of ledgers and paperwork piled up at Hardingham, the headquarters of my uncle's financial empire, and our raid on Lady Grove—the aristocratic mansion we had taken over—was merely a brief interlude. And above all else, there was Beatrice, my first love and the unreachable upper-class woman.

On the morning of the second day, I was sitting on the veranda recalling past memories and struggling to focus on Cothope's overly concise pencil notes. Suddenly, Beatrice appeared from behind the laboratory on horseback and pulled her reins to a stop. Riding a large black horse, she had a slightly flushed face from the ride.

I did not stand up immediately. I stared at her. "It's you!" I said.

She gazed at me steadily. "Yes, it's me," she answered.

I had no time for pleasantries. I got up from my seat and bluntly asked the question that popped into my head.

"Whose horse is that?" I asked.

She looked me straight in the eyes. "It belongs to Carnaby," she answered, referring to the wealthy, corrupt aristocrat who possessed her.

"How did you get here? This way?"

"The wall collapsed."

"Collapsed? Already?"

"The wall between the plantations collapsed significantly."

"So you just rode through there and happened to come all the way here?"

"I saw you yesterday. So I rode over to see you." Before I knew it, I had stepped closer to her, looking up at her face.

"I am nothing more than a ruined man, a mere trace of who I was," I said.

She did not answer, only looking at me firmly with a strange look of possessiveness in her eyes.

"As you know, I am now the sole survivor of that massive collapse. I am currently tumbling through the massive scaffolding of the social system, falling to the bottom.... Whether I survive intact at the bottom and find freedom, or fall through the cracks into the darkness where I disappear from people's sight for a year or two, is entirely up to luck."

"Because of the sun," she said irrelevantly, "your face is burnt.... I'm getting down."

She swung her body down into my arms, and we stood facing each other.

"Where is Cothope?" she asked.

"He left."

Her gaze shifted back and forth between the laboratory and me. We stood very close, feeling incredibly intimate, yet simultaneously feeling an astonishing sense of separation.

"I've never seen this cottage of yours before," she said. "I'd like to take a look."

She wrapped her horse's reins around a veranda post, and I helped her tie them.

"Did you go to Africa and get what you wanted?" she asked.

"No," I answered. "I lost my ship."

"So you lost everything?"

"Yes, absolutely everything."

She walked into the living room of the cottage ahead of me, and I saw that she was gripping her riding crop very tightly in her hand. She looked around for a moment, then looked at me.

"It looks comfortable," she said.

Our eyes were having a completely different conversation than the words coming out of our mouths. A dark, gloomy heat enveloped us and drew us together, but an unusual awkwardness kept us apart. After a moment of silence, she gathered herself and began examining my furniture.

"You have chintz curtains. I thought men weren't meticulous enough to hang curtains without a woman. Ah, of course, your aunt must have done it! And a sofa, a brass fireplace guard, and... is that a pianola? That must be your desk. I always thought men's desks would be messy and covered in dust and cigarette ash."

She stepped lightly toward my color prints and small bookshelf. Then she went over to the pianola. I watched her carefully.

"Does this machine play as well?" she asked.

"What do you mean?" I asked.

"I'm asking if this machine plays."

I had been lost in thought, but I snapped back to reality.

"It's like being played by a gorilla that has a musical sense but fingers all the same length. And it has a kind of soul, too.... That machine is my entire world of music."

"What kind of pieces do you usually play?"

"When I want to clear my head while working, I listen to Beethoven. He is... exactly the way I always want to work. Sometimes I listen to Chopin or other composers, but mostly Beethoven. Yes, Beethoven."

Silence fell between us again. She forced herself to speak.

"Play something for me." She turned away from me and looked at the music roll rack, became interested, picked up the first part of the Kreutzer Sonata, and then hesitated. "No," she said. "Let's do this one!"

She handed me Brahms's Piano Concerto No. 2, Op. 58, and curled up on the sofa, watching me as I slowly prepared to play....

"Say," she said when I had finished playing. "That's truly wonderful. I didn't know a machine like that could play like that. My heart is really stirring..."

She approached, looking down at me. "I should hold more concerts," she said abruptly, hovering nervously in front of the filing cabinet with a laugh. "Now... what should we listen to next?" She picked another piece by Brahms. And then we came to the Kreutzer Sonata. It is truly bizarre how Tolstoy, the great Russian writer, imbued that piece with countless suggestions, corrupted it, and turned it into an intimate symbol full of scandal. When I played the first part of that piece, she approached the pianola and hesitated above me. I sat stiffly... waiting.

Suddenly, she grabbed my bowed head and kissed my hair. She cupped my face with both hands and kissed my lips. I also put both arms around her, and we kissed together. I sprang from my seat and embraced her.

"Beatrice!" I called out. "Beatrice!"

"My love," she whispered, almost breathlessly, holding me tight. "Oh! My love!"

II

Like everything else in this massive process of social dissolution we live in, love too is something adrift, a fruitless fragment broken off from existing networks of relationships. The reason I confess this love story here is because it is so utterly out of nowhere and alien, and it is so astonishing that it has absolutely no meaning and is nothing other than itself. This love shines in my memory like a bright, chance flower blooming suddenly in the middle of ruins left by a catastrophe. For nearly two weeks, the two of us met and made love. This powerful passion—fettered, crippled, sterilized, and corrupted by our purposeless civilization—seized me once again, filling me with passionate ecstasy and solemn joy. But as you know, all of it was vain and purposeless. Once again, I felt the conviction: "This is the only thing that matters. Nothing else is as important as this." We were infinitely serious in the limited happiness we could enjoy. I have absolutely no memory of laughter between us.

From that first meeting in the cottage until we parted, that time lasted for exactly twelve days.

Except for the very end, those days were the peak of summer, and the moon was waxing. We met recklessly, day by day. At first, we were so focused on each other, so absorbed in expressing ourselves and understanding the other, that we hardly cared how our relationship might look to others. We met almost publicly.... We talked about ten thousand things, and about ourselves. We loved. We made love. No writing of mine can properly express those transformed hours. The facts are nothing. Everything we touched, even the humblest things, became glorious. How can I describe the unadorned tenderness, the ecstasy, the feeling of completely possessing one another? I sit at my desk now, thinking of things that cannot be put into words.

I came to know too much about love, and now I know what love ought to be. Scarred and stained, we loved; we parted basely and inevitably, but at least I encountered true love.

In the reedy shallows stretching out from the pine-shaded Woking Canal, I remember her beginning to talk about the things that had happened to her before she met me again, as we sat in the canoe....

She told me many stories, and those stories perfectly connected and welded together the fragmented pieces in my memory, creating the illusion that I had already known her stories all along. Of course, aside from a flashing doubt now and then, I had neither known nor suspected the truth.

She showed me how life had shaped her. She spoke about her girlhood after I had come to know her. "We were poor, we tried to hide our poverty, and we struggled just to survive. We used to kill time visiting here and there. I should have gotten married. The opportunities given to me weren't very good ones. I didn't like those men."

She paused for a moment. "And then Carnaby, the wealthy aristocratic sponsor, came along."

I stayed still. She looked down, dipping one finger lightly into the water as she spoke.

"A person gets bored, bored beyond redemption. Especially when you wander around those incredibly expensive, massive mansions. The scale of them is immense. Women try to be useful to other women, and they have to act sweetly toward men. You have to dress up, too.... You're given food, exercise, and leisure time. That leisure time that feels like a sin if you don't fill it, that vast space, those empty opportunities. Carnaby is different from other men. He is a bigger figure.... People wander around making love. Everyone makes love. I did too.... And I'm not the type to do anything halfway."

She paused.

"Did you know?" she asked, looking up very calmly. I nodded.

"Since when?"

"During those last few days.... Actually, it didn't feel very important. Though I was a little surprised."

She looked at me quietly. "Cothope knew too," she said. "Instinctively. I could feel it, too."

"In the past," I began, "this fact would have been a massive issue. But now—"

"Nothing matters," she said, finishing my sentence. "I felt I just had to tell you this. I wanted you to understand why I didn't welcome you with open arms and marry you. I loved you." She paused. "I have loved you ever since the day I kissed you in the bracken. It's just... I had forgotten."

And suddenly, she buried her face in her hands and sobbed violently.

"I had forgotten, I had forgotten," she wailed, before falling quiet....

I splashed the paddle in the water. "Look here!" I said. "Forget it again! I am now a ruined man. Marry me."

Without raising her head, she shook it.

We stayed still for a long time. "Marry me!" I whispered.

She looked up, twisting a strand of hair back, and answered in an emotionless voice.

"I wish I could. Regardless, we had this time. It was a really good time... Was it for you, too? I couldn't show you everything I have to give. It's a poor gift, but considering what it means and what it could have been... But now we are nearing the end of it."

"Why?" I asked. "Marry me! Why should the two of us—"

"Do you think," she said, "that I could summon the courage to go to you and become a normal wife? While you work and live in poverty?"

"Why not?" I said.

She looked at me earnestly, her finger extended. "Do you really think that about me? Haven't you seen... all of me?"

I hesitated.

"I have never once sincerely thought about marrying you," she said firmly. "Never once. I did fall in love with you from the start, but when you became a successful man, I promised myself I wouldn't. I loved you passionately, and because you were so foolish, I even wavered at times. But I knew I wasn't a good enough woman. What could I have been to you? Just a woman with bad habits and bad associations, a tainted woman. And what could I do for you, or what kind of presence could I be? If I don't even qualify to be a rich man's wife, I certainly don't qualify to be a poor man's wife. Forgive me for lecturing you with common sense right now. But I wanted to tell you this truth, somehow."

She stopped speaking when I made a gesture. As I raised my body, the canoe rocked with my movement.

"I don't care." I said. "I want to marry you and make you my wife!"

"No," she said. "Don't ruin things. That's impossible!"

"Impossible!"

"Think about it! I can't even brush my own hair by myself! Can you get a maid for me?"

"Good God!" I shouted, utterly bewildered. "Can't you learn to brush your own hair for me? You say you love a man—"

She waved both hands at me. "Don't ruin things," she cried. "I gave you everything I had. Everything I could give. If I could do it, if I were a woman worthy of doing it, I would have. But I am a broken, corrupted woman, and you are a penniless man. When we are making love, we are lovers, but think of the tremendous gap in our habits, mindsets, will, and upbringing when we are not making love. Think about it. And don't think about it! Don't think about it yet. We spent stolen time. We might still have a little more time left!"

Suddenly, with a deep darkness in her eyes, she dropped to her knees and came toward me. "Who cares if the canoe flips?" she cried. "If you say one more word, I'll kiss you. And I'll hold you tight and sink to the bottom.

"I'm not afraid of that. Not afraid at all. I will die with you. Choose how you want to die, because I will gladly die with you. Please, listen to me! I love you. I will always love you. Because I love you, I don't want to degrade into a dirty, familiar existence rolling in the mud with you. I gave you everything I could. I had everything I could have.... Tell me." She came closer. "Was I like the dusk to you, like a warm dusk? Is the magic still there? Listen to the sound of the water falling from your paddle. Look at the warm evening light in the sky. Who cares if the canoe flips? Come closer to me. Oh, my love! Come closer. Like that."

She pulled me in, and our lips met.

III

I asked her once again to marry me.

It was our last morning together, and we met very early, around dawn, knowing we had to part. The sun didn't rise that day. The sky was heavily overcast, the morning air was chilly, illuminated by a clear, cold, soulless light. A dense dampness weighed down the air, as if it would turn to rain at any moment. When I think of that morning, the texture of rain-soaked grey ash always comes to mind.

Beatrice had changed, too. The spring vitality had vanished from her movements. The realization that she too would one day grow old struck me for the first time. she had become the same flesh as any other ordinary person. The softness was gone from her voice and demeanor, and the dusky magic her presence radiated had vanished. I saw all this perfectly clearly, and I felt sorry about it and pitied her. But those facts did not alter or diminish my love for her in the slightest. After we exchanged half a dozen awkward sentences, I bluntly brought up the main point.

"Well," I cried out. "Will you marry me?"

"No," she replied. "I will keep my life."

I asked her to marry me in a year. She shook her head.

"This world is not that tough a place." I said. "Despite the disasters I'm facing right now. I now know how to handle the world. If I could only work for you... in a year I could be a prosperous man."

"No," she said. "I'll put it brutally. I am going to return to Carnaby."

"But—!" I wasn't angry. I felt no jealousy, no wounded pride, no sense of injustice. I only felt an ashen desolation and a desperate disconnect.

"Look here," she said. "I stayed up all night. Every night. I thought about this issue every single moment we weren't together. I'm not answering you on impulse. I love you. I love you. I could repeat this tens of thousands of times. But we are here now, and—"

"Spending the rest of our lives together." I said.

"We won't be together. We are together now. We have been together. We are full of memories, and I will never forget a single one of them."

"Neither will I."

"So I want to end it in this state and leave it as it is. You know, my love, what else could we do?"

She turned her pale face to me. "Everything I know about love, everything I dreamed or learned about love, I poured out for you over these past few days. You think we could live together and continue loving. No! I won't engage in vain repetitions for your sake. You've had the best part of me, all of me. After all this is over, do you want us to meet again in some place like London or Paris, scrambling to a miserable dress shop or meeting secretly in a private room?"

"No," I said. "I want you to marry me. I want you to play the game of life with me as an honest woman. Come and live with me. Be my wife, my partner. Bear my children."

I looked at her pale, frowning face, and I felt like I might still be able to persuade her. I stammered.

"Good God! Beatrice!" I cried. "But this is cowardice and folly! Are you afraid of life? Someone as great as you! No matter what happened in the past, no matter who we were in the past, what does it matter? We have a new world before us! Let's start clean and fresh together. We'll fight to the end! I am not a simple lover who will just stand by when you go down the wrong path. I will speak clearly and correct our differences to the very end. The single thing I want, the single thing I need is... to have you, more of you, and even more of you! This love-making is... just love-making. It's merely a part of our lives, just an incident—"

She shook her head and abruptly cut me off. "This is everything." she said.

"Everything!" I protested.

"I am wiser than you. Wiser beyond words." She turned her eyes to me, and tears were shining in them.

"I wish you wouldn't say anything. Except for what you're saying right now," she said. "But that's nonsense, my love. You know it's nonsense even as you say it."

I tried to maintain a heroic attitude, but she wouldn't listen to me.

"It's no use," she cried out almost nervously. "This little world made us who we are now. Don't you know? Can't you see what kind of woman I am? I know how to make love. I know how to make love and be loved beautifully. My love, don't blame me. I gave you everything I had. If I had anything more than that... I've experienced it over and over and thought about it thoroughly. My head aches this morning, my eyes ache too.

"The light inside me has gone out, and I am a sick, tired woman. But I am speaking wisdom. Bitter wisdom. I cannot be any kind of helper to you, any kind of wife, any kind of mother. I am ruined.

"I was ruined by this wealthy, lazy way of living. Every habit is wrong, every taste is wrong. The world is wrong. A person can be ruined by wealth just as much as by poverty. If I could have, if I wasn't certain that I would collapse and become a burden before we even went the first half-mile of this journey, wouldn't I have faced life with you? Here I am, condemned to hell! In hell! But I won't drag you into hell too. You know what kind of woman I am! You know. You aren't foolish enough not to know the truth. You try to threaten me while dreaming of romance, but you know the truth. I am a tacky, despicable woman... a woman whose life is over because she was sold for money. I am—. My love, you probably think I behaved debauchedly, but these past few days I have behaved more properly than I ever have in my life.... You are a man, so you don't understand.

"Once a woman is ruined, she is completely ruined. She is dirty to the bone. Her life is over."

She continued walking as she wept.

"You're a fool for wanting me." she said. "You're a fool for wanting me. Both for my sake and for yours. We did everything we could. This is just a romance—"

She wiped the tears from her eyes and turned to me. "Don't you understand?" she asked defiantly. "Do you still not know?"

We faced each other silently for a moment.

"Yes," I said. "I understand."

We walked together for a long time without saying a single word, sad and slow, hesitating to turn toward our parting. When we finally turned back, she broke the silence again.

"I had you," she said.

"Heaven and hell," I said, "cannot change that fact."

"I wanted to..." she continued. "At night I would talk to you and prepare speeches. But now that I try to speak, my tongue is tied. But to me, it's as if these moments we spent together will last forever. Moods and states come and go. Today my light is out, but..."

To this day, I still don't know whether she said the word "chloral", a type of sleeping pill/sedative, or if I just imagined she did. Perhaps my half-unconscious diagnosis flashed that word in my brain. Or perhaps it was a bizarre trick of my memory, or a suggestive possibility picking at my wounds. But that word is deeply engraved in my memory, as if branded by fire.

When we finally reached the garden gate of Lady Osprey's, an upper-class lady's, mansion, a drizzle started to fall.

She held out her hand, and I took it.

"I was yours," she said in an exhausted, passionless voice. "Even though it was meager, I gave you everything I had. Will you forget?"

"Never," I replied.

"Not a single touch or a single word?"

"No."

"You will forget," she said.

We looked at each other without a word, and her face was full of exhaustion and misery.

What could I have done? What was there left to do?

"I wished—" I started to say, then stopped.

"Goodbye."

IV

That was supposed to be the last time I saw her, but by a twist of fate, I saw her one more time. Two days later, I was at Lady Grove, though I have completely forgotten what errand brought me there. As I was walking back to the station, assuming she had already left, she passed in front of me. She was riding a horse with Carnaby, just like the first time I saw her. That chance encounter ambushed us without any mental preparation. With a pale face and dark eyes, she rode by, barely even looking at me. When she saw me, she flinched, stiffened, and bowed her head. However, because Carnaby thought I was a defeated man who had fallen and lost everything, he greeted me casually and amicably, shouting out pleasant, predictable well-wishes.

They disappeared from view, leaving me alone by the roadside....

And right then, I finally tasted the ultimate bitterness of life. For the first time in my life, I felt complete emptiness, writhing in emotions that could provoke no action, overwhelmed by unspeakable shame and pity. I had parted from her numbly, and even when my uncle collapsed and died, I maintained my composure without shedding a single tear. But running into my lost Beatrice by chance like this finally wrung the tears out of me. My face contorted, and tears streamed down my cheeks. All the magic she had given me turned into wild sorrow. "Oh God!" I cried out. "This is too cruel." I turned my face toward where she had disappeared and made pleading gestures toward the beech trees, cursing my fate. I wanted to do something absurd. I wanted to chase after her, save her, and turn back time so she could start her life anew. I wonder what would have happened if I had run until I was out of breath, caught up to them, and pleaded with her through tears and gibberish. I almost did just that.

But there was no one on earth or in heaven to respect my curses or my tears. Meanwhile, a man who had been trimming the opposite hedge appeared and stared at me.

Suddenly and ridiculously, I hid my expression, pretending nothing was wrong in front of that man, walked on, and caught my train....

But the pain I felt then I have felt a hundred times since, and it is with me even at this very moment as I write. That pain haunts this book from beginning to end like a ghost, lingering around it. As far as I know, it is exactly that which possesses this book.
