# Chapter 3: Night and the Open Sea

I
Throughout this story, I have tried to tell things exactly as they happened to me. When I first began writing, I said I wanted to tell the story of myself and the world I found myself in, and I have done my best to achieve that purpose. But whether I have succeeded, I do not know. All this writing now feels faded and lifeless, trite and meaningless to me; I know parts of it by heart, yet I am the least qualified person to judge my own work.

As I sift through the pile of manuscript before me, certain facts become much clearer. Specifically, I see how immense and inconsequential my experiences have been. Looking back at it all, my story is one of constant activity and urgency, yet it produced no real fruit—it is a story of sterility. I named this book *Tono-Bungay* after the fake medicine my uncle and I created, but I would have been much better off calling it *Waste*.
I have written of my wife Marion, who could not have children, and of my aunt, who is also childless. I have told the story of my first love, Beatrice, who squandered her youth and talent in a futile existence. What hope remains for a people whose women no longer bring forth life?
I think of all the energy I have poured into vain pursuits. I think of the busy, empty schemes I plotted with my uncle, of his massive mansion 'Crest Hill' which he ambitiously started building but abandoned midway, and of his passionate career that took the world by storm. Tens of thousands of men envied him and wished to live as he did. But all of this is simply a spectacle of power running to waste. It is a story of people who only consume resources without ever replacing them, the story of an England wasting away in a feverish pursuit of meaningless trade, money-making, and pleasure. And after all this, I am now building destroyers—weapons of war!

Others may view this country differently, but this is how I have seen England. Somewhere in the earlier pages of this manuscript, I compared our present splendor and abundance to the October foliage just before the frost brings down the leaves. I still feel that is an apt comparison. Perhaps my perspective is wrong. It may be that because I am, in a sense, a decaying entity myself, I only see decay in everything around me. To others, this world might look like a scene of achievement and construction shining with hope. I, too, have something like hope, but it is a very distant hope, one that finds no promise in the current British Empire or in any of the great achievements of our time.

How history will record all of this, and how time and chance will ultimately prove these things, I do not know. I am merely recording how the world appeared in the mind of one man living in this era.

II
At the same time I was writing the final chapter of this book, I was extremely busy with matters concerning a new destroyer we had just completed. Writing and ship-building—these two tasks alternated in a strangely complementary way. About three weeks ago, I had to put this novel aside to devote all my time, day and night, to assembling and finishing the engines. Last Thursday, the destroyer we call 'X2' was completed, and I took her down the River Thames and out into the North Sea, near the island of Texel, for a speed trial.

It is a strange phenomenon how a person's impressions can sometimes fuse together into a unity, forming a continuity with things that had previously felt entirely alien and disconnected. That rush down the Thames mysteriously connected itself to this book.

As I traveled down the Thames, I felt as though I were reviewing all of England in a new, parallel manner. Only then did I clearly see the England I had wanted to show my readers. The thought came to me slowly as I navigated out of the Pool of London, and it settled sharply in my mind by the time I sailed out into the wide North Sea and drifted into the dreams of the night.

It was not so much a deep thought at the time, but rather a kind of visual thinking that developed in my mind like a photograph. The X2 sliced through the dirty, oily river water like scissors tearing through canvas. My conscious mind was entirely focused on steering the ship safely under bridges and weaving through the steamboats, barges, rowboats, and piers. I lived with my hands and eyes fixed straight ahead. At that moment, I thought of the scenery only as obstacles, yet a corner of my mind was capturing the entire landscape with photographic completeness and vividness.

"This is it," a realization came to me. "This is England. This is exactly the image of England I wanted to capture in my book. This is it!"

We set off in the late afternoon. Our engines roared as we slipped out of our shipyard above Hammersmith Bridge. After bustling about for a moment, we headed downstream. We glided lightly down Craven Reach, passing Fulham and Hurlingham, and sailed past the long stretches of muddy meadows and suburbs. We went through Battersea and Chelsea, rounded the neat riverside corner of Grosvenor Road, and passed under Vauxhall Bridge until Westminster unfolded before us. We dodged a fleet of coal barges, and there on the left, bathed in the October sunshine, stood the Houses of Parliament. The flags were flying, and Parliament was in session.

I looked at it absentmindedly at the time, but later it rose in my memory as the centerpiece of that afternoon's grand panoramic view. That stiff, angular Victorian Gothic building, with a massive clock tower that looked like a Dutch clock, seemed to suddenly approach and stare at me. It then seemed to do a slow half-turn as it swept past, standing still behind me as if watching me depart. The building seemed to ask me, "Are you telling me you do not respect me?"

Respect? Certainly not! Inside that massive Victorian architecture, landlords, lawyers, bishops, railway executives, and commercial magnates pace back and forth. They are trapped in the hopeless tradition of commercializing the old aristocratic order of Bladesover, mimicking a vulgar gentry that has sold its honor for wealth. I have observed them closely enough to know their true nature. The Irish and Labour members run around their feet making a fuss, but achieving little; and as far as I can see, they have no better plans either. Respect such a thing? There are various trappings of dignity, but who can they deceive? The King rides down in a golden coach to hold a glittering ceremony, wearing long robes and a crown. There is a display of thick and thin legs in white stockings, legs in black stockings, and cunning old gentlemen draped in ermine. 
I remembered a congested afternoon I once spent with my aunt, buried under a pile of agitated women's hats in the Royal Gallery of the House of Lords. There, I watched the King walk in to open Parliament, and I saw the Duke of Devonshire—dressed like a gaudy peddler—looking terribly bored with the cap of maintenance resting on a tray slung from his shoulders. What an absurd spectacle it was!

Undoubtedly, this country of England is quaint, dignified in places, and full of sweet associations. But that does not change the essence of the reality hidden beneath those splendid robes. That reality is simply greedy trade, base profit-seeking, and shameless advertising. Despite the wearing of treasured robes, concepts like kingship and chivalry are completely dead amidst it all—just as dead as that crusader my uncle championed when he swung his weapon at the nettles outside the Duffield church.

I thought a great deal about the panorama I saw that bright afternoon.

Sailing down the Thames like that is like running your hand through the pages of the book of England from beginning to end. Starting at Craven Reach, you feel as if you are in the heart of old England. Behind us lay Kew Gardens and Hampton Court Palace, steeped in memories of kings and cardinals. We first passed between the Bishop of Fulham's garden party grounds and Hurlingham, the playground for our nation's sporting instincts. The overall atmosphere was profoundly English. There was ample space, ancient trees, and the very best qualities of our homeland gathered in those upper reaches. Putney, too, gave a sense of the Anglican church, albeit on a shrinking scale.
Then, the old aristocratic estates like Bladesover vanished as new developments began to spill over. First came a squalid stretch of mean houses lining both sides of the river, followed by the gloomy industrialism of the southern bank. On the northern bank, a long, elegant row of fine houses—home to artists, writers, and administrators—stretched from Cheyne Walk almost to Westminster, acting as a polite facade hiding a vast wasteland of slums behind it. Mile after mile, there was a long, slow crescendo: houses packed tighter and tighter, an endless succession of church towers, architectural moments, and bridges, until we entered the second movement of the London symphony, with Lambeth's old palace on our flank and the Houses of Parliament at our bow! Then Westminster Bridge lay ahead, and as you flashed through it, the round-faced clock tower craned its neck to look down at you again. New Scotland Yard glared at you, standing squarely like a fat Beefeater policeman miraculously disguised as the Bastille.

For a stretch, you encounter the true essence of London. There is Charing Cross railway station, the heart of the world, and the Embankment on the north side, lined with newly built hotels that overshadow the Georgian and Victorian architecture. On the south side lie mud, massive warehouses, factories, chimneys, shot towers, and advertising billboards. The northern skyline grows increasingly complex and beautiful, making you ever more thankful for the architect Christopher Wren. Somerset House is as picturesque as the Civil War; people are reminded once more of the original England, feeling the delicate, lace-like quality of the Restoration era in the sculpted sky.

Then came Astor's strongbox and the lawyers' Inns of Court., There, I recalled a past memory of myself. I remembered a time when I trudged westward along the Embankment with heavy steps, seriously weighing a job offer of three hundred pounds a year from my uncle...,

I drove the ship through this core, essential section of London. Ignoring it all, the X2 shoved her bow under the foam like a black hound pushing through a field of reeds. What scent the ship was tracking, even I, her maker, could not tell.

It is also in this section that you first encounter seagulls and are reminded of the sea. We passed Blackfriars Bridge. Just under these two bridges, and right between them, lies the finest bridge moment in the world. Behold! Rising above the noise of the rough warehouses, above the fierce competition of merchants, utterly out of place in its beauty and completely detached, floats Saint Paul's Cathedral! "Of course!" people say, "It's Saint Paul's!" It stands as the symbol of the elegance achieved by the old Anglican culture—a more modest, restrained version of St. Peter's in Rome, colder and grayer, yet still ornate. It has never been overthrown or disavowed. It is simply that the tall warehouses and the roar of traffic have forgotten it; everyone has forgotten it. Steamboats and barges pass by indifferently, ignoring the cathedral, while a tangled web of telephone wires and utility poles slices blackly into its thin, mysterious mist. A moment later, when traffic allows you to turn your head and look for it, it has already melted away like a cloud into the grayish-blue London sky.

After that, the traditional, superficial England falls away from you entirely. The third and final, most massive movement of the London symphony begins, where the neat order of the old regime is completely dwarfed and swallowed up. London Bridge appears, and colossal warehouses tower around you, waving massive cranes. Seagulls circle and scream in your ears, and giant ships lie moored among the barges. You have now entered the port of the world. Time and again in this book, I have described England as a feudal system suffering from fatty degeneration, swollen by an enormous accident of hypertrophy.

At the end of this panorama, I must strike that note one last time. I remember the ancient Tower of London—a small, neat, sunlit, and precious collection of buildings nestled in a gap among the warehouses. This delightfully provincial and dignified cluster of buildings is overshadowed by the most vulgar and typical achievement of modern England: the fake Gothic ironwork shell of Tower Bridge. That Tower Bridge perfectly balances and validates the dreary pinnacles and clock tower of Westminster. That fake Gothic bridge stands at the very gateway to the Sea, our mother of change!

But beyond that lies a world of chance and nature. For the third part of the London panorama transcends all laws, orders, and priorities; it is the seaport and the sea. The ship traveled down the widening river, passing a grotesquely diverse array of vessels: massive steamers, giant sailing ships flying flags from all over the world, a monstrous tangle of barges, brown-sailed lighters looking like a coven of witches, staggering tugboats, and a noisy, jostling crowd of cranes and masts, docks, warehouses, and overbearing billboards. A massive vista of docks opened up on the left and right, and beyond and between them were church spires, small patches of indescribably old and worn houses, and riverside pubs. These were the remnants of old townships that had long since been torn apart and submerged by these new growths. And amidst it all, there was no sign of a plan, an intention, or a comprehensive desire. That is the very essence of this landscape. Day by day, the pressure of commerce and traffic grew, swelling grotesquely before anyone noticed. Someone built a wharf, someone else erected a crane, this company started working, and that company jumped in. Pushing and shoving against each other, they created this indigestible, massive monster of traffic. We dodged and wove through it all, driving passionately toward the high seas.

I remember laughing aloud when I caught a glimpse of the name of a London County Council steamboat that crossed my path. The ship was named *Caxton*, after England's first printer. Another was named *Pepys*, and yet another *Shakespeare*. They looked so wildly out of place splashing around in that chaotic river. I felt an urge to pluck them out of the water, wipe them dry, and put them back on the shelf of some English gentleman's library. Everything there was alive. Things flashed, splashed, and rushed by. Ships moved, tugboats panted, ropes were pulled taut, and barges drifted down the river carrying sailors sweating at the oars. The river swirled entirely with the wake of the passing ships, splitting into millions of little ripples, curling and foaming under the whip of the ceaseless wind. We drove past it all. In Greenwich to the south, there is a fine stone facade where all the records of victory are inscribed in the Painted Hall, and next to it stood an inn called the "Ship," where long ago, before the Port of London became too crowded, the gentlemen of Westminster used to hold an annual dinner. As we passed, the old facade of the hospital was warming in the light of the setting sun. After that, the river opened wide to the left and right, and the presence of the sea grew stronger and overwhelming. The river stretched endlessly from Northfleet to the Nore sandbank.

Finally, with the sun behind you, you emerge into the eastern sea. You increase your speed, slicing through the oily waves louder and faster. *Swish, swish, splash, swish.* The hills of Kent—where I once fled from the hypocritical Christian teachings of Nicodemus Frapp—fall away to the right, and Essex falls away to the left. The land recedes and vanishes into a blue haze. The large, slow ships trailing the tugboats, and the sturdy tugboats themselves forging silently ahead, are all molded into wet gold as we froth past them. These ships stand out, bound on strange missions of life and death, off to kill men in unfamiliar lands. And now, behind us, there is only a blue mystery and the ghostly flicker of unseen lights, and soon even these disappear. My destroyer and I tear across a vast, gray space toward the unknown. We charge into the massive spaces of the future, and the turbines begin to speak in strange, incomprehensible tongues. We head out to the open sea, toward windy freedom and trackless paths. One by one, the lights sink below the horizon. England and the Kingdom, Britain and the Empire, the old prides and the old devotions—they all slide down the flanks and stern of the ship, sinking below the horizon, and pass... they pass. The river passes... London passes, England passes...

III
This is the tone I have tried to emphasize. It is the tone that rings clearly in my mind whenever I think of anything beyond the purely personal aspects of my story.

It is a tone of crumbling and confusion, of blindly swelling change, of a bubbling mixture of futile love and sorrow. But through that confusion, another sound can be heard. Through the confusion, something drives forward. It is both a human achievement and the most inhuman of all existing things. Something emerges from it.... How can I express the value of something so essential, yet so formless? It is something that calls to men like me with an irresistible appeal.

In the previous chapters, I represented it through the symbol of my destroyer—cold, sleek, and entirely disconnected from most human interests. Sometimes I call this reality "Science," and sometimes I call it "Truth." But it is something we draw from the heart of life through pain and effort, something we untangle and make clear. Others serve it through art, literature, and social invention, seeing it in a thousand different forms and under a hundred different names. I always see it as austerity and beauty. This 'something' we clarify is the very heart of life. It is the only thing that endures. Men and nations, eras and civilizations, all make their contributions and pass on. I do not know exactly what this 'something' is. I only know that it is supreme. It is a thing, a quality, an element. Today, it can be found in colors, in norms, in sounds, or in thoughts. It emerges from the life we live and feel each year, appearing across generations and across ages. But how and why it exists is completely beyond the grasp of my intellect....

Yet, as I drove alone across the wild, heaving circle of the sea through the night, above the roaring murmur of my engines, I felt a full sense of that 'something.'

Far off in the northeastern sea, the lights of a naval squadron flickered, waving white swords of light into the sky. I kept my distance so they remained hull-down below the horizon, and soon their lights faded like dry summer lightning over the watery edge of the earth.... I fell into almost formless thoughts, into doubts and dreams that cannot be expressed in words. And it felt good to me to relentlessly drive forward, cutting through the starlit, windy night air, endlessly riding over the long, black waves.

IV
By the time I returned up the shining river, passing the old gray Tower of London, with the four sick and starving journalists who had been permitted to accompany me, it was already morning and the day had broken....

I very clearly remember the backs of those journalists as they walked away down a side street, moving with a kind of damp exhaustion. They were good lines and bore no ill will toward me. In their exaggerated, degraded Kiplingesque style, which glorifies imperialism, they packaged me to the public as a modest little button on the satisfied stomach of the British Empire. Though, to tell the truth, the X2 was never intended for the British Empire, nor for the hands of any European power. We offered this destroyer to our own people first, but they wanted nothing to do with me. And I have long since stopped troubling myself over such matters. I have abandoned my illusions and come to view myself, and my country, from an outsider's perspective. We are made, and we pass away.

We are all things that are made, that struggle onward with a hidden mission, and pass out into the open sea.
