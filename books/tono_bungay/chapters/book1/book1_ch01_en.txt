# Chapter 1: Of Bladesover House, and My Mother; and the Constitution of Society

I

Most people in this world seem to live according to a script. Their lives have a beginning, a middle, and an end, all neatly flowing together according to the rules of their specific type. You can easily categorize them as this or that kind of person. To borrow a theater term, they are "character actors," no more and no less. They have their set class and their place. They know exactly how they are expected to act and what they are entitled to receive. In the end, the size of their tombstone announces how well they played their part. 

But there is another kind of life—one that isn’t so much lived as it is sampled. A person gets hit by some unusual, sideways force that knocks them out of their social class. For the rest of their life, they live diagonally, bouncing from one kind of experience to another. That was my fate, and it’s the reason I’m finally writing something like a novel. I have an unusual series of intense impressions that I feel an urgent need to share. I’ve experienced life at vastly different levels, and at every level, I engaged with it intimately and authentically. I have lived like a local in many different social worlds. I was once the unwanted houseguest of my cousin, a baker who later died in the Chatham charity hospital. I secretly ate snacks smuggled by servants in a mansion's pantry. I was looked down upon for my lack of manners by a gas company clerk’s daughter (whom I later married and divorced). And to go to the opposite extreme—oh, those glorious days!—I was once a guest at an Earl's estate party. I admit she was a countess whose wealth was her main asset, but a countess nonetheless. I’ve seen these people from every angle. At dinner parties, I met not just titled aristocrats but truly great figures. Once—it’s my shining memory—I spilled champagne down the trousers of the British Empire's greatest statesman while we were flushed with mutual admiration. I wouldn’t dare insult him by naming him here! 

And once, though it’s a very minor detail in my life, I killed a man. 

Yes, I’ve seen a truly bizarre variety of people and ways of living. High or low, they are all strange. Deep down, they are very similar, yet on the surface, they are weirdly different. Since I’ve traveled so widely, I wish I could have gone just a little further up and down the social ladder. Knowing royalty would surely be fascinating and a lot of fun, but my contact with princes was limited to very public events. On the other end of the scale, I never really got to intimately know those dusty but charming people who wander the summer roads drunk with their families, pushing strollers, selling lavender, and carrying sunburned children and mysterious bundles. (The fact that they travel as a family does excuse their minor offenses.) The laborers, farmhands, sailors, and stokers sitting in the old licensed pubs are also beyond my reach, and they probably always will be. My interactions with dukes have been just as pathetic. I went hunting with a duke once, and in a burst of sheer snobbery, I tried my hardest to shoot him in the legs. But I missed. 

I’m sorry I haven’t experienced every part of society. 

You might ask how I managed to cross so many social boundaries and see such a wide slice of British society. It was an accident of birth. In England, it always is. To be honest, everything in the universe is an accident. But I digress. I was my uncle's nephew, and my uncle was Edward Ponderevo. It’s already been ten years since he shot across the financial sky like a comet! Do you remember the Ponderevo era? I mean the *great* days of Ponderevo. Maybe you even invested a little in his world-shaking business! If so, you know him all too well. Riding on the back of his fake miracle cure, Tono-Bungay, he blazed across the empty sky—not just like a comet, but like a massive rocket! Investors were in awe of his rising star. At his peak, he exploded into a magnificent cloud of stock promotions. What a time it was! He was the Napoleon of household conveniences!

I was his nephew—his special, closest nephew. I held onto his coattails the entire time. Before he made it big, I made pills with him in a pharmacy in Wimblehurst. I was, you could say, the stick of his rocket. After our incredible launch, after he manipulated millions of pounds falling like golden rain, and after I got my bird's-eye view of the modern world, I crashed back down. Now I’m twenty-two years older, perhaps a bit scarred and blistered, my youth gone and my manhood worn away. But I learned a lot. I crashed into this shipyard by the Thames, into the heat and the hammering, into the hard, undeniable reality of steel. And here, in my spare time, I reflect on it all and jot down the scattered notes that make up this book. It was more than a metaphorical flight, you see. The peak of that career was undoubtedly our real flight across the English Channel in the Lord Roberts Beta airship. 

I have to warn you, this book is going to be a bit of a jumble. I want to trace my social journey (and my uncle’s) as the main plot. But since this is my first—and almost certainly my last—novel, I also want to include all the things that shocked me, amused me, and left an impression, even if they don't move the story forward. I also want to tell the honest truth about my bizarre romantic relationships. They tortured me, confused me, and completely threw me off balance. They still feel full of irrational, confusing emotions, and I think I'll feel clearer once I write them down. I might even drift into describing people I only met briefly, simply because it’s fun to remember what they said and did to us, especially how they acted under the brief, blinding spotlight of Tono-Bungay and its even flashier spin-offs. That spotlight really exposed some of their true colors, I can promise you that! Honestly, I want to include everything. My approach to a novel is inclusive, not strict. 

Tono-Bungay is still on the billboards. It still sits in every pharmacy storeroom. It still soothes the coughs of old people, brightens their tired eyes, and loosens their tongues. But its social glory and financial brilliance have vanished from the world forever. And I, the sole survivor burned by the explosion, sit writing about it here. The air is never quiet, filled with the clang and roar of machinery. My desk is covered with blueprints and bits of models dealing with speed, air pressure, and water pressure—things entirely different from Tono-Bungay. 

II

I look at what I’ve written so far and wonder if this actually explains what I’m trying to do with this book. I seem to have given the impression that I want to throw together a messy stew of anecdotes and experiences, with my uncle floating in the middle like a big piece of meat. I admit that now, with my pen on the paper, I realize what a massive, brewing mix of lessons, emotions, and theories I have to deal with. In a way, I see how hopeless this book is right from the start. I suppose what I’m really trying to capture is Life itself—exactly as one man experienced it. I want to tell *my* story, and my overall view of things. I want to express what I strongly feel about the rules, traditions, and ideas we call society, and how we poor individuals get pushed around, tempted, and stranded in these confusing, shifting currents. I think I’ve reached an age where things start to look real. They aren't just material for daydreams anymore; they are interesting on their own. I’ve reached the age of criticizing and writing novels, and here I am writing mine—my one and only novel. And I’m doing it without any of the discipline or ability to hold back that regular novelists supposedly learn.

Before I started this, I read my fair share of novels and even tried writing a few times. I found that the constraints and rules of the art form were impossible for me to follow. I like to write, and I’m deeply interested in it, but it isn’t my true skill. I’m an engineer with a couple of patents and a lot of ideas. Whatever artistic talent I have has gone into turbines, boat building, and the problem of flight. Try as I might, I can’t be anything but a messy, undisciplined storyteller. I have to wander, stumble, make comments, and theorize if I want to get my thoughts out. I don’t have a neatly constructed story to tell; I have messy reality. Even my love story—which you’ll hear all about if I can keep being this ruthlessly honest—doesn't fit into a tidy narrative. It involves three different women. It’s all tangled up with everything else. 

But I hope I’ve made enough excuses for my disorganized style. I think I’d better get straight to telling you about my childhood and my early memories in the shadow of Bladesover House. 

III

There came a time when I realized that Bladesover House was not everything it seemed. But when I was a little boy, I believed with total faith that it was a perfect, complete miniature universe. I believed the Bladesover system was a small—and honestly, not that small—working model of the entire world. 

Let me try to explain how it felt. 

Bladesover sits up on the rolling hills of Kent, maybe eight miles from Ashborough. Behind the house, on the crest of a hill, sits an old wooden pavilion designed to look like the Roman Temple of Vesta. In theory, you can see two waters from there: the English Channel to the south and the River Thames to the northeast. The estate park is the second largest in Kent. It’s beautifully wooded with well-placed beeches, lots of elms, and some sweet chestnuts. It’s full of little valleys filled with ferns, springs, a stream, three beautiful ponds, and herds of fallow deer. The house was built in the eighteenth century out of pale red brick, styled like a French chateau. Except for one gap in the hills that opens up to distant blue views—tiny farmhouses with hop-drying kilns, small woods, wheat fields, and the occasional glint of water—its 117 windows look out at nothing but its own vast, handsome lands. A semicircular wall of massive beech trees hides the local church and village, which cluster picturesquely around the main road along the edge of the great park. In the far northern corner of the estate is a second, dependent village called Ropedean. It was less lucky because it was farther away, and also because of its priest. This clergyman was actually rich, but he was spitefully cheap because his tithe income had dropped. On top of that, he completely alienated the great ladies of Bladesover by referring to the Lord's Supper as the "Eucharist." As a result, Ropedean stayed in the shadows throughout my childhood. 

The unavoidable message sent by that massive park and beautiful house, looming over the church, the village, and the countryside, was that they were the most important things in the world. Everything else only mattered in relation to them. They represented the Landed Gentry, the "Quality." The rest of the world—the farmers, the laborers, the shopkeepers in Ashborough, the upper servants, the lower servants, and the estate workers—only lived and breathed because the upper class allowed it. And the upper class did it so quietly and completely. The great house blended the earth and sky together so solidly. The stark contrast between its spacious halls, drawing rooms, galleries, and airy staff quarters, versus the meager respect given to the local vicar and the cramped, stuffy rooms of the post office workers and the grocer, hammered this idea home. This belief only broke when I was thirteen or fourteen. A weird streak of inherited skepticism made me doubt whether Reverend Bartlett actually knew everything about God. Taking that doubt a step further, I started questioning if the gentlemen class were inherently right, and if they were actually necessary to the world. But once that skepticism woke up, it took me far and fast. By fourteen, I was committing terrible blasphemies and sacrilege. I decided I was going to marry a viscount’s daughter, and in open rebellion, I gave her half-brother a black eye—the left one, I think. 

But I’ll get to that later. 

Like I said, the great house, the church, the village, and the workers and servants in their proper places seemed to me like a closed, perfect social system. Around us were other villages and great estates, and the upper class—those fine Olympian gods—visited each other, weaving their lives together. Country towns seemed like nothing more than collections of shops, marketplaces for the tenant farmers, and centers for whatever basic education they needed. They were just as dependent on the landowners as our village was. I thought this was how the whole world worked. I thought London was just a bigger country town where the gentlemen kept city houses and did bigger shopping under the majestic shadow of the greatest gentlewoman of all, the Queen. It seemed like a divine order. I didn’t realize until the Tono-Bungay business really took off that this beautiful facade was already rotting. I had no idea that forces were at work that would soon destroy this elaborate social system—the same system my mother taught me so carefully so I would know my "place." 

There are still many people in England today who haven’t realized this. Sometimes I wonder if only a tiny minority of English people understand how much of this old social order is already dead. The great houses still stand in their parks, and the cottages still huddle respectfully on the edges, their ivy touching the eaves. If you travel north through Kent from Bladesover, you can see the English countryside stubbornly trying to look exactly as it used to. It’s like an early day in a beautiful October. The touch of change rests over everything, unfelt and unseen. It hesitates for a moment before it grabs hold and ends it all forever. Just one frost, and the whole surface of things will be stripped bare. Connections will snap, patience will run out, and all our pretty leaves of pretense will be left rotting in the mud. 

We still have to wait a little while for that. The new order might be well on its way to forming. But it's like those magic lantern shows they used to put on in the village, called "Dissolving Views." The old picture fades but stays visible in your mind, while the new picture replacing it remains confusing and hard to read for a long time. Because of that, the new England our children's children will inherit is still a riddle to me. The

Concepts of democracy, equality, and above all, indiscriminate social mingling have certainly never truly taken root in the English mind. 
But what new ideas are taking hold? I hope this entire book will shed a little light on that question. We English never clearly define things; we reserve our words for jokes and sarcasm. Meanwhile, the old structures and attitudes still remain, subtly altered and continually changing, sheltering strange new occupants.

Bladesover House has been leased out, fully furnished, to Sir Reuben Lichtenstein ever since the old Lady Drew passed away. It was a bizarre experience for me to visit that estate—where my mother had once worked as a housekeeper—right when my uncle was at the peak of his Tono-Bungay medicine empire. It was fascinating to observe the subtle little changes that had occurred under this new ownership. To borrow a metaphor from my days studying mineralogy, these Jewish families were not so much a new British upper class as they were "pseudomorphs" mimicking the shape of the old aristocracy. The Jewish people are highly intelligent, but not quite intelligent enough to completely hide their cleverness. I wished I could have gone downstairs to take in the atmosphere of the servants' pantry. I know it would have felt entirely different from what I remembered.

Over at Hawksnest, I noticed a similar imitation taking place. A newspaper tycoon—the sort who frantically jumps from one loud, sink-or-swim scheme to another using stolen ideas—had bought the property outright. Meanwhile, the Redgrave estate had fallen into the hands of beer brewers.

Yet, as far as I could tell, the villagers noticed absolutely no change in their world. As I walked through the village, two little girls curtsied, and an old laborer instinctively touched his hat in deep respect. He still believed he knew his place in society—and mine. I didn’t know the man, but I genuinely wanted to ask him if he remembered my mother, and whether he thought my uncle or old Lichtenstein was brave enough to give up their social standing as easily as that.

In the English countryside of my childhood, every human being had a defined "place." It was assigned to you at birth, much like your eye color, and it was your inescapable destiny. Above you were your superiors, below you were your inferiors, and there were even a handful of unstable, ambiguous individuals whom, for everyday purposes, you might vaguely consider your equals. At the absolute center and peak of our social system was Lady Drew, "Her Ladyship." She was a withered, talkative, and incredibly old woman with an astonishing memory for family trees. Beside her, and nearly as old, was her cousin and companion, Miss Somerville. These two old women lived like dried-up seeds inside the massive shell of Bladesover House—a shell that had once been vibrantly filled with fashionable dandies, powdered ladies with beauty patches, and polite gentlemen carrying swords. When they had no guests, they spent their entire days in the corner parlor just above the housekeeper’s room, passing the time reading, napping, or petting their two dogs. As a boy, I always imagined these two pitiful old creatures as superior beings living, like God, somewhere beyond the ceiling. Occasionally, they would thump around, and you could hear their footsteps overhead. This made them feel more real without taking away from their elevated status. Sometimes I actually saw them. Naturally, if I bumped into them in the park or the shrubbery—where I was strictly trespassing—I would hide or run away in reverent terror. But on proper occasions, I was summoned into their sacred presence. I remember "Her Ladyship" back then as a figure draped in black silk and a gold chain, offering a shaky command for me to be a good boy. She had a terribly shrunken, wrinkled face and neck, and a trembling, veiny hand that would press a half-crown coin into mine. Miss Somerville hovered behind her, a pale figure dressed in faded lavender, white, and black, squinting her sandy-lashed eyes. She had bright yellow hair and a vivid complexion. On winter nights, as we sat in the housekeeper’s room warming our toes and sipping elderberry wine, her maid would reveal the simple secrets behind that lingering blush. After I got into a fight with young Garvell, I was, of course, banished from the estate and never saw those two pathetic, painted old goddesses ever again.

Then there was the "Company"—the guests who walked the floors above our respectful heads. I rarely saw them in person, but their habits and manners were constantly mimicked and gossiped about by their maids and valets down in the housekeeper’s and steward’s rooms, so I got to know them secondhand. I quickly realized that none of these guests were truly on Lady Drew’s level; they were either higher or lower, just like everything else in our world. I remember one time a Prince visited, accompanied by a real, living nobleman. That was a bit above our usual social circles and stirred up a lot of excitement, perhaps raising our expectations a bit too high. Afterward, Rabbits, the butler, burst into my mother’s room downstairs, his face red with indignation and tears in his eyes. "Look at this!" Rabbits gasped. My mother was speechless with horror. "This" was a sovereign—a mere one-pound coin—the kind of tip you might get from any ordinary commoner!

I recall that the days following the guests' departure were always tense. The social effort left the poor old women upstairs exhausted, irritable, vindictive, and suffering from physical and emotional indigestion.

Clinging to the lowest edges of these true high-society elites were the vicarage families, followed by those ambiguous people who were neither aristocrats nor commoners. In the typical English class system, the vicarage families definitely hold a unique position of their own. Nothing is quite as remarkable as the social progress the Church of England has made over the past two hundred years. Back in the early eighteenth century, a vicar ranked slightly below the head house-steward and was considered a suitable match for a housekeeper or any maid who had been dismissed without causing too much of a moral scandal. Literature from the 1700s is full of complaints from vicars who weren't allowed to stay at the dinner table long enough to share the pie. They managed to rise above these humiliations largely because so many younger sons of wealthy families entered the clergy. Whenever I encounter the arrogant entitlement of modern clergymen, I often think back to these historical facts. It’s fascinating to note that today, the oppressed, organ-playing village schoolteacher of the Church of England holds almost the exact same social standing as a seventeenth-century parson. In Bladesover, the local doctor ranked below the vicar but above the veterinarian. Artists and summer vacationers were squeezed in above or below this line depending on their appearance and how much money they spent. Below them, in a meticulously ordered hierarchy, came the tenant farmers, the butler and the housekeeper, the village shopkeeper, the head gamekeeper, the cook, the pub owner, the assistant gamekeeper, the blacksmith (whose rank was complicated because his daughter ran the post office—and made an absolute mess of the telegrams, too!), the shopkeeper’s eldest son, the first footman, the shopkeeper’s younger sons, his first assistant, and so on down the line.

I absorbed all these concepts of universal social ranking—and much more—at Bladesover. I soaked it all in while listening to the chatter of valets, ladies' maids, Rabbits the butler, and my mother in the bright, white-painted housekeeper’s room with its many cupboards and shiny chintz fabrics, where the upper servants gathered. I also learned from the footmen, Rabbits, and various estate workers chatting among the green felt tables and Windsor chairs in the pantry. Down there, Rabbits operated above the law, selling beer without a license or an ounce of guilt. I even listened to the housemaids and still-room maids in the cold, mat-carpeted pantry, or the cook, her kitchen maids, and passing friends gossiping in the warm glow of the kitchen's bright copper pots.

Naturally, the servants' own ranks were implied in these conversations, but their talk mainly focused on the ranks and standings of the aristocratic elites. Over on the small dresser between the cupboards in my mother's room sat an old peerage directory, a church registry (Crockford's), recipe books, Whitaker's Almanack, Old Moore's Almanack, and an eighteenth-century dictionary. There was another coverless peerage book in the pantry, a new one in the billiard room, and I seem to recall yet another in the odd little room where the upper servants kept their bagatelle board and enjoyed sweet desserts after the main staff dinner. If you had asked any of those upper servants how a particular Prince of Battenberg was related to, say, Mr. Cunninghame Graham or the Duke of Argyll, they could have told you instantly, without a moment's hesitation. As a boy, I heard endless discussions of this sort. If I remain a bit fuzzy today on courtesy titles and the exact usage of honorifics, I assure you it's not because I lacked the opportunity to learn those juicy details—it's because I stubbornly refused to care.

Dominating all these memories is the image of my mother. She didn't love me because I grew to look more like my father every single day. She knew her own place in society—and the place of everyone else in the world—with absolute, unbending certainty. The only exceptions were the place where my father was hiding, and some details regarding my own place. She frequently faced highly nuanced etiquette questions. I can still see and hear her saying, "No, Miss Fison, peers of England enter before peers of the United Kingdom, and he is merely a peer of the United Kingdom." She put a tremendous amount of effort into seating other people’s servants around her tea table, where the etiquette was incredibly strict. Sometimes I wonder if the etiquette in today's housekeepers' rooms is still as rigid, and what my mother would have made of a modern chauffeur.

All things considered, I’m glad I saw as much of Bladesover as I did. Seeing it when I was young, believing in its system completely, and later analyzing it, has allowed me to understand aspects of English society that would otherwise be entirely incomprehensible. I am convinced that Bladesover is the master key to understanding almost everything that makes the British unique—and everything that confuses foreign visitors about England and the English-speaking world. You must firmly grasp that two hundred years ago, all of England was exactly like Bladesover. True, the country has seen Reform Acts and changes in policies, but there has been no fundamental revolution since then. Everything modern and different has merely been intrusive additions or footnotes tacked onto this original, dominant system—sometimes rudely, sometimes apologetically. Once you understand this, you will immediately see why the "snobbishness" that characterizes English thinking is both logical and inevitable. Anyone who isn't living directly in the shadow of a Bladesover-style estate is essentially wandering around, constantly trying to find a lost sense of direction. We have never broken from our traditions. We haven't even symbolically destroyed them, the way the French physically did during the Reign of Terror. However, all the organizing principles have loosened, and the old traditional bonds have weakened or unraveled completely. In a way, even America is just a detached, outlying suburb of that same estate, which has expanded in very strange directions. George Washington, Esquire, was born into the gentleman class, and he very nearly became a King. As you may know, it was the classical teachings of Plutarch—not anything inherently American—that kept George Washington from taking a crown.

IV

I hated teatime in the housekeeper’s room more than anything else at Bladesover. And I hated it especially when Mrs. Mackridge, Mrs. Booch, and Mrs. Latude-Fernay were staying at the house. All three of them were retired, pensioned-off servants.

Old friends of Lady Drew had left them pensions as a posthumous reward for their long years of dedication to their minor comforts, and Mrs. Booch also served as the guardian of a favorite Skye terrier. Every year, Lady Drew invited them back—a reward and a celebration of their virtues, meant especially as an example for my mother and Miss Fison, the maid. They would sit around in shiny, stiff black dresses decorated with ruffles, lace, and beads, devouring huge amounts of cake and drinking vast quantities of tea with imposing dignity, their voices echoing off the walls.

I remember these women being absolutely massive. Objectively, they were probably normal-sized, but I was just a tiny boy, and in my memory, they have taken on nightmarish proportions. They loomed, they bulged, they hovered over me. Mrs. Mackridge was large and dark-complexioned. There was something astonishing about her head—namely, that she was bald. She wore a dignified cap, and right at the front, across her forehead, hair had been literally painted on. I have never seen anything like it since. She had once been the maid to the widow of Sir Roderick Blenderhasset Impey, some sort of governor or terrifying authority figure in the East Indies. Judging by the traces left behind in Mrs. Mackridge, Lady Impey must have been a truly crushing and stupendous woman. Lady Impey had likely been the Juno type—haughty, unapproachable, and fond of sarcasm and biting wit. Mrs. Mackridge possessed none of the wit, but along with the grand lady’s old satin dresses and trimmings, she had inherited her caustic tone and gestures. When she told you it was a fine morning, she somehow also made it sound like she was calling you an idiot—a low-class idiot at that. If you spoke to her, she had a way of acknowledging your pathetic little voice with a loud, scornful "Haw!" that made you want to set her on fire. She also had a habit of saying "Indeed!" while slowly lowering her eyelids.

Mrs. Booch was a smaller woman with brown hair, strange little curls dangling on either side of her face, and large blue eyes. Her entire intellectual capacity was limited to a small handful of predictable, recycled phrases. Oddly enough, Mrs. Latude-Fernay has left no impression on my memory whatsoever, other than her name and the visual impact of a gray-green silk dress covered in gold and blue buttons. I assume she was a large blonde woman. Then there was Miss Fison, the maid who served both Lady Drew and Miss Somerville. At the far end of the table, sitting opposite my mother, was Rabbits the butler. For a butler, Rabbits was an unassuming man, and at teatime, he didn't look like the traditional butlers you know. He wore a morning coat and a black tie dotted with blue spots. Still, he was a large man with bushy sideburns, even if his clean-shaven mouth looked weak and small. I sat among these people on a tall, hard, early-Gregorian chair, just trying to survive, like a fragile little seedling wedged between massive boulders. My mother kept a strict eye on me, determined to crush any slight display of childhood energy. It was hard on me, but perhaps it was also hard on those overfed, aging, pretentious people to have my youthful restlessness and rebellious, skeptical eyes intruding upon their dignified gathering.

Teatime dragged on for nearly forty-five minutes, and I was forced to sit through the whole thing. Day after day, the conversation was exactly the same.

"Sugar, Mrs. Mackridge?" my mother would ask.

"Sugar, Mrs. Latude-Fernay?"

The word "sugar" would spark something in Mrs. Mackridge's mind. "They say," she would announce—at least half of her sentences began with 'they say'—"that sugar is fattening nowadays. Many of the best families don't take it at all."

"Not in their tea, ma'am," Rabbits would chime in helpfully.

"Not in anything," Mrs. Mackridge would reply with a crushing, absolute tone, taking a sip of her tea.

"What will they say next?" Miss Fison would wonder.

"They do say the strangest things!" Mrs. Booch would add.

"They say," Mrs. Mackridge continued stubbornly, "that doctors are no longer recommending it."

My Mother: "Is that so, ma'am?"

Mrs. Mackridge: "Yes, ma'am."

Then, addressing the entire table, she would add: "Before he died, poor Sir Roderick consumed massive quantities of sugar. Sometimes I wonder if it might have hastened his end."

That concluded the first conversational skirmish. A brief, gloomy silence was then observed out of respect for the sacred memory of Sir Roderick.

"George," my mother said, "don't kick the chair!"

Then, perhaps, Mrs. Booch would bring up one of her favorite topics. "The days are getting nicely longer," she would say, or when the season changed and the days grew shorter, "How quickly the evenings draw in!" It was a very useful conversation starter for her, and without it, I cannot imagine how she would have managed to socialize with anyone.

My mother, who sat with her back to the window, always considered it polite to turn around and look at the scenery outside, acknowledging the lengthening or shortening of the day out of consideration for Mrs. Booch.

This would lead to a lively debate about exactly how many days were left until the summer or winter solstice, which would eventually fade away as everyone grew tired of it.

Then Mrs. Mackridge might take over. She had a few intellectual habits, one of which was reading the *Morning Post*, a conservative newspaper of the British upper class. The other ladies occasionally glanced at it, but usually only to read the obituaries, marriage announcements, and birth notices on the front page. This was, of course, the grand old *Morning Post* that cost threepence, not the light and flashy modern paper of today. "They say," she began, "that Lord Tweedums is going to Canada as Governor-General."

"Ah!" Mr. Rabbits chimed in. "Is that so?"

"Isn't he," my mother said, "the Earl of Slumgold's cousin?" She already knew he was. It was an entirely irrelevant and unnecessary comment, thrown in merely to join the conversation.

"That's right, ma'am," Mrs. Mackridge replied. "I hear he was extremely popular when he was Governor in New South Wales, Australia. People looked up to him greatly. I knew him when he was young, and he was a very pleasant, agreeable young man."

A brief silence of respect followed.

"His predecessor," Mr. Rabbits said—he had learned a precise, emphatic way of speaking from some clergyman, but had failed to acquire the elegant upper-class English accent to match it—"got into quite a bit of trouble in Sydney, Australia."

"Hah!" Mrs. Mackridge said scornfully. "So I've heard."

"After finishing his assignment, he stayed nearby at the Templemorton estate. I remember people gossiping about him after he left again."

"Oh?" Mrs. Mackridge asked inquiringly.

"The trouble he caused was from quoting poetry, ma'am. He said something like... 'They left their country for their country's good.' Apparently, the Australians took this as a reminder that, even though they were reformed now, they were originally exiled British convicts. From what I heard, everyone agreed it was an incredibly tactless thing to say."

"Sir Roderick always used to say," Mrs. Mackridge stated. "The first essential quality for a colonial governor is—" She paused and glared fiercely at me. "And the second quality—" She stared at me again. "And the third quality—" Only then did she release me from her gaze. "Is tact." Noticing my skeptical expression, she added firmly, "I have always thought that was an exceptionally true remark."

I promised myself that if I ever found the tumor of "tact" growing in my soul, I would rip it out by the roots, throw it away, and stomp on it.

"Colonial people are very strange," Mr. Rabbits said. "Very strange indeed. I saw a bit of them when I was at Templemorton. Sure, they are outwardly polite and spend their money freely... but to be honest, some of them made me very nervous. They watch you. Right when you're serving them. And they pretend not to be looking at you..."

My mother said nothing during that discussion. The word "colonies" always made her anxious. I think she was afraid that if she thought about it too much, my runaway father—who was probably a bigamist and caught up in some dreadful revolutionary ideas—might suddenly and shockingly reappear. She had absolutely no desire to find him again.

Strangely enough, when I was a little boy eavesdropping on these conversations, I had my own firm ideas about the British colonies, and I secretly mocked Mrs. Mackridge's upper-class sense of colonial superiority. I figured the sun-tanned, free, and proud pioneers of colonial Britain merely tolerated these aristocratic invaders as bizarre, outdated relics, and certainly didn't welcome their rule.

But I don't mock them anymore. Now, I'm not so sure.


V

It is a little hard to explain why I didn't simply accept the ways of the world as anyone else in my position would have naturally done. I suppose it comes down to my innate skepticism and my inability to easily fit in and conform with others. My father seemed to have been a skeptic, and my mother was certainly a cold, unfeeling woman.

I was an only child, and to this day, I have no idea if my father is dead or alive. Before I was old enough to form clear memories, he ran away, unable to endure my mother's suffocating moral standards. He left without a trace, and in her anger, my mother destroyed every single reminder of him. I have never even seen a photograph of him or a sample of his handwriting. I suspect the only reason she didn't destroy her marriage certificate—and me along with it—to completely erase the humiliation of her marriage was because of societal expectations and basic decency. I must have inherited some of her moral numbness, the kind that allowed her to burn every personal item he ever left behind. He must have given her gifts when they were lovers—books with affectionate notes, letters, a pressed flower, or some kind of token ring. She kept her wedding ring, but threw everything else away. She never even told me his first name or spoke a single word about him, though there were times I almost dared to ask. Everything I know about him—which isn't much—came from my hero, my Uncle Ponderevo. My mother wore her ring, kept her marriage certificate in a sealed envelope at the bottom of her largest trunk, and paid to send me to a private school in the Kentish hills. I didn't spend every school holiday at the great Bladesover House. Whenever Lady Drew was annoyed by recent houseguests or just wanted to take her frustration out on my mother, she would ignore my mother's usual holiday notice, and I would simply have to "stay on" at school.

But those occasions were rare, and between the ages of ten and fourteen, I probably averaged about fifty days a year at Bladesover.

I don't deny that staying there was good for me. The Bladesover estate, which swallowed up the entire surrounding countryside, retained a certain grandeur. The Bladesover system did at least one good thing for England: it wiped out the narrow-minded peasant mentality. Even if many of us still lived our lives tied to the pantry and the servants' quarters, we were at least freed from the dream of scraping by, living parasitically off a few chickens and pigs. The grounds offered elements of a liberal education. There were vast green lawns not used for manure or farming; there was mystery and material to spark the imagination. It was still a deer park. I observed the lives of those spotted animals, heard the stags calling, stumbled upon young fawns in the fern bushes, and found bones, skulls, and antlers in isolated spots. There were corners that gave a hint of what the word "forest" truly meant, offering glimpses of raw, unpolished natural beauty. The slope in the west wood, covered in bluebells under the fresh green beech trees in the dappled sunlight, still shines in my memory like a precious sapphire. It was the first time I consciously encountered true Beauty.

And inside the house, there were books. I never saw the trashy novels old Lady Drew read—I later learned she was obsessed with things like Maria Monk's convent exposés—but in the past, the Drew family had an intellectual, Sir Cuthbert, the son of Sir Matthew who built the house. Up in an old, neglected room, despised and forgotten by the rest of the household, were Sir Cuthbert's books and treasures. On a rainy winter day, my mother allowed me to dig through them. Sitting beneath a skylight, on a shelf above piles of tea and spices, I became familiar with Raphael through a large portfolio of Hogarth's satires and a huge book of engravings showing the Raphael Rooms in the Vatican. I also saw what major European cities looked like around 1780 through several landscape books printed with heavy iron plates. There was also a massive, complex 18th-century world atlas that taught me a great deal. It featured elaborate decorations around each map title: a fisherman and his boat for the Netherlands, a Cossack cavalryman for Russia, and for Japan, bizarre people dressed in—I say this deliberately—"pagodas." Back then, every continent had "Terrae Incognitae" (unknown lands), and there were places like Poland and Sarmatia, lands that have since vanished. Armed with a dull pin, I made countless imaginary voyages across that massive, inaccurate, and majestic world. The books in that tiny old closet had probably been banished from the main drawing room during the Victorian era's revival of "good taste" and weakened traditional beliefs, but my mother had no idea what kind of books they were. Because of this, I was able to read and understand the excellent, sound rhetoric of Thomas Paine's *Rights of Man* and *Common Sense*—brilliant books that were once praised by bishops before being relentlessly smeared by history. There was also an uncensored copy of *Gulliver's Travels*. It might have been heavy reading for a boy, but I don't think it was too much. I have never regretted missing out on a sheltered, overly sanitized upbringing. The satire in the land of Traldragdubh made my blood boil, exactly as the author intended, but the Houyhnhnms (the horse-like race) made me hate Jonathan Swift, and I never really liked horses after that. I also remember reading translated versions of Voltaire's *Candide* and *Rasselas*. And, as enormous as it was, I genuinely believe I read all twelve volumes of Edward Gibbon's *The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire* from start to finish, albeit in a hazy sort of way, occasionally checking the atlas as I went.

This reading only fueled my thirst for more, and I began sneaking into the main drawing room to raid the bookcases. Before my reckless, unholy bravery was finally discovered by Ann, the old head housemaid, I had devoured quite a few books. I remember trying to read a translation of Plato's *Republic*, but finding it completely uninteresting at the time; I was far too young to grasp it. But *Vathek*—*Vathek* was magnificent. That kicking incident! The part where everyone was forced to kick each other!

Whenever I think of *Vathek*, my childhood memories of the grand drawing room at Bladesover always come flooding back.

It was a vast, long room with a dozen or more floor-to-ceiling windows facing the park. Each window was draped with elaborate, heavy-fringed silk or satin curtains, topped with a canopy (I think that's what it's called), and featured solid white shutters that folded back into the thick walls. At either end of this huge, quiet space was an immense marble fireplace. The fireplace near the bookcases featured a statue of the she-wolf nursing Romulus and Remus, supported by statues of Homer and Virgil. I can't remember the design of the fireplace at the other end. Above one fireplace hung a flat, boasting portrait of Frederick, Prince of Wales, twice life-size but softened by the oily gleam of the paint. Above the other was an equally massive group portrait of deceased Drew ancestors, dressed scantily as forest gods against a stormy sky. Down the center of the ornate ceiling hung three chandeliers, each dripping with hundreds of crystal pendants. And on the endlessly wide carpet—which felt as large as the lost land of Sarmatia from my atlas—sat islands and archipelagos of floral chintz-covered chairs, sofas, tables, massive Sèvres porcelain vases on pedestals, and bronze statues of men and horses. Somewhere in this wilderness, I would stumble upon a massive harp next to a lyre-shaped music stand, and a grand piano.

My secret book-borrowing missions were daring, dangerous operations.

I would come down the main servants' stairs—which was allowed—but the illegality began at a small landing where I carefully slipped through a red baize-covered door. A narrow hallway led to the main hall, where I would scout for Ann, the old head housemaid. The younger maids were friendly, so they didn't matter. Once I knew where Ann was, I would dash across the open space at the foot of the grand staircase—a staircase no one had properly descended since powdered wigs went out of fashion—and make for the drawing room door. A creepy, life-sized porcelain Chinese figure would wobble and smirk at my lightest footstep. That door was the danger zone. It was a double door set into a thick wall, meaning I couldn't hear the swish of a feather duster on the other side before opening it. Darting into those massive rooms in pursuit of abandoned crumbs of knowledge—it was a very rat-like existence, wasn't it?

I also remember finding Langhorne's translation of *Plutarch's Lives* on those shelves. It feels strange now to think that I gained my sense of pride, self-respect, my understanding of the state, and the seeds of public spirit in such a sneaky, secretive way. It is equally strange that it was an ancient Greek man, dead for eighteen hundred years, who taught me those things.


VI

The school I attended was exactly the kind of school the Bladesover system permitted. The prestigious public schools that briefly flourished during the Renaissance had been completely taken over by the ruling class. The lower classes weren't expected to need schools at all. So, the middle class got the schools it could afford: private schools run by unqualified frauds who were free to set up shop anywhere. My school was run by a man energetic enough to get a diploma from the College of Preceptors, and given how cheap the tuition was, I have to admit the place could have been much worse. The building was a gloomy, yellow-brick house on the outskirts of the village, with an outbuilding made of plaster and lath serving as the classroom.

I don't remember my school days being unhappy. In fact, I recall a lot of chaotic, mixed-up fun. But, at the risk of being wildly misunderstood, I cannot claim we were good or refined students. We fought a lot. Not orderly, rules-based boxing, but vicious, murderous "scraps" where we actually tried to hurt each other, sometimes even using our boots. If nothing else, it made us tough. Several of the boys were the sons of London pub owners; they knew the difference between a real fight intended to cause pain and a structured boxing match, practiced both, and swore like sailors despite their young age. Our cricket pitch was bare dirt around the wickets, and we played sloppily, constantly arguing with the umpire. Most of the teaching was done by an awkward, nineteen-year-old lout in cheap, ready-made clothes who taught terribly. The headmaster and owner taught arithmetic, algebra, and Euclidean geometry himself, and even taught trigonometry to the older boys. He had a strong bias toward mathematics, and looking back, I think he actually taught us quite well, even by the standards of a British public school.

We enjoyed one priceless privilege at that school: spiritual neglect. We treated each other with the rough, natural simplicity of young boys. We mouthed off, threw punches, and smacked each other on the head. We thought of ourselves as Indian braves, cowboys, and other honorable figures, not young English gentlemen. We never felt the heavy burden of singing hymns like "Onward, Christian Soldiers," and we weren't forced into premature religious devotion while sitting on cold oak benches at Sunday service. All of that was wonderful. We spent our rare pennies at the village shop on uncensored reading material—*Boys of England* magazines and cheap penny dreadfuls. It was fantastic stuff, pre-dating writers like H. Rider Haggard and Robert Louis Stevenson. It was poorly printed and badly illustrated, but absolutely brilliant for us. On half-holidays, we were granted the rare freedom to wander for miles across the countryside in groups of two or three, talking about anything and dreaming wild dreams. There was so much in those walks! To this day, the landscape of Kent—with its low, wide horizons, hop farms, golden wheat fields, round oast houses, square church towers, and a backdrop of rolling downs and steep woods—still fills me with the pleasure of scenery, mixed with a faint sense of adventure.

That beauty. We smoked sometimes, but no one ever taught us what proper "boyish" things were. For example, we never raided an orchard. Even though there were orchards all around us, we thought stealing was a sin. We did swipe the occasional apple, turnip, or strawberry from the fields, but it felt like a shameful, criminal act, and we always felt guilty afterward. We had our days of adventure, but they were spontaneous events, our very own adventures. 

There was one hot day when a few of us, walking toward Maidstone, were tempted by the devil to skip ginger beer and got dreadfully drunk on ale instead. And there was a time when our young minds caught the Wild West fever, and we even thought about buying pistols. Young Roots from Highbury came back with a revolver and cartridges, and one holiday afternoon, six of us set out to live a free, wild life. We fired our first shot deep inside the old flint mine at Chiselstead and nearly blew our eardrums out. Then we fired again in a primrose-filled wood near Pickthorn Green, where I gave a false alarm about a gamekeeper, and we ran away in a panic for a whole mile. 

After that, Roots suddenly took a shot at a pheasant on the high road near Chiselstead, and young Barker terrified him by lying about how strict the poaching laws were. In the end, we hid the pistol in a dry ditch outside the school field. A day or two later, we went back for it, ignored the soot and rust on the barrel, and tried to shoot a rabbit from three hundred yards away. Young Roots fired at a molehill from twenty paces, kicking up a cloud of dust, burning his fingers, and scorching his face. Once the weapon showed this strange habit of spitting fire back at the shooter, we never fired it again.

One of our main thrills was cheeking the people riding in vans and carts on the Goudhurst road. Other memorable moments included getting myself covered in white dust at the chalk pits past the village, and catching jaundice after swimming stark naked with three other boys in the stream across Hickson’s meadows, with old Ewart leading the way. Those free, imaginative afternoons! They meant so much to us, and they did so much for us. In those days, every stream flowed from the undiscovered source of the Nile, and every thicket was an Indian jungle. 

And I say this with pride: I invented our best game. I got the idea from the drawing room at Bladesover House. We found a wood with a "No Trespassing" sign and acted out the Retreat of the Ten Thousand—the ancient Greek mercenaries' march. We bravely hacked our way through patches of stinging nettles blocking our path, and when we finally reached the open high road, which we called the sea, we made sure to fall to our knees and weep. Sometimes we would burst out like this, crying and cheering, right in front of startled travelers. I usually played the famous general, Xenophon—and please make sure to draw out the "o." I had my own way of pronouncing classical names. To me, Socrates rhymed with Bates. Unless some strict scholar glares at me for it, I still use those fond, outdated mispronunciations. My brief dip into Latin during my days as a pharmacist never washed that habit away. Well, even if I carelessly mispronounced the names of those great men of the past, at least I met them as living, equal beings, speaking a living tongue. All in all, my school days could have been much worse, and thankfully, they gave me a lifelong friend.

That friend was Ewart. After many ups and downs, he is now an artist carving monuments in Woking. Oh, he was so lanky he looked like he was spilling out of his clothes! He was a long-limbed country boy, ridiculously tall compared to my compact build. Except that he didn’t have a black mustache under his nose back then, he had the same round, knobby face he has today, the same bright, lively hazel eyes, the same steady stare, thoughtful pauses, and sly answers. Surely no boy ever played the fool as well as Bob Ewart, and no one had a better knack for filling the world with wonder. Ordinary things vanished when Ewart was around; everything he described became rare and unforgettable. I first heard the word "love" from him, though by then, its thorns were already stuck deep in my heart. I know now that he was the illegitimate son of the brilliant, careless artist Rickmann Ewart. He brought the light of a relaxed world—one that hadn't turned its back on beauty—into the brewing ferment of my mind.

I won him over by telling him the story of Vathek, and from then on, we were inseparable storytellers. We merged our minds so completely that sometimes I wonder how much of Ewart rubbed off on me, and how much he became a second version of myself.

VII

Then, just after I turned fourteen, came my tragic disgrace.

It happened during my summer holidays, and it was all because of the Honorable Beatrice Normandy. As people say, she "came into my life" before I was twelve.

During the quiet lull after the three great women left for the year, she appeared without warning. She moved into the old nursery upstairs, and every day she had tea with us in the housekeeper's room. She was eight years old and came with a nurse named Nannie. At first, I didn’t like her at all.

Nobody liked this invasion of the downstairs rooms. The two of them caused trouble, which was a terrible offense. Driven by duty to her charge, Nannie made demands that left my mother speechless. She would ask for eggs at odd hours, demand that milk be boiled again, or refuse a perfectly good milk pudding—not asking politely, but giving orders as if it were her right. Nannie was a dark, long-faced, quiet woman in a gray dress. She had a sneaky stubbornness that eventually overwhelmed and crushed everyone. She made it clear that she was "under orders," like a character in a Greek tragedy. She was a strange relic of the past, a devoted, trusted servant. In exchange for a lifetime of secure employment, she had handed over all her pride and willpower to the wealthy people who hired her. The deal was unspoken, but binding. In the end, they would give her a pension, and she would die as a resented fixture in some boarding house. Deep down, she had built a massive habit of relying on the upstairs folks, squashing any complaints from her own soul and twisting her very instincts. She seemed stripped of gender and personal pride, pouring a cold, joyless devotion into raising another woman's child—a devotion perfectly suited for an emotionless parting later on. She treated all of us like objects meant only to run errands for her charge. But the Honorable Beatrice was willing to lower herself to our level.

The strange events of later years make it hard for me to clearly remember her childhood face. When I think of Beatrice, I picture the person I came to know later—when I knew her so well that I could draw her today, capturing hundreds of delicate details you might miss just by looking. But even back then, I remember noticing how impossibly soft her skin was, and how her eyebrows were finer than the softest feather on a bird's breast. She was a slightly precocious, fairy-like little girl who blushed easily. She had dark, naturally curly hair that sometimes fell over her eyes, and those eyes could look mischievously dark one moment and clear hazel the next. From the very start, after barely glancing at the rabbits, she decided that I was the only truly interesting thing at the tea table.

The adults would chat in their stiff, boring way, telling Nannie the same tired stories about the park and the village they told everyone else. Meanwhile, Beatrice stared at me across the table with a ruthless curiosity that made me squirm.

"Nannie," she said, pointing a finger. Nannie ignored a question from my mother to listen to her. "Is he a servant boy?"

"Hush," Nannie said. "He is Master Ponderevo."

"Is he a servant boy?" Beatrice repeated.

"He is a schoolboy," my mother said.

"Then may I talk to him, Nannie?"

Nannie glared at me with brutal coldness. "You mustn't talk too much," she told the little girl, cutting her cake into thin strips. 

"No," she added sharply as Beatrice tried to speak again.

Beatrice turned vicious. She glared at me with unjustified hostility. "His hands are dirty," she said, stabbing at her cake. "And his collar is frayed."

Then she focused entirely on her cake, pretending I didn't exist. It filled me with hatred and a burning desire to make her admire me. The next day before tea, for the first time in my life, I washed my hands completely on my own, without anyone telling me to.

That was how we met, and soon her whims brought us closer. She caught a cold and had to stay indoors. She suddenly gave Nannie a choice: either she would throw a massive tantrum—which would involve screaming far too loudly for her frail, wealthy aunt upstairs—or I had to be brought up to the nursery to play with her all afternoon. Nannie came downstairs looking stressed and "borrowed" me. I was handed over to the little creature as if I were some large breed of kitten. I had never played with a little girl before, and I thought she was the most beautiful, wonderful, and radiant thing in the entire world. She found me to be the gentlest of slaves, though I made sure she knew I was fairly strong, too. 

Nannie was amazed at how happily and quickly the afternoon passed. She praised my manners to Lady Drew and my mother, who said she was glad to hear it. After that, I played with Beatrice several times. I still remember her toys as massive, splendid things, way bigger than anything I'd ever played with. We even played carefully with the giant dollhouse on the nursery landing. The Prince Regent had given it to Sir Harry Drew’s first child, who died at age five. It was a pretty good model of Bladesover House itself, held eighty-five dolls, and cost hundreds of pounds. Under her bossy directions, I played with that glorious toy.

When the holiday ended, I went back to school dreaming of beautiful things and begged Ewart to tell me about love. I made up a grand story based on the dollhouse, and when Ewart added his touch, it quickly grew into our very own island city of dolls.

Secretly, I decided that one of the dolls looked just like Beatrice.

There was one other holiday where I caught a glimpse of her, though strangely, my memories of that second visit are blurry. Then a year passed, and my disgrace arrived.

VIII

Now that I sit down to write my story and put things in order, I realize for the first time how scattered and illogical memory can be. We remember actions but forget the reasons behind them. We clearly remember random moments that stand out for no reason—fragments that don't connect to anything or lead anywhere. I must have seen Beatrice and her half-brother quite a few times during my last holiday at Bladesover, but I barely remember what those moments were like. The great crisis of my childhood stands out vividly as a turning point, but when I try to find the details that led up to it, I can't put them in any logical order. 

This half-brother, Archie Garvell, was a new factor. I remember him clearly: a blond, arrogant-looking, lanky boy, much taller than me but probably not much heavier. I also remember that we hated each other instinctively from the start. Yet, I can't remember our first meeting at all.

Looking back at the past is like digging through an abandoned attic ransacked by a crazy thief. I can't even explain why these children were at Bladesover in the first place. They were among Lady Drew's countless cousins, and according to the servants downstairs, they were candidates to inherit the estate. If they were, they didn't succeed. But that massive place, with all its fading glory, fine furniture, and grand traditions, was entirely under the old lady's control. I believe she used that power to torment and manipulate people who wanted a piece of it. Lord Osprey was one of them, and she hosted his motherless child and stepchild partly because he was poor, but also, I think, in a faint hope of finding some affection or spark of imagination through them. Nannie was gone by this second visit, and Beatrice was looked after by a terribly sweet but useless, poor young woman from a military family whose name I never knew. I remember them being wildly unruly and adventurous children. It was also understood that I wasn't a suitable friend for them, so our meetings had to be as secretive as possible. But it was Beatrice who insisted we meet.

I am certain I knew a lot about love at fourteen. I loved Beatrice as fiercely as any grown man could, and in her own way, she loved me back. It’s part of the polite, handy pretense of our society to act like children our age don't think, feel, or know anything about love. It’s amazing how well the English keep up these pretenses. But I can't hide the truth: Beatrice and I talked about love, kissed, and held each other.

I remember parts of one conversation under the bushes by the wall. I was on the park side, and the lady I worshipped was sitting somewhat clumsily on top of the wall. Did I say clumsily? You should have seen the sweet little imp. I can see her perfectly balanced on the wall, with the bright, rustling bushes behind her that I wasn't allowed to step through. And far away in the background, the grand roofline of Bladesover House stood tall against a cloudy sky. We must have been having a very serious, business-like talk about my social standing.

"I don't love Archie," she blurted out of nowhere. Then, leaning forward with her hair falling around her face, she whispered, "I love you!"

But she was also pushing to make sure I wasn't a servant, and never would be.

"You'll never be a servant—ever!"

I swore to that very easily, and it's a promise I've kept naturally.

"What will you be?" she asked.

I quickly ran through the professions in my head.

"Will you be a soldier?" she asked.

"And get yelled at by idiots? No way!" I said. "Leave that to the farm boys."

"But an officer?"

"I don't know," I said, dodging the embarrassing truth. "I'd rather join the navy."

"Don't you want to fight?"

"I'd like to fight," I said. "But there's no honor in being a common soldier."

"I have to be told to fight and be looked down upon while doing it. How could I ever be an officer?"
"Couldn't you be?" she asked, looking at me with doubt. In that moment, the massive wall of our social class seemed to open between us.
So, like any proud boy, I decided to brag my way through the problem. I told her I would join the Navy because I was poor, and boasted that I "knew" mathematics, which army officers didn't. I claimed Admiral Nelson as my hero and proudly spoke of my future at sea. "He loved Lady Hamilton," I said, "even though she was a noblewoman. And I will love you."
We were just getting to that point when the dreadful governess called out, "Beatrice! Beatrice!"
"Nasty woman!" the young lady said, trying to continue our conversation, but the governess made it impossible.
"Come here!" she said suddenly, holding out a grubby little hand. As I stepped very close, she ducked her head over the wall until her dark, foggy hair tickled my cheek.
"You are my humble, faithful lover," she demanded in a whisper, her flushed, warm face almost touching mine, her eyes dark and shining.
"I am your humble, faithful lover," I whispered back.
Then she wrapped her arm around my head, offered her lips, and we kissed. Even as a young boy, I trembled all over. That was how we shared our first kiss.
"Beatrice!" The voice was fearfully close now.
With one wild kick of her black-stockinged leg, the young lady vanished. A moment later, I heard her casually and cleverly making up excuses for why she hadn't answered the governess.
Feeling like a criminal, I decided it was best to stay out of sight. I slipped around the corner into the West Wood and wandered alone through one of the winding fern valleys that made the Bladesover estate so diverse, lost in daydreams and playing by myself. For that day and many days after, the kiss on my lips felt like a seal, planting the seeds of dreams every night.
I also remember an expedition the three of us—she, her half-brother Archie Garvell, and I—made into the West Wood. (The two of them were supposed to be playing in the shrubbery.) We pretended to be Indians, built a teepee out of beech logs, stalked imaginary deer, crept up to watch rabbits feeding in a clearing, and nearly caught a squirrel. The game was full of constant arguing between Archie and me because we both insisted on being the main character. I only managed to keep the upper hand because I had read ten times as many books as he had. I also scored points by knowing how to find the eagle shape inside a fern stem.
Somehow—I don't remember exactly how it happened—Beatrice and I, hot and messy, crawled into the tall ferns to hide from Archie. The huge leaves towered over us, five feet high. I led the way since I knew how to crawl through the brush without shaking the leaves and giving us away. In warm weather, the ground under the ferns is wonderfully clear and smells faintly sweet. The stems are black at the bottom and turn green at the top. When you crawl on your stomach, it feels like a miniature tropical forest. I led, and Beatrice followed. When the green clearing appeared ahead, I stopped. She crawled up to me and pressed her hot little face close to mine. Once again, she breathed near my face, suddenly threw her arm around my neck, pulled me down to the ground beside her, and kissed me over and over. We kissed, hugged, and kissed again without saying a word. Then we stopped, stared at each other hesitantly, and suddenly felt a bit awkward and confused. We crawled out, only to be easily caught by Archie.
That memory is very clear to me, along with other hazy ones. I remember old Hall, the gamekeeper, going out with his gun to shoot jackdaws, which was another shared experience, though I don't recall the details. Then, finally, the memory of our fight in the Warren stands out. Like most places in England called a "warren," it wasn't a rabbit enclosure. It was a long, sloping hill covered in thorns and beech trees, with a path that served as an alternate route to the downhill carriage road between Bladesover and Ropedean. I don't know how the three of us ended up there, but I vaguely guess it had something to do with the governess visiting the people at the Ropedean vicarage.
While talking about a game, Archie and I suddenly started arguing over Beatrice. I made what I thought was a fair offer: I would be a Spanish nobleman, she would be my wife, and he would play an entire tribe of Indians trying to kidnap her. Asking a boy to play a whole Indian tribe with the chance to capture such a great prize seemed like an attractive offer. But Archie suddenly took offense.
"No," he said. "We can't do that!"
"Can't do what?"
"You can't be a gentleman, because you aren't one. And you can't pretend Beatrice is your wife. It's... it's disrespectful."
"But..." I said, looking at her.
Archie must have still been holding a grudge from something else that happened that day. "We let you play with us," he said, "but we can't allow things like that."
"What nonsense!" Beatrice said. "George can if he wants to."
But Archie got his way. I let him have his way and only started to get angry a few minutes later. By then, we were still arguing about what to play next. Nothing seemed to please everyone.
"We don't want to play with you at all," Archie said.
"Yes, we do," Beatrice said.
"He drops all his 'h's when he talks."
"No, I don't!" I snapped in a moment of anger.
"There you go!" he yelled. "You just said 'E' instead of 'He'! E! E! E!"
He pointed his finger at me, striking right at my deepest shame. The only response I had was to charge at him. "Whoa!" he cried out as I attacked him with a dark scowl. He stepped back into a decent fighting stance, blocked my punch, hit my cheek, and laughed with surprise and relief that he actually landed a blow.
That turned me into a creature of pure, murderous rage. He might have been as good at boxing as me, or even better—he didn't know yet that I knew how to fight—but I had been in a few bare-knuckle brawls to the bitter end. I was used to giving and taking brutal pain, and I doubt he had ever been in a real fight. Within ten seconds, I sensed his weakness. I realized everything about the modern English upper class: they never push to the painful limit, they hide behind rules and petty ideas of honor that ruin true honor, and they want praise for things they only half-finish.
He seemed to think his first hit and a few others would change the outcome, and that I would give up when my lip started bleeding onto my clothes. So, less than a minute into the fight, he lost his aggression, fighting back only in short bursts while I battered him however I wanted. I breathlessly and fiercely demanded to know if he had had enough, just like we did at my school. I didn't realize that his refined upbringing and high-class rules made it equally impossible for him to fight back and win, or to just give up.
I clearly remember Beatrice dancing around us during the fight, thrilled in a very unladylike way. I was too busy to hear much of what she said. But she was definitely cheering for both of us, and looking back now—maybe just with the cynicism of getting older—I think she was cheering for whoever she thought was winning.
Then Garvell, stumbling backward under my fierce punches, tripped over a large piece of flint. Following the tradition of my class and school, I immediately threw myself on top of him to finish the fight. We were wrestling wildly on the ground when we realized a dreadful interruption had arrived.
"Shut up, you idiot!" Archie hissed.
"Oh, Lady Drew!" I heard Beatrice cry out. "They're fighting! They're fighting terribly!"
I looked over my shoulder. Archie's desire to stand up became unstoppable, and my urge to finish the fight completely vanished.
Two elderly ladies dressed in black and purple silk, fur, and dark, shiny jewelry had arrived. They had walked up through the Warren while their carriage took the easier road up the hill, and stumbled right into us. Beatrice immediately ran to them for protection, standing beside and slightly behind them. Archie and I stood up, looking defeated. The two old ladies stared at us with poor eyesight, clearly horrified. I had never seen Lady Drew's reading glasses tremble so violently.
"You haven't been fighting, have you?" Lady Drew asked.
"You have been fighting."
"It wasn't a fair fight," Archie snapped, glaring at me accusatorily.
"It's Mrs. Ponderevo's George!" Miss Somerville said, adding the crime of ungratefulness to my obvious sin.
"How could he dare?" Lady Drew cried out, looking terrifying.
"He broke the rules," Archie said, gasping for breath. "I slipped... and he hit me while I was down. He kneeled on me."
"How could you dare?" Lady Drew said.
I pulled out an expertly rolled-up handkerchief, wiped the blood from my chin, but offered no excuse for my "daring" behavior. For one thing, I was too out of breath to speak.
"He didn't fight fair," Archie sobbed.
From behind the old ladies, Beatrice watched me closely, without any anger. I think she was fascinated by how the busted lip changed my face. Through my confusion, I vaguely realized I couldn't say that these two had been playing with me. That would break the rules of their upper-class world. In that difficult moment, I decided to stay stubbornly silent and accept whatever consequences came my way.

IX

The justice system at Bladesover made an absolute mess of my case.
I sadly have to admit that the Honorable Beatrice Normandy, at the age of ten, betrayed me, abandoned me, and lied terribly about me. In truth, she was terrified of me and felt guilty. She wanted to escape the thought of being my "engaged lover" and the faint memory of our kiss. Her betrayal was shameful but entirely human. She and her half-brother lied in perfect harmony, painting me as a thug who randomly attacked his social betters. They claimed they were just waiting in the Warren when I came up and bothered them.
Looking back, considering the evidence she heard, Lady Drew's decision was actually reasonable and merciful.
The punishment was delivered to me by my mother, who I honestly believe was even more shocked by my social rebellion than Lady Drew. She lectured me at length about her ladyship's kindness and the wicked arrogance of my behavior, finally reaching the terms of my punishment. "You must go to young Master Garvell and beg his pardon."
"I won't beg his pardon," I said, speaking for the first time.
My mother paused, not believing her ears.
I folded my arms on her tablecloth and delivered my stubborn little ultimatum. "I won't beg his pardon no matter what," I said. "Understand?"
"Then you will have to go away to your Uncle Frapp's house in Chatham."
"I don't care where I have to go or what I have to do. I won't beg his pardon," I said.
And I never did.
From that moment on, it was me against the world. Maybe my mother felt some pity for me deep down, but she never showed it. She took the young gentleman's side. She tried very, very hard to make me say I was sorry for hitting him. Sorry!
I couldn't explain myself.
So I was exiled, sent to Redwood station in the horse-drawn cart with Jukes the coachman driving in cold silence. My few belongings were packed in a small fake-leather suitcase in the back.
I felt I had many reasons to be bitter. The game, by any standards I knew, had started out fair... But what made me most bitter was that the Honorable Beatrice Normandy had completely rejected me and run away as if I were a leper, without even taking the chance to say goodbye. She could have at least done that! What if I had told on her? But a servant's son is treated just like a servant. She had forgotten that, and now she remembered.
I comforted myself with wild fantasies of returning to Bladesover one day, powerful and ruthless like the Roman general Coriolanus. I don't remember the details, but I'm sure I imagined myself being incredibly generous and forgiving...
Well, anyway, I never said I was sorry for beating up young Garvell, and to this day, I'm still not sorry.
