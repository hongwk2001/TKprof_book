Chapter 3
The Wimblehurst Apprenticeship
1

Except for that one time I got emotional by the grave, I handled all these experiences pretty casually, as far as I can remember. With the easy adaptability of youth, I had already completely changed my world. I stopped thinking about my old school routine and pushed the memories of Bladesover—the massive aristocratic estate where my mother worked as a housekeeper—aside to process later. I threw myself into my new life in Wimblehurst, with the pharmacy at its center. I started studying Latin and medicine, focusing on the present with all my heart.

Wimblehurst is a very quiet, gray town in Sussex, rare for southern England because it's mostly built out of stone. I found its clean cobblestone streets, quirky sharp turns, and the refreshing park on the edge of town quite picturesque and pleasant. The whole area is under the control of the Eastry family. It was their influence and their desire to keep things dignified that kept the local train station nearly two miles away. 

Eastry House is so close it overshadows the whole town. If you walk across the marketplace—past the old jailhouse and the wooden stocks—and go by the huge, gray pre-Reformation church that looks like an empty skull, you immediately reach massive iron gates. Looking through them, down a long driveway lined with yew trees, you can see the bright white, grand facade of the mansion. Eastry House was much larger than Bladesover and a far more complete example of the 18th-century estate system. It controlled not just two villages, but a whole borough, sending its own sons and relatives to Parliament almost by right for as long as it held the voting power. Everyone was a part of this system. Everyone, that is, except my uncle. He stood outside of it and complained.

My uncle was the first real crack I saw in the massive facade of the aristocratic world that Bladesover had shown me. My uncle didn't respect Bladesover or Eastry at all. He didn't believe in them, and he couldn't even see them for what they really were. He’d make up strange phrases about them, endlessly rambling about wild, unbelievable ideas.

“This place,” my uncle said one quiet summer afternoon, standing in the open doorway and looking out at the town, “needs to wake up!”

I was in the corner, sorting through patent medicines.

“I’d love to let a dozen young Americans loose in here,” he said. “Then we’d see some action.”

I checked off Mother Shipton’s Sleeping Syrup on my list; we had just cleared out our display stock.

“Things have to be happening *somewhere*, George,” he burst out, his voice rising in frustration as he walked back into the small pharmacy. He fiddled with the display of empty fancy soap and perfume boxes at the end of the counter. Then he spun around restlessly, shoved his hands deep into his pockets, and pulled one out to scratch his head. “I have to do *something*,” he said. “I can't stand it.

“I need to invent something. And push it... I know I could.

“Or maybe write a play. There’s a lot of money in plays, George. What would you think if I wrote a play? There are so many things to do.

“Or maybe the stock exchange.”

He started whistling in that thoughtful way of his.

“By God!” he swore. “This isn't the real world—it’s cold mutton fat! That’s what Wimblehurst is. Cold, stiff mutton fat! And I’m buried in it up to my armpits. Nothing ever happens here, and nobody *wants* anything to happen except me! Up in London, George, things are happening. And America! George, I swear I wish I'd been born American, where things actually move.

“What can anyone do here? How can you grow? While we’re sleeping here, our money is dripping away into Lord Eastry’s pockets for rent, while men are up there...” He pointed vaguely over the dispensing counter toward London, waving his hand to show how busy it was, then gave me a wink and a knowing smile.

"What do they do there?" I asked.

"Hustle," he replied. "They do big things! Glorious things. Like margin trading. Ever heard of that, George?" He sucked air through his teeth. "You put down maybe a hundred pounds, and buy ten thousand pounds' worth of stock. See? That's a one percent margin. If the price goes up one percent, you sell and double your money. If it goes down—poof! It's gone. Then you try again! You can double your money every day, George. Men make fortunes or lose everything in a single hour. And the shouting! Zzzz... Well, that's one way, George. But there's another way—cornering the market!"

"Isn't that for big players?" I ventured.

"Sure, if you're dealing in wheat or steel. But imagine tackling something small, George. Just a little thing that only takes a few thousand pounds to control. Like drugs, for example. You risk everything you have on it. Pick a drug—say, ipecac. You buy up a lot of ipecac. Buy all there is! See? There you go! The supply of ipecacuanha isn't unlimited—it can't be!—and people desperately need it. Or take quinine! You bide your time, wait for a tropical war to break out, and then you snatch up all the quinine. Where is it? People have to have quinine for malaria, you know. Right? Zzzz.

"Good lord! There's no end to the little things you could corner. Dill-water—all those crying babies needing it for tummy aches. Eucalyptus, cascara, witch hazel, menthol—all the toothache remedies. Then there are antiseptics, curare, cocaine..."

"That would be pretty annoying for the doctors," I pointed out.

"They have to look out for themselves! By Jove, yes. They'll cheat you if they can, and you cheat them right back. Just like bandits. That makes it exciting. That's the romance of business, George. It's like you're up in the mountains! Imagine holding all the quinine in the world, and some millionaire's spoiled wife gets sick with malaria. That's having them in a tight squeeze, George! The millionaire would pull up in his motorcar outside, offering you any price you name. That would wake Wimblehurst right up. Lord! You have absolutely no vision down here. None at all. Zzzz."

He drifted into a daydream, muttering fragments like: "Fifty percent upfront, sir. Hand over the security tomorrow. Zzzz."

Back then, the idea of cornering the market on a drug struck me as a reckless monkey trick that no one would ever be allowed to pull off in real life. It sounded like the kind of nonsense someone would make up just to get a laugh out of Ewart and spark even wilder ideas. I thought it was just my uncle's way of talking. But I've learned better since then.

The whole basis of making money in the modern world is predicting what people will need soon, hoarding it so it's out of reach, and then bargaining your way to wealth. You buy up land where people will eventually want to build houses, or you secure rights that block crucial developments, and so on. Of course, a naive boy doesn't understand the darker side of human greed. A boy starts out believing that adults are wise. He doesn't realize how haphazard and dishonest the development of laws and customs has been. He assumes that somewhere in the government, there is a power—as strict as a headmaster—ready to stop all these harmful and foolish schemes. 

I admit, when my uncle talked about hoarding quinine, I genuinely believed anyone who tried that would immediately be thrown in jail. Now I know that anyone who actually succeeded would be far more likely to be given a seat in the House of Lords!

My uncle paced back and forth in front of the gold-labeled bottles and drawers for a while, dreaming of cornering this and that. But eventually, he came back down to reality in Wimblehurst.

"You have to be in London when these deals are happening. Down here in the country—!"

"Jerusalem!" he cried. "Why did I ever plant myself here? It's a dead end. The game is over. You've got Lord Eastry, and he owns everything except what his lawyers take. To get any more change around here, you'd have to blow him—and them—up with dynamite. *He* doesn't want anything else to happen. Why would he? Any change would mean a loss for him. He just wants everything to trundle along exactly as it has for the next ten thousand years. One Lord Eastry after another, one parson dying and another stepping in, one grocer dying and another taking his place. Anyone with any real ideas is better off leaving. In fact, they *have* left! Look at all these blessed people here! Look at them! They're all fast asleep, doing their jobs purely out of habit, like they're in a dream. Stuffed dummies could do the job just as well. They've all settled completely into their ruts. *They* don't want anything to happen, either. They're all broken like tamed horses. So there you have it! What are they even alive for?

"Why don't they just hire a clockwork pharmacist?"

He wrapped up the conversation the same way he always did. "I have to invent something—that's what I need to do. Zzzz. Something convenient. Something people want... Forge a new path. Can you think of anything, George? Something everyone wants but nobody has? I mean something you could sell for under a shilling? Well, think about it whenever you've got nothing better to do. See?"

2

I remember my uncle during that early period as young but already a bit overweight. He was constantly restless, fidgety, and talkative, filling my head with all sorts of contradictory ideas. He certainly taught me a lot....

For me, the years in Wimblehurst were a time of rapid growth. I spent most of my free time, as well as much of my time at the shop, studying. I quickly mastered the little Latin I needed for my exams and continued with my math, getting some help from government-sponsored science and art classes at the local grammar school. I eagerly took classes in physics, chemistry, math, and mechanical drawing. My exercise was mainly just going for walks. In the summer there was cricket, and in the winter, football, run by youth clubs that survived by begging for money from wealthy locals and politicians. But I never really cared for those games. I didn't find any close friends among the young men of Wimblehurst. After growing up with city kids in London, these country boys seemed awkward, slow, servile, sneaky, and mean. *We* city boys used to show off, but these locals just dragged their feet and resented anyone who acted differently. We talked loudly, but in Wimblehurst, you only heard what people really thought in sneaky whispers behind their hands. And even then, their thoughts weren't worth hearing.

No, I didn’t like those country boys, and I don't believe the old British estate system breeds good men. People talk a lot of nonsense about the tragedy of people leaving the countryside and the supposedly corrupting influence of city life. In my opinion, the English city dweller—even one from the slums—is infinitely better on a spiritual level. He is braver, more imaginative, and cleaner than his country cousin. I've watched both types when they thought no one was looking, so I know. There was something about my peers in Wimblehurst that disgusted me, though it's hard to explain. Heaven knows we were rough enough at my boarding school in London, but the Wimblehurst boys lacked the vocabulary or the courage for the things we did—like swearing, for instance. Instead, they showed a kind of dull, genuine creepiness. Yes, 'creepiness' is the right word—a truly low attitude. Whatever trouble we city boys got into back in London, it always had a spark of romantic imagination, no matter how rough. We read adventure magazines like *Boys of England* and told each other stories. In the English countryside, there are no books, no songs, no plays, not even any bold acts of rebellion. Those things either never arrived or were taken away and hidden generations ago, leaving their imaginations stunted and brutal. I think that is the real disadvantage of the rural English man. Because I know this, I don't join in the common complaints about the countryside losing its people to the "furnace" of the cities. Yes, they starve and suffer there, but they come out of it stronger, and they come out of it with souls.

In the evening, a typical young show-off in Wimblehurst would wash his face until it shined, put on something flashy like a bright vest or tie, and head to the local pub’s billiard room or card parlor. You'd quickly get sick of his slow, fake cleverness, the sneaky look in his dead eyes, and his idea of a "good joke," which was always whispered like a dirty little secret. I can still picture young Hopley Dodd, the auctioneer's son, who was considered the pride and joy of Wimblehurst. He'd sit there in his fur vest, clenching a bulldog pipe, wearing riding pants and boots even though he didn't own a horse. He would lean forward, watching the pool table from under the brim of his perfectly tilted hat. His entire vocabulary consisted of about five phrases. He’d say things like "Hard lines!" or bleat "Good business" in a deep voice. He also had a long, slow whistle that everyone thought was the absolute peak of comedy. He was there every single night.

You also knew he would never admit that *I* was actually good at pool; he treated every shot I made like a lucky accident. I thought I played decently for a beginner, though I'm not so sure now. But young Dodd's constant doubting and his annoying catchphrases eventually killed my desire to hang out at the pub, so I suppose his noise did me some good.

I didn't make any friends among the young men there. And even though I was becoming a teenager, I have no real romances to report. It’s not that I wasn't noticing girls in my mid-teens. I did manage to awkwardly chat with a young dressmaker's apprentice, and a student teacher at the local school was even rumored to be involved with me. But I didn't feel any true passion for either of them. True love was still something I only experienced in my dreams. I only kissed those girls once or twice, and the reality confused me more than it fulfilled my fantasies. They definitely weren't "the one." I will talk a lot about love in this story, but I should warn you now that I was always a rather clumsy lover. I understood physical desire perfectly well—too well, in fact—but I was shy when it came to real love. In all my early attempts at dating, I was torn between physical urges and a romantic fantasy that wanted everything to be perfect and beautiful. And I was haunted by the memory of Beatrice—her kisses in the bushes and on the wall. Somehow, that memory set my standards too high for what Wimblehurst had to offer. I won't deny that I tried a few clumsy, boyish attempts at romance there, but because of all these mixed feelings, nothing ever really happened. I didn't leave any broken hearts or a wild reputation behind. When I finally left, I was still inexperienced and a bit frustrated, carrying only a natural, growing interest in sex and romance.

If I fell in love with anyone in Wimblehurst, it was my aunt. She treated me with a kindness that was only half motherly. She showed interest in my books and my certificates, and she teased me in a way that made me adore her. Without even realizing it, I grew deeply fond of her....

Overall, my teenage years in Wimblehurst were hard-working and quiet. I arrived as a boy in short jackets and left almost a man. They were such quiet years that my biggest memories are studying advanced math one winter and taking a government physics exam. Many different desires were growing inside me, but my main drive was a serious, youthful urge to work, learn, and somehow escape the world of Wimblehurst. I wrote to my friend Ewart often. Looking back, my letters were self-conscious and not very smart, complete with dates written in Latin and quotes that Ewart used to make fun of. I was definitely a bit of a snob back then. But to be fair to myself, it was more than just arrogance about my education. I had a very serious sense of discipline and preparation that I'm not ashamed of at all. I was serious. I was more serious then than I am now, and more serious than any adult seems to be. Back then, I was capable of real effort and noble acts.... Those things are beyond me now. I don't see why, at forty, I shouldn't admit that I respect my younger self. I stopped being a boy very suddenly. I truly believed I was about to step into a bigger, important world and do significant things there. I thought I...

I felt destined to accomplish something meaningful in a world that had a clear purpose. Back then, I didn't realize, as I do now, that life would mostly consist of the world imposing things on me. Young people never seem to understand that reality. And, as I mentioned, my uncle unconsciously played a major role in my education. He likely gave shape and voice to my frustration with Wimblehurst—my desire to escape its clean, picturesque emptiness—which only made my feelings stronger. In a way, having that clarity helped me be patient. "I'll make it to London soon," I would tell myself, echoing his words.

Looking back, I remember him always talking in those days. He talked to me about theology, politics, the wonders of science and art, about human passions and affections, the immortality of the soul, and the peculiar effects of drugs. But more than anything, he constantly talked about success, businesses, inventions, and massive fortunes. He spoke of the Rothschilds, silver tycoons, the Vanderbilts, and the Goulds—nineteenth-century billionaires—along with stock flotations, cash-outs, and the mysterious ways fate deals with men. Of course, all this applied only to places that hadn't completely sunk to the stagnant, lifeless level of "Cold Mutton Fat."

When I recall those early conversations, I always picture him in one of three poses. Sometimes we were in the dispensary behind a tall partition, with him perhaps grinding something in a mortar while I rolled pill dough into long strips and chopped it with a wide, fluted knife. Other times, he stood staring out the shop door past the display of sponges and atomizers while I watched him from behind the counter. Or he would lean against the small drawers behind the counter while I hovered in front, dusting. Thinking of those days brings back the faint, constant scent of perfume in the shop, mixed with streaks of various drug odors. I can still picture the rows of dry, gold-labeled glass bottles lined up in front of the mirror behind him. I remember my aunt sometimes marching into the shop, aggressively cheerful, on a mission to playfully tease her husband. She got a lot of amusement out of the abbreviated Latin on those gold labels. "Ol Amjig, George," she would read mockingly. "And you pretend this is almond oil! Oh my! And that one is mustard. Honestly, George!"

"Look at him, George, trying to look dignified. I'd love to slap an old label right across his belly, just like his bottles, saying 'Ol Pondo.' That must be Latin for 'impostor.' You'd look beautiful with a stopper on top, George."

"You're the one who needs a stopper," my uncle would say, sticking his face out....

My aunt, bless her heart, was quite slim and delicate in those days, with a rosebud complexion and a love for marital banter and gentle horseplay. She spoke with the faintest hint of a lisp. She had a wonderful sense of humor, and once I stopped feeling awkward at meals, I began to see the vast, fragile web of nonsense she had spun around her marriage. It had become her reality. She mocked the world in general and attached the word "old" to more things than I've ever heard before or since. "Here's the old newspaper," she would tell my uncle. "Now don't go sticking it in the butter, you silly old Sardine!"

"What day is it, Susan?" my uncle would ask.

"Old Monday, Sausage," she would reply, adding, "I've got all my old washing to do. Don't I know it!"...

She had clearly been the life of the party among her school friends, and this playful attitude had become second nature to her. It made her incredibly fun for me to be around in that quiet town. Even her normal walk seemed to say "hello!" I think her main goal in life was to make my uncle laugh. Whenever she succeeded—with a new nickname or some bizarre, absurd remark—she would hide behind a mask of fake surprise, looking like the happiest woman on earth. When my uncle finally did laugh, I have to admit it was, as the Baedeker guidebooks say, "rewarding." It started with heavy breathing and snorting before breaking into a clear "Ha ha!" At its peak in those younger days, it involved him tumbling around, doubling over, clutching his stomach, crying, and howling in agony. I never saw him laugh that hard at anyone else; he was usually too serious for that, and he rarely laughed much at all after those early years.

She also threw things at him constantly, determined to keep things lively despite the boredom of Wimblehurst. She threw out-of-stock sponges, cushions, wads of paper, clean laundry, and bread. Once, when they thought the errand boy, the little maid, and I were safely out of the way in the backyard, she smashed a box of eight-ounce bottles I had left out to dry while attacking my uncle with a soft new broom. She occasionally threw things at me, too, though not often. Laughter always seemed to surround her—the three of us sometimes shared fits of hysterics. Once, the two of them came home from church absolutely mortified because they had burst into laughter during the sermon. Apparently, the vicar had tried to blow his nose with a black glove instead of his handkerchief. Afterward, she simply picked up her own glove by the finger and gave him an innocent but piercing sideways glance. That simple move made my uncle explode with laughter. We laughed about it all over again at dinner.

"But it just shows you what Wimblehurst is like," my uncle shouted, suddenly turning serious, "that we'd all laugh at something so silly! We weren't the only ones giggling, either. Not by a long shot! And my God, it was funny!"

Socially, my uncle and aunt were almost entirely isolated. In towns like Wimblehurst, the lives of shopkeepers are always socially isolated. The wives had no one unless they happened to be sisters or best friends with another shopkeeper's wife, while the husbands usually met in the private rooms of local pubs or the billiard room at the Eastry Arms. But my uncle mostly spent his evenings at home. When he first arrived in Wimblehurst, I think he showed off his big ideas and ambition a little too aggressively. After briefly tolerating him, the town rebelled and did its best to turn him into a joke. Whenever he walked into a pub, all conversation stopped.

"Are you here to tell us everything, Mr. Ponderevo?" someone would ask politely.

"Just you wait," my uncle would answer, flustered, before sulking for the rest of his visit.

Or someone would feign perfect innocence and announce to the room, "I hear they're talking about rebuilding all of Wimblehurst. Has anyone else heard? They say it's going to be a real modern, enterprising place—sort of like the Crystal Palace."

"We'll get an earthquake and a plague before we get that," my uncle would mutter, to the immense delight of everyone listening, before adding something inaudible about "Cold Mutton Fat."...

III

We were driven apart by a financial disaster that hit my uncle. At first, I didn’t fully understand what it meant. He had taken up what I thought was an innocent, intellectual hobby he called "stock-market meteorology." He borrowed some of my graph paper, studied it for a while, and decided to track the rising and falling stock prices of certain railway companies, like the Union Pacific.

"There’s really something to this, George," he told me. I had no idea that his "discovery" involved investing all his spare money, along with most of the inheritance my mother had left in trust for me.

"It’s as clear as day," he explained. "Look, here’s one wave pattern, and here’s another! These are the stock prices for the Union Pacific railroad over a month. Mark my words, next week they’ll drop a full point. We’re approaching the steep part of the curve again. See? It’s completely scientific and verifiable. So, we apply it! You buy when it's low and sell when it's high, and that's it!"

I was so convinced this was just a harmless game that I was completely overwhelmed when I finally realized he had taken it tragically seriously.

He took me on a long walk over the hills toward Yare and across the wide, brush-covered commons by Hazelbrow to break the news to me.

"There are ups and downs in life, George," he said, pausing halfway across the wide-open space to look at the sky. "...I missed one factor in the Union Pacific analysis."

"You did?" I asked, startled by the sudden change in his voice. "But you don't mean...?"

I stopped in the narrow, sandy path and looked back at him. He stopped too.

"I do, George. I mean exactly that. I'm ruined! I am completely bankrupt right here and now."

"Then...?"

"The shop is ruined too. I'll have to leave it."

"And what about me?"

"Oh, you! You'll be fine. You can transfer your apprenticeship. And... well, I’m not the kind of man to be careless with trust funds, you can be sure of that. I kept that in mind. There’s still some of it left, George—trust me! It's a decent amount."

"But what about you and Aunt Susan?"

"This isn't exactly how we meant to leave Wimblehurst, George, but we have to go. There will be an auction. All our things will be hauled out and tagged with lot numbers. Ugh! ...It was a fun little house in many ways. Our first one. Furnishing it was quite an adventure... We were very happy..." His face twisted at the memory. "Let's keep going, George," he said shortly, looking as though he were choking back tears.

I turned my back on him and didn't look around again for a while.

"That's how things are, you see, George," I heard him say after a moment.

When we reached the main road again, he stepped up beside me, and we walked in silence for a time.

"Don't say anything at home just yet," he said presently. "It's the fortunes of war. I need to pick the right time to tell Susan, otherwise she'll get depressed. Not that she isn't incredibly strong no matter what happens."

"All right," I said, "I'll be careful." At the time, it felt too selfish to bother him with questions about his responsibility for my money. He let out a small sigh of relief when I agreed and soon started talking cheerfully about his plans. But he occasionally slipped into a sudden, dark mood. "Those others!" he said, as if the thought had just struck him.

"What others?" I asked.

"Damn them!" he said.

"But who do you mean?"

"All those stubborn, slow-moving local shopkeepers! Ruck the butcher, Marbel the grocer. Snape! Good Lord, George, how they’ll laugh at us!"

I thought about him a lot over the next few weeks. I clearly remember the last conversation we had before he handed the shop—and me—over to his successor. Luckily, he managed to sell the business entirely, which included my apprenticeship contract. He even avoided the horror of having our furniture auctioned off.

I remember that on the way to or from that final meeting, Ruck the butcher stood in his doorway and grinned at us, showing his long teeth.

"You half-witted pig!" my uncle muttered. "You grinning hyena." Then he called out loudly, "Pleasant day, Mr. Ruck!"

"Going to make your fortune in London, then?" Mr. Ruck said slowly, clearly enjoying himself.

Our final walk took us along the path to Beeching, up the hills, and almost all the way around to Steadhurst. My feelings were a tangled mess as we walked. By then, I had fully realized that, plainly speaking, my uncle had robbed me. The six hundred pounds my mother had spent her life saving for my education and future business had been eaten away. Most of it had disappeared into the unexpected drop in the Union Pacific stock that was supposed to be a peak. And he still gave no explanation about whatever was left. I was too young and inexperienced to demand the money or know how to get it back, but the thought of it sent streaks of dark anger through my mixed emotions. 

But you know, I also felt deeply sorry for him—almost as sorry as I felt for Aunt Susan. Even back then, I saw right through him. I knew he was weaker than I was. His hopeless, irresponsible childishness was just as obvious to me then as it was on his deathbed. I also saw the imaginative foolishness that somehow redeemed and excused him. Perhaps because of some strange twist in my mind, I found myself trying to defend him, even if it meant blaming my poor old mother for leaving everything in his untrustworthy hands.

I might have forgiven him completely if he had shown even a hint of apology. But he didn't. He kept trying to reassure me, which only annoyed me more. His main concern, however, was for Aunt Susan and himself.

"It's crises like this bankruptcy that truly test a person's character, George," my uncle said. "Your aunt is handling this wonderfully."

He made a groaning noise, as if deep in thought.

"Of course she cried," he continued. I could clearly see the painful proof of that in her swollen face and red eyes. "Who wouldn't? But now she's cheerful again! She is truly a remarkable woman."

"Naturally, we'll be sad to leave this little house. It's a bit like Adam and Eve being chased out of paradise. Goodness, the old poet John Milton—who wrote Paradise Lost—was a genius!"

"He wrote: 'The whole world was before them, where to choose their place of rest, and God's providence was their guide.'"

"Isn't that a beautiful thought, George? Guided by providence! Well, thankfully we don't have to worry about raising kids like Cain or Abel right now!", Cain and Abel were the sons of Adam and Eve, and Cain committed the first murder by killing his brother.,

"Anyway, moving up to London won't be so bad. We might not have the scenery or the fresh air we have here, but there is real life! I've found us some small but very comfortable rooms, considering the circumstances. And I will succeed again. We aren't finished yet. We haven't been beaten. Don't ever think that, George. Before I am done, I will pay back every single penny I owe—twenty shillings to the pound. Mark my words, George, and I will pay you back twenty-five shillings to the pound. I found a job in a major London firm within twenty-four hours. I had other offers, too. If I had gone elsewhere, I could have made four or five shillings more a week. I could name the places. But I told them straight out: I need the wages to live on now, but my real goal is opportunity and growth. We understood each other perfectly."

My uncle puffed out his chest and stared bravely at imaginary employers through his round glasses.

We would walk in silence for a while as he mentally replayed and polished his job interview. Then, he would suddenly blurt out some cliché.

"Life is a battlefield, George, my boy!" he would shout, or, "Life has its ups and downs!"

He ignored or brushed aside my cautious questions about my own situation. "Everything will be fine," he would say, or, "Leave all that to me. I'll take care of it." Then he would quickly change the subject to the philosophical and moral lessons of our situation. What else could I do?

"Never put all your money into a single chance, George. That is the lesson I've learned from this. Always keep some funds in reserve. The odds that I was right were better than a hundred to one. I calculated it later. But we were ruined by that one tiny chance of failure. If I had only held back a little money, I would have immediately invested it in Union Pacific railroad stocks the very next day. When the stock prices went up, I would have made a profit and we would be fine. But that's reality for you!"

His thoughts grew more serious.

"It's when you run into unavoidable bad luck like this, George, that you realize you need religion. Inflexible scientists and thinkers like Spencer and Huxley don't understand that. But I do. I've been thinking about it a lot lately, in bed and everywhere else. I was thinking about it this morning while I shaved. I hope this doesn't sound disrespectful, but God works through those slimmest of chances, George. Do you see? Never be too sure of anything, whether it's good or bad. That is what I've realized. I was so certain of my judgment. I am a very picky man. Would I have risked someone else's trust money on Union Pacific stocks if I didn't think it was a perfectly safe investment? But in the end, it was a disaster!"

"This is a great lesson for me. I started with one hundred percent certainty, and this is where I ended up. In a way, you could see it as God punishing me for my pride. I lay awake at night thinking about that, George. I thought about it while shaving this morning, too, and I think that's the silver lining in all this. Deep down, I'm a mystic when it comes to these things. You can calculate and plan what you want to do, but in the end, does anyone really know what they are doing? When you are most certain you are in control, unseen forces are actually directing things right over your head. In a sense, you are just being swept along. Whether the odds are a hundred to one or one to a hundred, what does it matter? You are simply being led by providence."

It is strange that I listened to him back then with unspeakable contempt. Looking back now... well, I have to ask myself, am I really any better than he was?

"I wish," I snapped, suddenly losing my temper, "that providence would lead you to explain what happened to my money, Uncle."

"I can't do that without some paper to calculate the numbers on, George," he replied. "But don't worry about that at all. Just trust me. Trust me."

And in the end, I had to.

I think the bankruptcy hit Aunt Susan quite hard. As far as I can remember, her cheerful, bouncy energy completely disappeared. There was no more joking around in the shop or scurrying happily through the house. But she didn't make a fuss, and I could only see the faint traces of hidden tears in her face. In the end, she didn't cry. But the rigid, forced calm on her face was more heartbreaking to me than any tears could have been.

"Well," she said to me as she walked through the shop toward the carriage. "We're off, George! Off to our second home! Goodbye!"

She pulled me into her arms, kissed my cheek, and hugged me tightly. Then she rushed into the carriage before I could even reply.

My uncle followed her. His confident and bold behavior seemed entirely out of place for our sad reality, and his face was unusually pale. He turned to the man taking over the counter.

"Here we go!" my uncle said. "When one man falls, another rises. You will find this to be a nice, quiet little business if you run it calmly. It's a very good business. Do you have any other questions? No? Well, if you need to know anything, write to me. I will explain everything to you. Business, the location, the people—anything. Oh, you'll find there is quite a lot of the anti-bile digestive pills in stock. I found that making them calmed my nerves the day before yesterday, so I made them all day. There must be thousands! And where is George? Ah, there you are! I will write to you, George, and explain everything about your money in complete detail. Everything!"

It hit me then, clear as day, that I was truly saying goodbye to Aunt Susan. I stepped out onto the sidewalk. She was leaning forward, her big blue eyes and small face staring intently at the shop. To her, that shop had been both a giant dollhouse and her very own cozy little home.

"Goodbye!" she called out to the shop and to me. Our eyes met for a second, both of us looking a bit lost. My uncle bustled out, gave the driver a few completely unnecessary instructions, and climbed in beside her.

"All set?" the driver asked.

"Yes," I replied.

The driver lightly flicked his whip, and the horse started to move. Aunt Susan's eyes found me again.

"Keep up with your science and all your hobbies, George, and make sure to write—"

"Tell me when you become a professor," she said cheerfully.
She stared at me for another second, her eyes growing wider and brighter, her smile fixed. She took one last look at the bright little shop still proudly displaying the "Ponderevo" sign, then quickly ducked back into the cab out of sight. The cab pulled away, and I saw Mr. Snape, the barber, watching it leave from inside his shop with quiet satisfaction, exchanging smiles and knowing nods with Mr. Marbel.

IV

As I said, I was left in Wimblehurst as part of the shop's fixtures, staying on with its new owner, Mr. Mantell. Mr. Mantell plays no further part in this story except that he wiped away all traces of my uncle. As soon as the novelty of his arrival faded, I found Wimblehurst to be a dull and lonely place, and I began to miss my Aunt Susan terribly. The summer advertisements for cough syrup were taken down. The red, green, and yellow colored water bottles were put back in their places. The wooden horse used to advertise veterinary medicine—which my uncle used to paint like a champion racehorse while making sizzling noises—was painted white again. I threw myself into studying Latin even more fiercely than before, until I passed my preliminary exams and could drop it. After that, I focused on math and science.

There were classes in electricity and magnetism at the local Grammar School. I won a small beginner's prize in my first year and a medal in my third. I also did well in chemistry, human physiology, and physics. There was a broader, less focused subject called physiography, where we skimmed through various sciences. We studied geology as an evolutionary process from ancient microscopic fossils to modern buildings, and astronomy as a record of perfect, unchanging planetary movements. I learned from poorly written, condensed textbooks with barely any experiments, but I still learned. That was only thirty years ago, and I remember learning that the electric light was a useless, expensive toy, the telephone was a bizarre novelty, and electric trains were a practical joke. As far as I knew, there was no argon, no radium, and no white blood cells, and aluminum was a rare, expensive metal. The fastest ships in the world only traveled at nineteen knots, and aside from a few crazy people, nobody thought humans could ever fly.

A lot has changed since then. But when I last saw Wimblehurst two years ago, its pleasant quietness hadn't changed at all. No new houses had been built in the town itself, though there was some construction near the train station. But despite the quiet, it was a good place to study. I quickly completed the minor requirements for the Pharmaceutical Society exams. Since candidates weren't allowed to take the final exam until they were twenty-one, I needed to pass the time and keep my studies on track. So, I decided to study for a Bachelor of Science degree at London University, which seemed like an incredible but almost impossible goal. A degree in math and chemistry really appealed to me, even if it felt terrifyingly out of reach. So, I got to work. Eventually, I had to take a holiday and go to London to officially enroll, which is how I ended up seeing my aunt and uncle again. In many ways, that trip was a turning point. It was my very first real impression of London. I was nineteen, and by sheer coincidence, the closest I had ever been to that massive human wilderness was a short visit to the town of Chatham. And Chatham was the biggest town I had ever seen. Because of this, London hit me with a shocking freshness, like the sudden revelation of a completely different side of life.

I arrived on a dark, smoky day via the South Eastern Railway. Our train was half an hour late, constantly stopping and starting. Past Chiselhurst, I noticed the growing number of suburban houses. We passed through more and more housing developments, and the empty spaces of vegetable gardens and dull grass began to shrink. Soon, we entered areas tangled with railway lines, large factories, massive gas tanks, and sprawling, smelly slums made up of tiny, dingy homes. The number of houses, along with their grime and poverty, kept increasing. Here a massive pub, there a public school, and over there a bleak factory. Far to the east, a strange, out-of-place forest of ship masts and rigging loomed for a while. The houses became more crowded, stacking up into dense apartment buildings. I was amazed by this endless world of grimy people. Industrial smells of leather and breweries drifted into the train car, and the sky grew darker. The train rumbled loudly over bridges and rattled past streets packed with delivery carts. I looked down and suddenly heard a loud roar as we crossed the River Thames. I caught a glimpse of tall warehouses, gray water crowded with barges, and wide banks of indescribable mud. Finally, we pulled into Cannon Street Station. It was a massive, filthy cavern, with trains packed tightly across the floor and more porters standing along the platforms than I had ever seen in my life. I stepped off the train with my suitcase and fought my way through the crowd, realizing for the first time just how small and weak I could feel. In a world like this, a medal in electricity and magnetism felt completely useless.

Afterward, I took a cab down a narrow street that rushed like a canyon between tall warehouses, and I looked up in amazement at the blackened, gray stones of St. Paul's Cathedral. The traffic in the bustling Cheapside district—which was mostly horse-drawn buses back then—seemed massive, and the noise was deafening. I wondered where all the money came from to pay for so many cabs, and what kind of business could support this endless, shoving stream of rushing men in silk hats and long coats. Down a side street, I found the alcohol-free Temperance Hotel that Mr. Mantell had recommended. The porter in the green uniform who took my bag seemed to look down on me quite a bit.

V

The university enrollment process kept me busy for four full days. Finally, I had a free afternoon, so I made my way through a confusing maze of crowded streets to find Tottenham Court Road. London was just so huge! It seemed endless. It felt like the entire world had been replaced by crowded building fronts, construction fences, and paved streets. I eventually got there and asked around until I found my uncle working behind the counter of the pharmacy he managed. It didn't look like a particularly high-end business.
"Lord!" he said as soon as he saw me. "I was just hoping something would happen!"

He greeted me warmly. I had grown taller, and he seemed to have gotten shorter, smaller, and rounder, but otherwise, he hadn't changed. He looked a bit shabby to me. After some mysterious haggling in the back room, he managed to get permission to leave with me. The silk hat he pulled out and put on had seen better days, but he was just as cheerful and confident as ever.

"You've come to ask me about all that business," he cried. "I never did write to you."

"Oh, among other things," I said, suddenly feeling overly polite. I decided to change the subject away from his management of my inheritance to ask how Aunt Susan was doing.

"We'll get you out of here," he suddenly said. "Let's go somewhere. It's not every day we have you in London."

"It's my first visit," I replied. "I've never seen London before." This made him ask what I thought of it, and the rest of our conversation was exclusively about London, ignoring all other minor topics. He took me down the Hampstead Road almost to the Cobden statue, turned into some back alleys on the left, and finally arrived at a chipped front door that opened with his key. It was one of many similar old front doors, topped with fanlights and cards advertising rooms to let. We entered a dingy hallway that was not only narrow and dirty but also depressingly empty. When he opened a door, my aunt appeared. She was sitting by the window with a small sewing machine on a little bamboo table. Her "work"—a plum-colored walking dress in what seemed to be its most detailed stage of tailoring—was scattered all over the room.

At first glance, she seemed a bit plumper than before, but her complexion was as clear and her china-blue eyes as bright as ever.

"London doesn't cover me in soot," she said.

I was glad to see she still enjoyed teasing my uncle. "What brings you poking your nose in here at this hour, you old Gubbitt?" she snapped when he appeared, still spotting the humor in things with her experienced eye. Upon seeing me behind him, she let out a little cry and stood up, beaming. Then her expression grew serious.

I was surprised by my own emotion at seeing her. She stood an arm's length away, placed a hand on each of my shoulders, and looked at me with joyful scrutiny. After a brief hesitation, she gave me a quick peck on the cheek.

"You're a grown man now, George," she said as she let me go, and continued to look at me for a while.

Their living arrangement was typical for London. They occupied the first floor, known as the "dining-room floor," of a small house, and used a small, inconvenient basement kitchen that had originally been a scullery for the maids. The two rooms—the bedroom at the back and the living room at the front—were divided by folding doors that were never opened anymore, especially not when guests were present. Naturally, there was no bathroom, and running water was only available in the kitchen below. My aunt did all the housework herself. She could have afforded to hire help if the house hadn't been so awkwardly designed as to make it impossible. The only option would have been a live-in maid, but there was nowhere for her to sleep. The furniture was their own; some of it was secondhand, but it looked generally cheerful to me, showcasing my aunt's love for cheap, brightly patterned muslin. Looking back, it must have been a very cramped and uncomfortable home, but at the time, I accepted it as just the way things were. I didn't realize how absurd it was for respectable people without debt to live in a house so clearly unsuited to their needs, so wasteful of effort, and so devoid of beauty. It's only now, as I write this, that I see the fundamental absurdity of an intelligent society living in such makeshift homes. It feels like wearing secondhand clothes.

You see, it was a natural outcome of the class system that places like Bladesover—the traditional country estates—represent. Vast areas of London, miles of streets, seem originally built for typical middle-class families of the early Victorian era. There must have been a huge building boom in the 1830s, 40s, and 50s. Streets must have sprung up rapidly in places like Campden Town, Pentonville, Brompton, West Kensington, and various minor southern suburbs.

I doubt these houses were used by single families for long. I suspect that almost from the start, tenants improvised by taking in lodgers and subletting. These houses featured basements where servants—an older, more submissive generation willing to live underground like troglodytes and climb endless stairs—worked and lived. The dining room, slightly above ground level and fitted with folding doors, was where the large family ate wholesome meals of boiled or roast meat with soggy potatoes, followed by pie, and where they read or sewed in the evenings. The drawing room upstairs, also with folding doors, was kept for rare guests. That was the vision of the industrious builders. But even as these houses went up, society was changing, destroying the very family structures meant to fill them. Better transport allowed wealthier middle-class families to move to the suburbs. Meanwhile, education and factory jobs reduced the supply of tough, obedient girls willing to endure the drudgery of basement work. At the same time, a new class of poorer middle-class people emerged—clerks and employees like my uncle—for whom no housing was built. These people had no clear idea of how they should live, and they didn't fit into the traditional Bladesover class hierarchy that dominates our thinking. No one cared about housing them decently, leaving them at the mercy of the free market. They had to squeeze into whatever space they could find. The landlords survived this clumsy development without financial loss. Increasingly, these houses fell into the hands of married workers, struggling widows, or retired servants with savings. These people took on the quarterly rent and barely scraped by, subletting furnished or unfurnished rooms.

Looking back, I remember a poor, gray-haired old woman who looked as if she had just woken up in a dustbin. She came out into the basement area and looked up at us as we left the front door for our tour of London. She was one of those subletters. She made a precarious living by renting the whole house and subletting it piece by piece, which earned her enough for food and a place to sleep in the attic and basement. If she couldn't keep her rooms rented, she would end up destitute and be replaced by another desperate soul.

It is a foolish society that forces entire classes of useful, honest, and loyal people to live in such squalid, unsuitable housing. Relying on the savings and naivety of old women to meet landlords' demands is hardly a sound economic system. But if anyone doubts this is still happening today, they only need to spend an afternoon looking for a room in any of the London areas I mentioned.

But where was I in my story? My uncle had decided I needed to see London. As soon as my aunt put on her hat, the three of us went out to make the most of what was left of the day.

VI

My uncle was delighted that I had never seen London before.

My uncle immediately acted as if he owned the entire metropolis. "George, London is a place that takes a lot of understanding," he said. "It's an incredible place. Absolutely massive. It's the richest city in the world, the largest port, and the greatest manufacturing center. It's the capital of the Empire—the center of civilization and the very heart of the world! Look at those sandwich men down there, carrying advertisement boards on their chests and backs! Check out the hat on the third guy! What a sight! You don't see poverty like that back in Wimblehurst, George! And a lot of those men were actually top-honors graduates from Oxford University. Brought low by alcohol! It's an amazing place, George. It's like a whirlpool or a dangerous sea that can toss you high into the air or drag you all the way down to the bottom."

I only have a very confusing memory of our afternoon tour of London. My uncle led us all over the place, showing us his version of the city while talking wildly and taking us on a completely random route. Sometimes we walked, and other times we climbed up onto the roofs of the tall, swaying horse-drawn omnibuses that acted as the buses of the time, moving through the heavy, chaotic traffic. At one point, we even stopped for tea at an Aerated Bread Shop, a popular and affordable chain cafe from the nineteenth century. But I clearly remember when we walked down Park Lane, a wealthy residential street next to the park, under a cloudy sky. My uncle kept pointing out the mansions of the lucky and rich, sighing with deep admiration.

I also remember that as he spoke, my aunt kept watching my face, as if she were trying to use my expressions to figure out whether or not to believe his big claims.

"Have you ever fallen in love, George?" she suddenly asked while eating a sweet bun in the tea shop.

"I'm too busy for that, Aunt," I replied.

She took a huge bite of her bun and waved the remaining piece of bread in her hand to show that she had more to say.

"How are you going to make your big fortune?" she asked as soon as she could speak again. "You haven't told us that part yet."

"The electricity business," my uncle answered for me, taking a deep breath after gulping down his black tea.

"Well, if I can ever manage to do it," I added. "Personally, I think I'd be happy with a smaller success rather than a massive fortune."

"We are going to make a huge fortune, and it's going to happen all of a sudden," she said.

"Or at least, that's what this old man keeps saying." She nodded her head toward my uncle.

"He won't tell me when it's going to happen, so I can't get anything ready. But the day is coming. We're going to ride around in a horse-drawn carriage and have our own garden. A garden as big as a bishop's."

She finished her bread and brushed the crumbs off her fingers. "I'll be really glad when we get that garden," she added. "It's going to be a truly enormous one, with a rose garden and all sorts of things. It will have fountains, ornamental pampas grass from South America, and even a greenhouse."

"You'll get all of it, for sure," my uncle said, his face turning a little red.

"And our carriage will be pulled by grey horses, George," she continued. "It's so nice to imagine these things when I'm feeling down. We'll eat dinner out at restaurants all the time. And when we go to the theater, we'll sit in the stalls—the most expensive seats right in front of the stage. And we'll have money, money, and more money."

"You might be joking," my uncle said, humming a little tune for a moment.

"As if an old dolphin like him could ever make any real money," she said, suddenly looking at his side profile with a flash of affection. "He'll just roll around like a dolphin in the water."

"I'm going to do something big," my uncle said. "Just you wait! Tsk, tsk!" He tapped a one-shilling coin against the marble table.

"Well, when you do finally make some money, you'll have to buy me a new pair of gloves first," she said. "The fingers on these are completely past the point of sewing up. Look at this, you old cabbage!" She shoved the torn glove right under his nose and made a funny, fierce face.

At the time, my uncle just smiled at her jokes. But later, when I went back to the pharmacy with him—the cheap little shop actually got busier in the evening and stayed open late—he brought the subject back up in a low, explaining tone of voice. "Your aunt is a little impatient, George. She keeps nagging me. I suppose it's only natural. Women just don't understand how long it takes to build up a solid foundation. Yes... I am quietly building my foundation right now in a specific direction. Now, look here... I have this space. I have my three assistants. Tsk, tsk. If you judge my situation by my immediate income, it might not look as good as I deserve, but strategically, it's brilliant. It's exactly what I want. I am making my plans and gathering my strength for the attack."

"What kind of plans are you making?" I asked.

"Well, George, you can trust one thing: I never rush anything. I think about this and that, but I don't go talking about it carelessly. This is... no, actually, I don't think I can tell you. But then again, why shouldn't I?"

He stood up and closed the door that led out into the shop. "I haven't told anyone else," he said, sitting back down. "But I owe you something."

His face flushed a little, and he leaned forward across the small table toward me.

"Listen closely!" he said.

I leaned in to listen.

"Tono-Bungay," my uncle said very slowly and clearly.

I thought he was telling me to listen to some strange noise coming from far away. "I don't hear anything," I said reluctantly, looking at his eager face. He just smiled, looking completely undefeated. "Listen again," he said, and repeated, "Tono-Bungay."

"Oh, you mean that word!" I said.

"Huh?" he said.

"But what does it mean?"

"Aha!" my uncle said, swelling up with pride and joy. "What is it? That's exactly the question you should be asking! What won't it be?" He poked me hard in what he thought were my ribs. "George," he cried. "George, just keep your eyes on this space! There's much more to come."

And that was all the information I could get out of him.

I believe that was the very first time the word "Tono-Bungay" was ever spoken out loud on planet Earth. Well, unless my uncle had been muttering it to himself in his bedroom, which is actually very likely. When he said it, it certainly didn't feel like the start of a new era. If someone had told me that evening that this strange word would act as a magical password, unlocking all the pride and pleasure hidden behind London's gloomy exterior, I would have laughed out loud.

"Let's get down to business," I said after a pause, forcing a cold effort. I brought up the issue of the trust fund—the inheritance he was managing for me.

My uncle sighed and leaned back in his chair. "I wish I could explain all these business matters as clearly to you as they are to me," he said. "But anyway... go ahead! Say what you need to say."

VII

After I left my uncle that evening, I fell into a deep depression. It seemed that my uncle and aunt were living—I know I've used this word too much, but I have to use it again—*dingy* lives. They seemed to be drifting in an endless crowd of dingy people. They wore worn-out, shabby clothes and lived uncomfortably in cheap, second-hand houses. They walked back and forth on sidewalks always coated with a thin layer of slippery, greasy mud, under grey skies that offered no ray of hope—nothing but dinginess until the day they died. It seemed absolutely certain that my mother's small life savings had been completely swallowed up without a trace. It also seemed clear that my own future was simply to fall into this vast, dingy ocean of London and be swallowed whole sooner or later.

London, which I had once dreamed would be an adventurous escape from the sleepy country town of Wimblehurst, had vanished from my hopes. I could vividly picture my uncle pointing at the grand mansions in the wealthy neighborhood of Park Lane, while accidentally revealing the frayed edges of his shirt cuffs. I could still hear my aunt's voice: "So I'll be riding in a carriage then. At least, that's what the old man says."

My feelings toward my uncle were incredibly complicated. I felt deeply sorry for both him and my Aunt Susan, because it seemed obvious they would be stuck living this way forever. At the same time, I was angry at his endless vanity and incompetence. His foolishness had stolen my chances of independent study and trapped my aunt in those grey rented rooms. When I got back to Wimblehurst, I couldn't hold back; I wrote him a letter full of childish sarcasm and genuine bitterness. He never replied.

Believing this was my only way out, I threw myself into my studies more fiercely and stubbornly than ever before. After some time, I wrote to him again in a softer tone, and he sent back a vague, evasive reply. After that, I tried to push him out of my mind and just kept working.

Yes, that first harsh encounter with London during the wet, cold gloom of January had a massive impact on me. It was a crushing disappointment that marked a turning point in my life. I had imagined London as a vast, free, welcoming, and adventurous place. Instead, I found it messy, cruel, and completely indifferent.

I didn't realize at all what human warmth might be hidden behind those grey building fronts, or what weaknesses that threatening exterior might soon reveal. It is a common mistake of youth to overestimate the intentional "will" behind things. I couldn't see that the dirt, the despair, and the misery of London might simply be because the city was like a foolish, senile old giantess—too lazy and stupid to keep herself clean or put on a brave face for the world. No! I was caught in the same kind of delusion that caused people to burn witches in the seventeenth century. I actually believed that London's grubby disorder had some sinister, grand, and deliberate purpose behind it.

My uncle's exaggerated gestures and empty promises filled me with doubt and a kind of fear for him. Inside this vast, unforgiving world, he seemed like a lost little creature, too foolish to keep quiet. I also felt full of pity and a deep tenderness for Aunt Susan, who was doomed to follow his erratic, unpredictable fortunes, mocked by his grandiose promises.

I would learn better later on. But throughout my final year at Wimblehurst, I worked with the terrifying image of London's grim, dark underbelly haunting my soul.
