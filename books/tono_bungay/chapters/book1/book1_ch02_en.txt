Chapter 2
My Entry into the World and the Last I Saw of Bladesover, the great aristocratic estate,
1
When I was banished from Bladesover House—and at the time, everyone thought I was gone for good—my mother vengefully sent me first to her cousin Nicodemus Frapp. In the Victorian middle class, it was common courtesy to call adult cousins "uncle." Later, she sent me to become a fully contracted apprentice to my actual uncle, Ponderevo.
But I ran away from my cousin Nicodemus's care and returned to Bladesover House.
My cousin Nicodemus Frapp was a baker in a back street—more like a slum, really—just off the miserable, narrow main road connecting the beautiful towns of Rochester and Chatham in Kent. To be honest, he was a shock to me. He was completely controlled by his young, plump, endlessly pregnant, and constantly "sick" wife. He was a stooped, slow-moving, dark-haired man who did everything reluctantly. Flour was dusted in his hair, his eyelashes, the wrinkles of his face, and the seams of his coat. I've never had a reason to change my first impression of him; he remains a dreadful memory, a walking caricature of incompetent simplicity. As I remember him, he was the absolute perfection of a slave mentality.
He had no pride in his appearance. He believed dressing up and looking nice wasn't for "people like him." So, he let his wife—who had no talent for it—cut his black hair whenever she felt like it, and he let his fingernails grow until they were disgusting to look at. He had no pride in his business and zero initiative. His only "virtues" were avoiding certain behaviors and working hard.
"Your uncle," my mother used to say, "isn't much to look at, and he's not fun to talk to, but he's a Good, Hard-Working Man." In their twisted worldview, there was a kind of pathetic honor in grueling labor, no matter how pointless it was. Another point of honor was getting up before dawn just to stumble around exhausted.
It was made very clear to me that this Good, Hard-Working Man would have considered owning a pocket handkerchief to be absurdly fancy and spoiled.
Poor old Frapp! He was a dirty, crushed product of Bladesover's magnificent wealth. He didn't fight back against the world at all. He was drowning in small debts that eventually grew big enough to ruin him. Whenever any actual effort was required, his wife would suddenly complain of pains and her "delicate condition." God sent them many children, but most of them died, which only gave them more practice in the "virtue" of submissively accepting tragedy.
Surrendering to "God's will" was their excuse for ignoring every duty and emergency. There were no books in their house. I doubt either of them could read for more than a minute at a time anyway. It amazed me that, day after day, fresh food was simply piled on top of the stale bread and permanent trash that covered their living room table.
You might wonder if they even felt uncomfortable in this dark, dusty life, except they were clearly searching for comfort. They found it on Sundays—not in heavy drinking or wild parties, but in imagining the blood of Christ. They gathered with twenty or thirty other gloomy, unwashed people, all wearing dull colors to hide the dirt, in a small brick chapel with a broken, wheezing organ.
There, they found comfort in the belief that everything fair, free, and beautiful in life—all ambition, pride, honor, and joy—was doomed to burn in hell forever. They saw themselves as God's chosen insiders, eagerly watching Him mock His own creations. Or at least, that's how I remember them. What they enjoyed even more than the idea of God punishing the lucky, the brave, and the happy was the absolute certainty that they themselves were predestined for Heavenly Glory.
"There is a Fountain, filled with Blood
Drawn from Emmanuel's Veins,"
they would sing. I can still hear the wheezing drone of that hymn. I hated them with the harsh, unforgiving judgment of a teenage boy, and a pang of that hatred still hits me now. As I write this, the sounds and images come rushing back. I see those obscure, undignified people: a fat woman with asthma; an old Welsh milkman with a tumor on his bald head who acted as their intellectual leader; a loud shopkeeper with a massive black beard; his pale, heavily pregnant wife; and a hunched tax collector wearing glasses.
I can hear their talk about souls, using ancient, worn-out phrases from the dry ports of the Middle East—talk of the "balm of Gilead," "manna in the desert," and gourds providing shade and water in a thirsty land. I also remember how, after the service, their conversations sounded religious but were actually medical, with the women gathering to whisper about pregnancies and childbirth. Since I was just a boy, they didn't care if I overheard.
If the Bladesover estate is my key to understanding England, then my absolute certainty that I understand Russia was probably born from watching Uncle Frapp's social circle.
I slept in a dirty, sheet-covered bed with the two oldest surviving Frapp children. I spent my weekdays helping in the chaotic mess of the shop and bakery, running bread deliveries, and dodging my uncle's nosy questions about my relationship with the "Blood of Christ." I also had to listen to his constant complaining that the ten shillings a week my mother paid him wasn't enough to cover my room and board. He desperately wanted to keep that money, but he also wanted more.
There were no books, no comfortable chairs, and no quiet corners in that house to read. No newspapers ever brought news of the outside world into their holy isolation. My horror of it all grew every day. Whenever I could, I escaped into the streets and wandered around Chatham.
The newsstands fascinated me. They sold cheap, poorly printed papers—especially the *Police News*—filled with crude drawings that made even the stupidest person understand a never-ending stream of nasty crimes. There were pictures of women murdered and stuffed into boxes under floorboards, old men beaten by robbers at midnight, people shoved out of moving trains, and happy lovers shot or burned with acid by jealous rivals. I got my first look at nightlife through those ugly illustrations of "police raids" on various clubs.
Mixed in with these crime papers were comics featuring "Sloper," a cartoon character representing the urban working-class Englishman, wandering around with a gin bottle and a fat umbrella. There were also newspapers showing the blank, friendly faces of the Royal Family. They appeared over and over, visiting places, opening buildings, getting married, having babies, and lying in state when they died—doing everything and nothing. They were a well-meaning, completely mysterious race apart from the rest of us.
I have never gone back to Chatham. The impression it left on me is one of miserable, cramped poverty, without a single ray of genuine kindness. Everything there was the exact opposite of Bladesover. In fact, Chatham proved and worsened everything Bladesover represented.
Bladesover claimed to be the real England, the heart of the land. I've already explained how its wide, airy spaces and massive dignity made the local village, church, and vicar's house look small and unimportant. In Chatham, I saw the flip side of that setup. Since the entire vast county of Kent was carved up into great estates for the aristocracy, the leftover people—anyone who wasn't a good tenant farmer, a hard worker, or a submissive member of the Church of England—were shoved out of sight. They were crammed together to rot in places like Chatham, surviving as best they could...

It smelled of a packed garbage can. They were expected to be grateful even for that; that seemed to be the whole idea.

I wandered around this wasteland of crowded dinginess with young, receptive, wide-open eyes. Thanks to the blessing, or curse, of some fairy godmother, I kept asking, "But why, after all?"

I walked up through Rochester once and caught a glimpse of the Stour valley above the town. It was a horrible sight, full of cement works, foully smoking chimneys, and rows of tiny, ugly, uncomfortable, and grimy workmen's cottages. It was my first glimpse of what industrialization must look like on someone else's land. I also spent some hours on the streets facing the river, drawn by the magic of the sea. But I saw barges and ships stripped of any magic, mostly used for hauling cement, ice, timber, and coal. The sailors seemed gross and sloppy to me, and the ships looked clumsy, ugly, old, and dirty. I realized that most sails don't even fit the ships that hoist them, and that poverty can look just as pitiful and wretched on a ship as it does on a person. When I watched the colliers unloading, seeing the workers in the hold filling tiny, silly sacks, and a continuous stream of blackened, half-naked men running back and forth with them on a plank over a thirty-foot drop into filth and mud, I was initially filled with admiration for their courage and toughness. But then I thought, "But why, after all?" The stupid ugliness of all this wasted muscle and endurance really hit home. Among other things, it was obviously wasting and degrading the coal itself. And to think I had imagined such great things about the sea!

Anyway, for a while, that career aspiration was put to rest.

But these impressions only came to me during my free time, and I didn't have much of that. Most of my time was spent doing chores for Uncle Frapp, and my evenings and nights were forced to be spent in the company of my two oldest cousins. The older one was an errand boy at an oil shop and intensely religious; I only saw him in the evenings at meal times. The other was enduring the summer holidays without much excitement. He was a remarkably thin, pathetic, stunted creature whose only lively activity was pretending to be a monkey. I am now convinced he suffered from some undiagnosed illness that sapped his energy. If I met him today, I would think he was a pitiful little thing and feel very sorry for him. Back then, I only felt a mixture of wonder and disgust. He constantly sniffed, would be exhausted after a two-mile walk, never started a conversation, and seemed to prefer his own company to mine. His mother, poor woman, called him the "thoughtful one."

Serious trouble unexpectedly arose from a conversation we had in bed one night. Some particularly religious comment from my older cousin irritated me intensely, and I flatly declared my complete disbelief in the entire concept of revealed religion. I had never spoken a word about my doubts to anyone before, except to Ewart, who had originally sparked them. I hadn't even fully resolved my doubts until the very moment I spoke. But it dawned on me then that the Frapps' entire concept of salvation wasn't just doubtful, it was impossible. I fired this realization into the darkness without hesitation.

My sudden denials certainly terrified my cousin.

At first, they couldn't understand what I was saying, and when they did, I honestly believe they expected lightning and fire to strike immediately. They immediately gave me more room in the bed, and then the older one sat up and expressed his horror at my wickedness. I was already a little frightened by my own boldness, but when he directly challenged me to take back what I said, what else could I do but confirm my rejection?

"There is no hell," I said, "and no eternal punishment. No God would be that foolish."

My older cousin cried out in horror, while the younger one lay there, scared but listening. "So you mean," said my older cousin, when he finally found the courage to argue, "you can just do whatever you want?"

"If you were a big enough jerk," I replied.

Our little voices went on endlessly, and at one point, my cousin got out of bed, made his brother do the same, and knelt in the dark to pray at me. I found that annoying, but I held my ground bravely. "Forgive him," said my cousin, "for he knows not what he says."

"You can pray if you want," I said, "but if you're going to insult me in your prayers, I draw the line."

The last thing I remember from that massive argument was my cousin lamenting the fact that he "had to sleep in the same bed as an infidel!"

The next day, he shocked me by telling the whole story to his father. This was completely against my personal code of conduct. Uncle Nicodemus sprang it on me during lunch.

"You've been saying strange things, George," he said abruptly. "You better watch what you say."

"What did he say, dear?" asked Mrs. Frapp.

"Things I couldn't repeat," he replied.

"What things?" I demanded hotly.

"Ask *him*," my uncle said, pointing his knife at his informer, making me realize exactly what my offense was. My aunt looked at the witness. "Not...?" she asked hesitantly.

"Worse," said my uncle. "Blasphemy."

My aunt couldn't take another bite. I was already feeling a bit guilty about my audacity, and now I was beginning to grasp the magnitude of the trouble I had started.

"I was just talking sense," I said.

I had an even more awful moment later when I ran into my cousin in the brick alley behind the yard, which led back to his grocery shop.

"You tattletale!" I yelled, and slapped him hard across the face. "Come on then," I challenged.

He jumped back, shocked and scared. His eyes met mine, and I saw a sudden flash of resolve. He turned his other cheek to me.

"Hit it," he said. "Hit it. *I'll* forgive you."

I felt I had never encountered a more disgusting way to avoid a beating. I shoved him against the wall and left him there, forgiving me, and went back into the house.

"You'd better not speak to your cousins, George," my aunt said, "until you're in a better frame of mind."

I immediately became an outcast. At supper that night, a gloomy silence was broken by my cousin saying,

"He hit me for telling you, and I turned the other cheek, Mom."

"He's got the devil riding on his back now," my aunt remarked, which made the oldest girl sitting next to me very uncomfortable.

After dinner, my uncle, in poorly chosen words, prayed for me to repent before going to sleep.

"Imagine if you died in your sleep, George," he said. "Where would you be then? Just think about that, my boy." By this point, I was absolutely miserable and scared, and this suggestion really unnerved me, but I maintained an unrepentant attitude. "You'd wake up in hell," Uncle Nicodemus added gently. "You wouldn't want to wake up in hell, George, burning and screaming forever, would you? You wouldn't like that?"

He tried hard to get me to "just take a look at the bakery oven fire" before I went to bed. "It might make you think," he said.

I was the last one awake that night. My cousins slept soundly, with the sleep of the faithful, on either side of me. I decided to whisper my prayers, but I stopped halfway because I felt ashamed, and perhaps also because I thought you couldn't manipulate God like that.

"No," I said, suddenly feeling confident, "damn me if you're cruel enough... But you're not. No! You couldn't be!"

I poked my cousins hard to wake them up, proudly told them what I'd said, and went peacefully to sleep, feeling my act of faith was complete.

I slept well not only that night, but every night since. As far as any fear of divine unfairness goes, I sleep soundly, and I know I will until the end of time. That declaration was a turning point in my spiritual life.

II

But I didn't expect the entire Sunday service to be focused on me.

It was. It all comes back to me—the focused attention, even the faint leathery smell of the room, and the rough texture of my aunt's black dress brushing against my hand. I can still see the old Welsh milkman "wrestling" with my soul; they all wrestled with me, using prayers or lectures. I held my ground firmly, even though their overwhelming certainty made me believe that by doing so, I was definitely and hopelessly doomed to hell. I felt they were probably right, that God was probably just like them, and that ultimately it didn't matter. To simplify things completely, I declared that I didn't believe in anything at all. They argued against me using Bible verses, which I now realize was an unfair way to argue. When I got home, still stubborn and eternally doomed, yet secretly very lonely, miserable, and scared, Uncle Nicodemus took away my Sunday pudding.

Only one person spoke to me like a normal human being on that day of judgment, and that was the younger Frapp boy. He came up to me in the afternoon while I was stuck upstairs with a Bible and my own thoughts.

"Hello," he said, shifting uncomfortably.

"Do you really mean to say there isn't—no one," he said, afraid to say the word.

"No one?"

"No one watching you—all the time."

"Why should there be?" I asked.

"You can't help your thoughts," my cousin said, "anyway. You mean—" He stopped pacing. "I probably shouldn't be talking to you."

He hesitated, then slipped away with a guilty look over his shoulder...

The following week made life completely unbearable for me; these people eventually drove me into an atheism that terrified me. When I found out the soul-wrestling was going to start again the next Sunday, my courage completely failed me.

I happened to see a map of Kent in a stationery store window on Saturday, and it gave me an idea for an escape. I studied it intently for about half an hour on Saturday night, memorizing a route through the villages, and got up and started walking towards Bladesover around five on Sunday morning while my two bedmates were still fast asleep.

III

I remember a little bit, though not as much as I'd like, of my long walk to Bladesover House. The distance from Chatham is almost exactly seventeen miles, and it took me until nearly one o'clock. It was very interesting and I don't think I was too tired, although one of my boots was pinching a bit.

The morning must have been very clear, because I remember looking back near Itchinstow Hall and seeing the Thames estuary, that river that has played such a large part in my life since. But at the time I didn't know it was the Thames; I thought this huge expanse of mud flats and water was the sea, which I had never seen up close before. Out on it were ships, sailing ships and a steamboat or two, heading up to London or out into the vast oceans. I stood for a long time watching them, wondering if I shouldn't have run away to sea after all.

The closer I got to Bladesover, the less sure I became of the kind of welcome I'd receive, and the more I regretted not choosing the other option. I suppose it was the dirty, clumsy look of the ships I'd seen up close that changed my mind about that. I took a shortcut through the Warren, cutting across the corner of the main park to intercept the people coming from church. I wanted to avoid meeting anyone else before I saw my mother, so I went to a spot where the path dipped between banks. Without completely hiding, I stood up among the bushes. This spot had the added benefit of making it impossible to run into Lady Drew, who would be driving around by the main road.

Standing up to ambush them like this gave me a strange feeling of being a bandit, as if I were some kind of intruding outlaw among all these orderly things. It's the first time I remember distinctly having that outlaw feeling, a feeling that has played a large part in my subsequent life. I felt there was no ready-made place for me; I had to force my way in.

Presently, coming down the hill, the servants appeared, straggling in twos and threes. First came some of the garden workers along with the butler's wife, then the two laundry maids, those strange, inseparable old women, then the first footman talking to the butler's little girl. Finally, walking solemnly and breathlessly beside old Ann and Miss Fison, came the black-clad figure of my mother.

My boyish mind decided on a playful entrance.
"Coo-ee, mother," I called out, popping up against the skyline. "Coo-ee!"

My mother looked up, turned very pale, and put her hand to her chest.

I suppose there was a terrible fuss over me. And, of course, I was completely unable to explain why I had come back. But I held my ground stubbornly. "I won't go...

go back to Chatham; I'll drown myself first." The next day, my mother dragged me off to Wimblehurst, aggressively hauling me to an uncle I had never even heard of, even though he lived quite close to us. She didn't tell me a word about what was going to happen, and I was too crushed by her obvious anger and the humiliation of my latest offense to ask for any details. I definitely don't think Lady Drew was "nice" about my sudden reappearance. My banishment was made final, stamped, and approved. I really wished then that I *had* run away to sea, despite the coal dust and misery I had seen in Rochester. Perhaps there were different worlds across the ocean.

IV

I don't remember much of the trip to Wimblehurst with my mother, except the image of her sitting bolt upright, seeming to look down on the third-class carriage we were traveling in, and how she stared out the window, avoiding my eyes, when she spoke about my uncle. "I haven't seen your uncle," she said, "since he was a boy..." She added, somewhat grudgingly, "Back then, people thought he was clever."

She didn't really care much for qualities like cleverness.

"He got married about three years ago and opened his own shop in Wimblehurst... So I assume his wife had some money."

She was thinking back to times she had long since put out of her mind. "Teddy," she finally said, sounding like someone who had been searching in the dark and finally found what they were looking for. "He was called Teddy... about your age... He must be twenty-six or twenty-seven now."

I immediately thought of him as "Teddy" the moment I saw him. There was something about his appearance that instantly fit the name—a certain "Teddiness," you might call it. It's hard to describe in other words. It was like quickness without any real grace, and energy without much intelligence. He darted out of his shop onto the sidewalk—a short figure dressed in gray, wearing gray carpet slippers. You got the impression of a slightly chubby young face behind gold-rimmed glasses, wiry hair sticking up and forward, and an uneven nose that occasionally looked hooked. His body already showed a bit of a belly, or a "bow window," as they say. He jerked out of the shop, stopped on the sidewalk, stared at something in the window with huge admiration, stroked his chin, and then, just as suddenly, shot sideways back through the door, bursting through it as if he were following his own outstretched hand.

"That must be him," my mother said, catching her breath.

We walked past his shop window, which I would soon know by heart. It was a pretty standard pharmacy window, except instead of the usual blue, yellow, and red bottles on top, there was a friction electrical machine, an air pump, and a few tripods and chemical flasks. Among these breakable items was a plaster horse indicating veterinary medicines, and below that were perfumes, sprayers, sponges, soda-water siphons, and similar items. Right in the middle was a neatly hand-painted sign with red lettering that read:

Buy Ponderevo's Cough Syrup *now*.
NOW!
WHY?
Twopence Cheaper than in Winter.
You Store Apples! Why not the Medicine
You are Bound to Need?

Later on, I would recognize this appeal as my uncle's signature style.

My uncle's face appeared above a display of baby pacifiers in the glass door. I noticed his eyes were brown and his glasses left a crease on his nose. It was obvious he didn't recognize us at all. After a hard stare, a look of professional politeness took over, and he threw open the door.

"You don't know me?" my mother panted.

My uncle didn't want to admit he didn't, but he was clearly curious. My mother sat down on one of the small chairs in front of a counter piled high with soap and patent medicines. She opened her mouth to speak, but nothing came out.

"A glass of water, madam," my uncle said, waving his hand in a dramatic curve before darting away.

My mother drank the water and finally spoke. "This boy," she said, "takes after his father. He looks more like him every day... And so I have brought him to you."

"His father, madam?"

"George."

For a moment, the pharmacist was completely confused. He stood behind the counter holding the glass my mother had handed back to him. Then, it clicked.

"By Gosh!" he said. "Lord!" he cried. His glasses fell off. He ducked behind a stack of boxed blood tonic to put them back on. "Eleven thousand virgins!" I heard him yell. He slammed the glass down. "Oriental Gums!"

He dashed out of the shop through a hidden door. We could hear his voice: "Susan! Susan!"

Then he reappeared, hand outstretched. "Well, how are you?" he said. "I was never so surprised in my life. Fancy!... *You!*"

He shook my mother's stiff hand, and then mine, very warmly, holding his glasses in place with his left index finger.

"Come right in!" he cried. "Come right in! Better late than never!" And he led the way into the living room behind the shop.

Coming from Bladesover, the room felt stuffy and small, but it was incredibly comfortable compared to the Frapps' living room. It smelled faintly of old meals. My strongest first impression was that absolutely everything was covered, draped, or wrapped in something. There was brightly patterned muslin wrapped around the gas light fixture in the middle of the ceiling and around the mirror over the mantelpiece. Fabric with ball-fringe hung along the mantel and bordered the fireplace, that was the first time I'd ever seen ball-fringe, and even the lamp on the small desk had a shade that looked like a giant muslin hat. The tablecloth and the window curtains had ball-fringe too, and the carpet was covered in a rose pattern. There were small cupboards on either side of the fireplace, and the alcoves were filled with poorly made shelves crammed with books and decorated with pink scalloped oilcloth. A dictionary lay face down on the table, and the open desk was covered with paper and signs of a recently abandoned project. My eyes landed on a paper with the words "The Ponderevo Patent Flat, a Machine you can Live in," written in large, bold letters. My uncle opened a small cupboard-like door in the corner of the room, revealing the narrowest, most twisted staircase I had ever seen. "Susan!" he yelled again. "Want you. Someone to see you. Surprise."

There was a muffled reply, then a sudden loud thud overhead, as if something had been thrown down in annoyance. This was followed by cautious footsteps descending the winding stairs, and finally, my aunt appeared in the doorway, resting her hand on the frame.

"It's Aunt Ponderevo," my uncle announced. "George's wife—and she's brought her son!" He looked around the room, suddenly darted to the desk, and flipped the paper about the "patent flat" face down. Then he waved his glasses at us. "You know, Susan, my older brother George. I've told you about him a million times."

He bustled over to the rug in front of the fireplace, took his place there, put his glasses back on, and cleared his throat.

My Aunt Susan seemed to be taking it all in. She was a slender, rather pretty woman of twenty-three or twenty-four, and I remember noticing how blue her eyes were and how clear and fresh her skin looked. She had delicate features, a small nose, a pretty chin, and a long, graceful neck extending from her pale blue cotton morning dress. She had a slightly perplexed expression, a small, puzzled wrinkle on her forehead that suggested she was trying—and failing—to follow my uncle's thought processes. It was a look of mild amusement mixed with a sense of hopelessness that had become a habit. She seemed to be thinking, "Oh Lord! What is he going on about *now*?" And as I got to know her better, I realized there was another layer to that expression, a secondary question to "What is he going on about?": "Is he serious this time?" She looked at my mother, then at me, and back to her husband.

"You know," he said. "George."

"Well," she said to my mother, stepping down the last three stairs and holding out her hand, "welcome. Even though it's a surprise... I can't offer you anything to eat or drink, I'm afraid, because there's nothing in the house." She smiled and looked teasingly at her husband. "Unless he mixes something up with his old chemicals, which he's certainly capable of doing."

My mother shook hands stiffly and told me to kiss my aunt.

"Well, let's all sit down," my uncle said, suddenly whistling through his clenched teeth and rubbing his hands together briskly. He pulled up a chair for my mother, raised and lowered the blind on the small window, and returned to his spot on the rug. "I must say," he announced decisively, "I'm very glad to see you."

V

While they talked, I focused almost entirely on my uncle.

I observed him in great detail. I remember his waistcoat was partially unbuttoned, as if he'd gotten distracted while getting dressed, and there was a small cut on his chin. I liked a certain humorous glint in his eye. With the intense fascination of an observant child, I watched how his lips moved—they were a bit crooked, and there was something almost sloppy about his mouth, causing him to lisp and hiss slightly when he spoke. And as he talked, a strange, almost triumphant expression kept appearing and disappearing on his face. He kept adjusting his glasses, which didn't seem to fit his nose right, fidgeted with things in his pockets, or clasped his hands behind his back while staring over our heads. He would often rock up on his toes and drop back onto his heels. He had a habit of sucking air through his teeth, which added a whispering sort of enthusiasm to his speech. It sounded like a soft "Zzzz."

He did most of the talking. My mother repeated what she had said in the shop, "I have brought George to you," and then let the main issue drop for a while. "Is this a comfortable house?" she asked. When he agreed, she added, "It looks... very practical. Not so big that it's hard to manage... Do you like Wimblehurst?"

My uncle replied by asking about the important people at Bladesover, and my mother answered as if she were a close personal friend of Lady Drew's. The conversation stalled for a bit, and then my uncle launched into a lecture about Wimblehurst.

"This place, of course," he began, "isn't exactly where I belong."

My mother nodded, as if she had expected him to say that.

"It doesn't give me any Scope," he continued. "It's a dead end. Nothing ever happens."

"He always wants something to happen," Aunt Susan chimed in. "Someday a whole bunch of things will happen at once, and it'll be too much for him."

"No, it won't," my uncle said cheerfully.

"Is business... slow?" my mother asked.

"Oh! We get by. But there's no Development—no growth. People just come in here to buy pills when they need them, or some horse medicine. They have to actually be sick before they get a prescription. That's the kind of people they are. You can't get them to try anything new or spend a little extra. For example, I've been trying lately to get them to buy their medicines in advance, and in larger quantities. But they won't hear of it! Then I tried to launch this little idea I had: a kind of insurance for colds. You pay a little bit every week, and when you catch a cold, you get a bottle of Cough Syrup as long as you can produce a convincing sniffle. See? But Lord! They have no imagination, they don't get it. There's no energy here, no Life. Live? They just trickle along, and so I have to trickle along too—Zzzz."

"Ah!" my mother said.

"It doesn't suit me," my uncle declared. "I'm the cascading type."

"George was like that," my mother said thoughtfully after a moment.

Aunt Susan continued the thought with a fond look at her husband.

"He's always trying to liven up this old business," she said. "Always putting up new signs in the window or dreaming up some scheme. You wouldn't believe it. Sometimes *I'm* the one who gets a shock."

"But it doesn't do any good," my uncle sighed.

"It does no good," his wife agreed. "This just isn't his element..."

Then a long silence fell over the room.

I had anticipated this pause from the start of their conversation, and I perked up. I knew exactly what was coming: they were going to talk about my father. I was sure of it when I noticed my mother staring at me thoughtfully in the silence, and then my uncle looked at me, and then my aunt did. I tried my hardest to look innocent and a bit dim-witted, but it didn't work.

"I think," my uncle said, "that George might find it more interesting to..."

take a walk around the market square than sit here talking with us. There's a pair of stocks out there, George—very interesting. Old-fashioned punishment stocks."

"I don't mind sitting here," I said.

My uncle stood up and very warmly guided me through the shop. He stood on his doorstep and cheerfully pointed the way.

"Isn't it sleepy here, George, huh? Look at the butcher's dog over there, sleeping in the middle of the street—and it's still half an hour until noon! If the trumpet for Judgment Day sounded, I don't think it would even wake up. Nobody would wake up! The guys up there in the graveyard—they'd just roll over and say: 'Nah—you're not fooling us! You see?'... Anyway, you'll find the stocks just around that corner."

He watched me until I was out of sight.

So, I never did get to hear what they said about my father.

VI

When I got back, my uncle had somehow become a more dominant and central figure. "That you, George?" he called out when the shop bell rang. "Come right in"; and I found him sitting in what looked like the seat of honor in front of the decorated fireplace.

All three of them were looking at me.

"We were just talking about making you a pharmacist, George," my uncle said.

My mother looked at me. "I had hoped," she said, "that Lady Drew would have done something for him—" She stopped.

"In what way?" my uncle asked.

"She might have put in a good word with someone, gotten him a position perhaps..." She clung to the servant's deep-rooted belief that all good things came through the favors of the upper class.

"He's not the kind of boy people do things for," she added, giving up on those dreams. "He doesn't cooperate. If he thinks Lady Drew wants something, he seems to want the opposite. And he's been disrespectful to Mr. Redgrave too... he's just like his father."

"Who's Mr. Redgrave?"

"The local minister."

"A bit independent?" my uncle asked briskly.

"Disobedient," my mother corrected. "He doesn't know his place. He seems to think he can get ahead by disrespecting people and ignoring the rules. Maybe he'll learn his lesson before it's too late."

My uncle stroked his nicked chin, then patted my shoulder. "Have you learned any Latin?" he asked suddenly.

I said I hadn't.

"He'll have to learn a little Latin," he explained to my mother, "to get qualified. Hmm. He could go down to the guy at the local grammar school—it was just recently reopened by the Charity Board—and take lessons."

"What, me? Learn Latin?" I cried out, thrilled.

"Just a little," he said.

"I've always wanted to," I said. "*Latin!*"

I had long been obsessed with the idea that not knowing Latin was a huge disadvantage in life, a point Archie Garvell had drilled into my head pretty intensely. The books I had read at Bladesover all reinforced that idea. Latin felt like a key to freedom for me, in a way that's hard to describe. And suddenly, just when I thought my education was completely over, I heard this!

"It's completely useless, of course," my uncle said, "except for passing exams, but there you have it!"

"You'll learn Latin because you have to learn Latin," my mother said, "not because you want to. And later on, you'll have to learn all sorts of other things..."

The idea that I was going to continue learning, that reading and studying books was still my official duty, wiped away all other concerns. For weeks, I had been pretty sure that those kinds of opportunities were closed to me forever. I suddenly became very interested in this new plan.

"So, will I live here?" I asked. "With you, and study... as well as work in the shop?"

"That's the plan," my uncle said.

I said goodbye to my mother that day feeling like I was in a dream; this new turn of events was so sudden and important to me. I was going to learn Latin! Now that she was past the humiliation of my failure at Bladesover, and had somewhat gotten over her initial strong dislike of asking my uncle for help, she had managed to arrange something that looked like a real future for me. Because of this, a tenderness crept into her manner that was much deeper than in any of our previous goodbyes.

I remember she sat in the train, ready to leave, and I stood at the open door of her compartment. Neither of us realized how soon we would completely stop being a burden to each other.

"You have to be a good boy, George," she said. "You have to study... And you mustn't rebel against the people who are above you and better than you... Or be jealous of them."

"No, mother," I said.

I promised without really paying attention. She was staring right at me, but I was busy wondering if there was any way I could start learning Latin that very night.

Then something touched her heart—a thought, a memory, or perhaps a premonition... The only porter on the platform started slamming the carriage doors shut.

"George," she said quickly, almost as if she were embarrassed, "kiss me!"

I stepped up into her compartment as she leaned down.

She grabbed me and hugged me tightly, which was a very unusual thing for her to do. I noticed her eyes were unusually bright, and then that brightness spilled over her lower eyelids and rolled down her cheeks.

For the first and last time in my life, I saw my mother cry. Then she was gone, leaving me feeling awkward and confused, temporarily forgetting even that I was going to learn Latin, and thinking of my mother as someone entirely new and unfamiliar.

The thought kept returning, even though I tried to push it away; it lodged itself in my memory, waiting for a day when I would understand it better. Poor, proud, rigidly narrow soul! Poor, difficult, misunderstood son! It was the first time it ever occurred to me that my mother might actually have feelings.

VII

My mother died suddenly the following spring, and, according to Lady Drew, very inconsiderately. Her ladyship immediately fled to the seaside resort of Folkestone with Miss Somerville and Fison, planning to stay there until the funeral was over and my mother's replacement was settled in.

My uncle took me to the funeral. I remember a long-running crisis in the days leading up to it because, as soon as he heard the news, he had sent a pair of plaid pants to a dye shop in London to be dyed black, and they didn't come back in time. He got very agitated on the third day and sent a flurry of increasingly angry telegrams, which achieved absolutely nothing. The next morning, he finally gave in, very grumpily, to Aunt Susan's insistence that he wear his formal evening suit. In my memory, his black legs—clad in very thin, shiny black fabric, because the suit clearly dated back to his slimmer youth—stand out like a giant statue blocking my view of my mother's funeral. I was also uncomfortable and distracted by a top hat he had bought me—my first top hat—which, like his, was made more solemn by a wide black mourning band.

I have a somewhat blurry memory of my mother's white-paneled housekeeper's room, and the strange feeling of her not being there. I remember the familiar faces looking different in their black clothes, and I think I recall feeling overly self-conscious because everyone was looking at me. My awareness of the new top hat probably came and went in the middle of all my emotional confusion. But then one memory stands out clearly and sadly from all these trivial, disconnected things: I am walking ahead of all the other mourners, right behind her coffin as it is carried along the churchyard path to her grave, while the old minister's slow voice speaks triumphant, solemn words over my head in a regretful, unconvincing tone.

"I am the resurrection and the life, says the Lord; he who believes in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live: and whoever lives and believes in me shall never die."

Never die! It was a beautiful, bright spring morning, and all the trees were bursting into green leaves. There were blossoms and flowers everywhere; the pear and cherry trees in the gravedigger's garden looked like sunlit snow, daffodils and early tulips nodded in the graveyard flowerbeds, there were daisies everywhere, and the birds seemed to be singing all around. And in the middle of it all was the end of the brown coffin, tilting on men's shoulders and half hidden by the minister's academic hood.

And so we arrived at my mother's open grave.

For a while, I just watched closely, seeing the coffin lowered and listening to the ritual words. It all seemed like a very strange procedure.

Suddenly, as the service was ending, I felt that something important had been left unsaid. I realized she had slipped away in silence, without forgiving me or hearing from me—assurances that were now lost forever. Suddenly, I knew I hadn't understood her. Suddenly, I saw her with affection; I remembered not so much the tender or kind things she did, but how I had gone against her wishes and frustrated her plans. I realized, to my surprise, that beneath all her harshness and strictness, she had loved me. I was the only thing she had ever loved, and until this moment, I had never loved her back. And now she was down there, deaf and blind to me, tragically defeated in her hopes for me, buried so deep she could never know...

I dug my fingernails into my palms and clenched my teeth, but tears blinded me, and I would have choked on sobs if I had tried to speak. The old minister kept reading, there was a mumbled response, and then it was over. I cried silently, internally, and it was only when we had left the churchyard that I could think and speak calmly again.

Burned into this memory are the small, black-clad figures of my uncle and Rabbits, telling Avebury, the gravedigger and undertaker, that "it had all gone very well—very well indeed."

VIII

That is the last thing I'll say about Bladesover. The curtain falls on it here, and it won't appear as a real place in this story again. I did go back there once more, but under circumstances that have nothing to do with my story. In a way, though, Bladesover has never left me; as I said at the beginning, it's one of those powerful, defining impressions that shape the way my mind works. Bladesover explains England; it has come to represent everything that is spacious, dignified, showy, and truly conservative in English life. It is my baseline for understanding society. That's why I've spent so much time describing it.

When I finally went back to the real Bladesover for a random visit, everything felt much smaller than I could have imagined. It was as if everything had shivered and shrunk a little under the touch of the Lichtenstein family. The harp was still in the drawing-room, but there was a different grand piano with a painted lid, an automatic piano player, and a ridiculous amount of "artistic" clutter and trinkets scattered everywhere. The whole place looked like a high-end London showroom. The furniture was still covered in floral fabric, but it wasn't the *same* kind of fabric, even though it tried to be, and the dangling crystal chandeliers were gone. Lady Lichtenstein's books had replaced the brown leather volumes I used to read—hers were mostly gift copies of modern novels, and magazines like the *National Review*, the *Empire Review*, and the *Nineteenth Century and After* were piled on tables next to popular new English books in flashy "artistic" covers, French and Italian novels in yellow paperbacks, and incredibly ugly German art manuals. There was plenty of evidence that her ladyship was dabbling in the Celtic revival, and there were dozens of ugly ceramic cats everywhere—she "collected" china and stoneware cats—in all colors and in every kind of deliberately funny, highly glazed shape.

It's ridiculous to pretend that rich bankers make better aristocrats than old landowners. The only things that make a true aristocrat are pride, knowledge, training, and a sense of duty. These new people weren't an improvement on the Drews at all. It wasn't a case of active, smart people beneficially replacing passive, unintelligent ones. It just felt like the massive, dull stupidity of the old upper class had been replaced by a smaller, more aggressive, and incredibly undignified kind of stupidity, and that was it. I thought Bladesover had gone through the exact same change between the 1870s and the new century that the respectable old *Times* newspaper had gone through, and God knows how much more of traditional British society. These Lichtensteins and people like them don't seem to offer any fresh energy for the country at all. I don't believe in their intelligence or their power—there's nothing new, creative, or revitalizing about them; they just have a chaotic instinct to acquire things. The fact that they and their kind are taking over is just one phase in the slow, massive decay of England's social structure. They couldn't have built a place like Bladesover, and they can't replace it; they just happen to be growing over it—like a fungus on a dying tree.

Well—that was my final impression of Bladesover.
