# Chapter 2: The Ponderevo Delusion

I

Throughout my student days, I never saw my uncle. I occasionally felt a pang of regret, knowing this meant keeping my distance from my Aunt Susan, but I purposely avoided him and kept up a cold, resentful attitude. During all that time, I don’t think I once remembered his boast about that mystic word that was supposed to change the world for us. Still, I hadn’t forgotten it entirely. It lingered as a vague, fleeting memory—which probably explains why I felt an odd sense of personal connection when I saw a new advertisement plastered on the street hoardings:

THE SECRET OF VITALITY,
TONO-BUNGAY.

That was all. It was simple, yet strangely eye-catching. I found myself muttering the word as I walked past. It grabbed your attention like distant cannon fire. "Tono"—what did that even mean? Deep, rich, and unhurried. "Bun-gay!"

Then came my uncle’s astonishing telegram, replying to a hostile letter I had sent him:
"Come to me at once. You are needed. £300 a year guaranteed. Tono-Bungay."

"Good Lord!" I cried out. "Of course! It’s a patent medicine! But what does he want with me?"

In his grand, Napoleonic way, my uncle had forgotten to include an address. The telegram had been sent from Farringdon Road, so after some hard thinking, I replied to "Ponderevo, Farringdon Road," hoping our unusual last name would be enough to reach him.

"Where are you?" I asked.
His reply came back immediately.
"192A Raggett Street, E.C."

The next day, after my morning lecture, I took an unauthorized holiday and went to find him. When I did, he was wearing a splendid new silk hat! It had a wide, rolled brim that was completely out of fashion, and its only real flaw was that it was far too big for him. It sat precariously on the back of his head while he bustled about in his shirtsleeves and a white waistcoat. He welcomed me with an almost holy enthusiasm, completely forgetting my bitter sarcasm and our years of estrangement. His glasses fell off at the sight of me, and his round, expressionless eyes shone brightly. He held out his plump, short hand.

"Here you are, George! What did I tell you? No need to whisper now, my boy. Shout it out! Spread the word! Tell everyone! Tono—Tono—, Tono-Bungay!"

Raggett Street was the kind of thoroughfare where someone seemed to have scattered a massive load of cabbage stalks and leaves. It opened off the top end of Farringdon Street. Number 192A was a shop with a chocolate-colored plate-glass front, plastered with the same posters I had seen on the street. The floor was covered in mud dragged in by dirty boots. Three energetic, rough-looking young men in neckerchiefs and caps were packing paper-wrapped bottles into wooden crates amid a mess of straw and dust. The counter was littered with those same wrapped bottles—a design now incredibly familiar to the world: bright blue paper featuring a grinning giant and printed instructions on how to take Tono-Bungay in practically every situation.

Beyond the counter, I remember seeing a girl coming down a staircase with more bottles. The background was blocked by a tall, chocolate-colored partition. The words "Temporary Laboratory" were painted on it in white letters, and over a door cut into the wood, it said "Office."

I knocked, but no one could hear me over the loud hammering, so I walked in. I found my uncle dressed exactly as I described, clutching a sheaf of letters in one hand and scratching his head with the other while dictating to one of three very busy typists. Behind him was another partition with a door marked "ABSOLUTELY PRIVATE—NO ADMISSION." This partition was made of the same universal chocolate-colored wood up to about eight feet, and glass above that. Through the glass, I could faintly see a cluttered array of crucibles and glass retorts. And—my God! Yes, it was the old air-pump from Wimblehurst! It gave me a little thrill to see it. The electrical machine was there too, though it looked seriously broken. Everything was placed right at eye level, clearly just for show.

"Come right into the sanctum," my uncle said, after finishing a sentence with "yours faithfully." He whisked me through the door into a room that completely failed to live up to the grand promise of the equipment outside. The dingy wallpaper was peeling in places. There was a fireplace, a cushioned armchair, a table holding two or three large bottles, cigar boxes on the mantelpiece, a locked whisky decanter, and a row of soda siphons. He carefully shut the door behind me.

"Well, here we are!" he said. "Going strong! Have a whisky, George? No? Wise man! Neither will I! You can see me working! Hard at it!"

"Hard at what?"

"Read this," he said, thrusting a label into my hand. It was the label that is now one of the most familiar sights in any pharmacy: the slightly old-fashioned turquoise border, the legendary text, the name printed in clear, bold black type, and the strongman standing amidst lightning bolts above two columns of clever lies printed in red. It was the label for Tono-Bungay. As I stared at it, puzzled, he said, "The ship has sailed. The ship is afloat. And I'm afloat too!" Then, in his throaty tenor voice, he burst into song:

"I'm afloat, I'm afloat on the fierce flowing tide,
The ocean's my home, and my ship is my bride!"

"It's a cracking song, George. Well, it's not so much a ship as a liquid solution, but it still works! We're doing it! By the way—hold on a second! I've just had an idea." He hurried out, leaving me to examine the nerve center of his operation at my leisure, while his dictatorial voice boomed from the outer office.

The den struck me as unprecedented and bizarre in its large, dingy, dirty way. The bottles were simply labeled A, B, C, and so on, and the old equipment sitting high on the shelf looked even more useless than when it had been used to impress the people of Wimblehurst. I had no choice but to sit down and wait for my uncle's explanation. I noticed a frock coat with satin lapels hanging behind the door; a dignified umbrella stood in the corner, and a clothes brush and a hat brush lay on a side table. Five minutes later, my uncle returned, checking his gold watch. "Getting on for lunchtime, George," he said. "You'd better come and have lunch with me!"

"How is Aunt Susan?" I asked.

"Brimming with life. I've never seen her so happy. All of this has really lifted her spirits."

"All of what?"

"Tono-Bungay."

"What exactly is Tono-Bungay?" I asked.

My uncle hesitated. "I'll tell you after lunch, George. Come along!" He locked the door to the sanctum and led the way. The narrow, dirty pavement was lined with carts, and we frequently had to dodge avalanches of porters carrying heavy loads to the delivery vans on Farringdon Street. He hailed a passing cab with great authority, and the driver was incredibly respectful. "Schaefer's Hotel," he ordered, and off we went. I grew more amazed by the minute. Schaefer's Hotel was near the corner of Blackfriars Bridge, the second of two large buildings with huge lace-curtained windows.

I have to admit I felt a magical charm in our relative proportions when the two massive doormen in light blue and red uniforms held the inner doors open for us with a deep bow that seemed entirely directed at my uncle. Instead of feeling four inches taller, I felt like we were the exact same height, and that I was just much thinner. Even more respectful waiters took his new hat and dignified umbrella and took our lunch order. He gave it with supreme confidence.

He nodded to several of the waiters.
"They know me already, George," he said. "Just watch. This place is alive! They have an eye for men on the rise!"

We focused on eating for a while, and then I leaned across my plate. "Alright, so tell me now," I said.

"It's the secret of vitality. Didn't you read the label?"

"Yes, but—"

"It's selling like hotcakes."

"But what is it?" I pressed.

"Well," my uncle leaned forward, covering his mouth with his hand, and spoke softly. "It's nothing more or less than..."

(Unfortunately, a twinge of conscience stops me here. Tono-Bungay is still on the market, and people are still buying it—including from me. No, I can't give the secret away.)

"You see," my uncle whispered slowly, his eyes wide and forehead wrinkled, "it tastes good because of [a certain flavoring and aromatic alcohol], and it gives you a kick because of [two very strong tonics, one of which heavily impacts the kidneys]. And [two other ingredients] make it quite intoxicating. Really perks you up. And then there's [the essential secret ingredient]. So there you have it. I found the recipe in an old book—except for [the most dangerous ingredient that attacks the kidneys], which was my own idea! A modern touch! And that's it!"

He went back to managing our lunch.

Soon, he led the way to the lounge—a lavish space decorated in red leather and yellow glazed pottery, filled with endless sofas and armchairs. We settled into two overly stuffed chairs with a Moorish clay table between us, drinking coffee and Benedictine liqueur. I was savoring the luxury of a tenpenny cigar. My uncle smoked a similar cigar like a man used to wealth; he looked energetic, knowledgeable, luxurious, and unexpectedly, like a bit of an upstart. The only slight flaw in our swagger was that we both clearly needed our cigars to be "mild." He leaned diagonally across his massive armchair to whisper in my ear, pulling up his short legs. I mirrored his receptive posture with my longer frame. If an unbiased observer had seen us, I'm sure we would have looked like two very sly, ambitious, and repulsive schemers.

"I want to bring you in on this, George," my uncle said around his cigar, puffing smoke. "For a lot of reasons."

His voice dropped lower, growing more cunning. He explained things that my inexperienced mind couldn't fully grasp. I remember him talking about long-term credit and profit-sharing with a wholesale pharmacy, more credit and future shares with pirate printers, and giving a third of the business to a powerful magazine and newspaper owner.

"I played them off against each other," my uncle said. I understood that instantly. He had gone to each of them in turn, claiming the others had already agreed to join.

"I put in four hundred pounds of my own money," my uncle said. "My entire life savings. And you know—"

He puffed out his chest confidently. "I didn't actually have five hundred pence. At least—"

For a brief moment, he looked genuinely embarrassed. "I did manage to produce the capital. You see, there was your trust fund—I suppose, strictly legally speaking, I should have sorted that out first. Tsk...

"It was a bold move," he continued, shifting the subject from honor to courage. Then, with his characteristic burst of piety, he added, "Thank God it all worked out!

"Now, I suppose you're wondering where you fit in? Well, the truth is, I've always believed in you, George. You've got grit—a stubborn kind of determination. Even if you scrape your shins and get knocked down, you keep going! You'll charge at any goal you set your mind to. I know a thing or two about character, George. Trust me. You have it—" He clenched his fists, thrust them forward, and made an explosive sound: "Whoosh! Yes, you do! I've never forgotten the way you devoured Latin back at Wimblehurst.

"Who-oo-osh! Your science knowledge and all that! Who-oo-osh! I know my own limits. There are things I can do," he whispered, as if revealing his life's greatest secret, "and things I can't. Look, I can create this business, but I can't keep it running smoothly. I'm too chaotic. I'm the pot that boils over, not the steady simmer. You, on the other hand, build the heat steadily. You're like a pressure cooker. That's you—steady, enduring, building up the pressure—and then, who-oo-oo-osh! Come in here and whip these lazy fools into shape. Teach them that 'whoosh.' That's it! That's what I need. You! Everyone else still thinks you're just a boy. Jump in with me and be a man. What do you say, George? Think of the thrill of it—a business on the rise, a real, living thing! Making it soar! Making it spin and buzz! Who-oo-oo-osh!" He waved his hand in the air, drawing mesmerizing circles. "Well?"

His proposal, spoken in hushed, confidential tones, began to take shape. I would dedicate all my time and energy to developing and organizing the business. "You won't have to write a single advertisement or make a single guarantee," he promised. "I'll handle all of that." And the telegram wasn't a bluff; I would get three hundred pounds a year. Three hundred a year! ("That's nothing," my uncle added. "The real prize will be your ten percent share of the vendor's stock when the time is right.")

A guaranteed three hundred a year! To me, it was an astronomical sum. For a moment, I was completely stunned. Could a business like this really generate that much money? I looked around at the opulent furniture in Schaefer's Hotel. Surely, there were many people making incomes like that.

My head was spinning from the unfamiliar liqueur and wine.

"Let's go back so I can look at the operation again," I said. "Let me see the upstairs and look around."

And I did.

"So, what do you think of it all?" my uncle finally asked.

"Well, for one thing," I said, "why don't you have those girls working in a properly ventilated room? Aside from anything else, they'd work twice as fast. And they should put the corks in before they wrap the labels around the bottles."

"Why?" my uncle asked.

"Because sometimes they mess up putting the cork in, and then the label is ruined."

"Come and fix it, George," my uncle said with sudden passion. "Come here and make it run like a machine. You can do it. Smooth everything out, and then make it soar. I know you can. Oh, I know you can."

II

My mind changed very quickly after that lunch. The hazy euphoria from the alcohol quickly gave way to a state of crystal-clear, impartial clairvoyance—a mental state I experience often. It comes and goes. I might lose it for weeks, but it always returns like a traveling judge, summoning up all my impressions, illusions, and passionate, stubborn actions. We went back downstairs into the inner room—the one pretending to be a scientific laboratory with its high skylights, but which was really just a hiding place. My uncle offered me a cigarette, which I took, and I stood before the empty fireplace. He propped his umbrella in the corner, placed his slightly-too-large silk hat on the table, blew his nose loudly, and pulled out a second cigar.

It struck me that he had shrunk significantly since our Wimblehurst days. The "cannonball" he seemed to have swallowed (his belly) was far more obvious and unapologetic than before. His skin had lost its freshness, and the bridge of his nose, pinched by glasses that still didn't fit right, was much redder. His muscles seemed looser, and his movements weren't as quick or sharp. Yet, as he sat there—looking suddenly very small to me—he was blissfully unaware of his own physical decline.

"Well, George!" he said happily, oblivious to my silent critique. "What do you think of it all?"

"Well," I said, "first of all... it's a damned scam!"

"Tut, tut!" my uncle replied. "It's completely legitimate—it's fair trade!"

"If that's trade, then trade is awful," I said.

"Everyone does it. Besides, there's no harm in the medicine—and it might actually do some good. It could do a lot of good, giving people confidence during an epidemic, for instance. See? Why not? I don't see how it's a scam."

"Hmm," I said, shrugging. "You either see it, or you don't."

"I'd like to know what kind of trade isn't a scam in its own way. Anyone who does heavy advertising is just selling something ordinary by claiming it's extraordinary. Look at Chickson—they made him a baronet! Look at Lord Radmore, who built his fortune lying about the alkali in his soap! And his ads were fantastic, too!"

"So you really think bottling this stuff, swearing it's the ultimate tonic, and making poor people buy it with their hard-earned money is honest?"

"Why not, George? How do we know it isn't the ultimate tonic for them?"

"Oh, please," I said, shrugging again.

"It's about faith. You're selling them faith. I admit our labels are a bit aggressive. But it's basically Christian Science. There's no point in making people doubt the medicine. Name one modern business that doesn't have to be... aggressive. It's the modern way! Everyone understands it; everyone expects it."

"But the world wouldn't be any worse off—in fact, it would be slightly better—if you dumped all your medicine down a drain into the Thames."

"I don't agree with that at all, George. For one thing, all our employees would be out of work. Unemployed! I'll grant you that Tono-Bungay might not be as beneficial to the world as, say, the cure for malaria. But the point is, George—it creates trade! And the world runs on trade. Commerce! The romantic exchange of goods and property. Romance. Imagination. Do you see? You have to look at the big picture. Don't miss the forest for the trees! Damn it, George, we have to do these things! There's no other way to survive. What do you plan to do with your life, anyway?"

"There are ways to live without fraud or lying," I said.

"You're being too rigid, George. I'll bet my hat there's no fraud here. But what's your plan? Go work as a chemist for some other businessman and take a salary without getting a share of the profits like I'm offering? That makes a lot of sense! Your money would still be coming from the same 'scams' you're criticizing."

"There are quiet, honest businesses out there. Places that supply good, necessary products without screaming advertisements."

"No, George. You're living in the past. The last businesses like that went bankrupt five years ago."

"Well, there's scientific research."

"And who pays for that? Who funded that massive City and Guilds institute in South Kensington? Enterprising businessmen! They like the idea of having some science going on; they want a handy expert around just in case. And what do you get for doing the research? Barely enough to live on, with no future prospects. They just keep you around to make discoveries, and if they feel like using them, they do."

"I could teach."

"For how much a year, George? How much? I assume you respect the historian Thomas Carlyle? (Lord, his book on the French Revolution is something else!) Apply Carlyle's test: solvency. Look at what the world pays teachers and scientists, and look at what it pays businessmen! That shows you who the world really values. 

There's a rough justice in these big things, George, above the surface injustice. I'm telling you, the world wants trade. Trade makes the world go round! Fleets of merchant ships! Venice! The Empire!"

My uncle suddenly stood up.

"Think it over, George. Think it over! Come up to the new place on Sunday—we've got rooms in Gower Street now—and see your aunt. She asks about you all the time, George, over and over. And she's always nagging me about your inheritance—even though I've always said, and always will, that I'll pay you back every penny with interest. Think it over. I'm not asking for me. I'm asking for you. For your Aunt Susan. For the whole enterprise. For the commerce of this country! We need you desperately. I'm telling you straight: I know my own limits. You could take over this place and make it run! I can just see you doing it, looking sour the whole time. 'Whoosh' is the word, George."

He gave me an endearing smile.

"I have to go dictate a letter," he said, dropping the smile, and vanished into the outer office.

III

I didn't give in to my uncle's temptations without a fight. I held out for a week, agonizing over my life and my future. It was a crowded, messy period of deep thought that even ruined my sleep.

My meeting with the university registrar, my conversation with my uncle, and the sudden, crushing realization that my passion for Marion was completely hopeless—all of it combined to push me into a crisis. What was I going to do with my life?

I remember certain moments of my indecision very clearly.

I remember walking home after my talk with my uncle. I went down Farringdon Street to the Embankment along the river, because I thought the route through Holborn and Oxford Street would be too crowded for thinking. That stretch of the Embankment from Blackfriars to Westminster still reminds me of that massive hesitation.

You have to understand, from beginning to end, I saw the business with my eyes wide open. I saw its ethical and moral bankruptcy clearly. I don't think I ever wavered for a second in my belief that selling Tono-Bungay was a thoroughly dishonest act. I knew the medicine was harmful trash—slightly stimulating, aromatic, and appealing, but highly likely to cause addiction to stronger tonics, and insidiously dangerous to anyone with kidney problems. A large bottle cost about sevenpence to make and package, and we were going to sell it for two shillings and sixpence, plus the medicine stamp tax. 

But what deterred me even more than the dishonesty was the sheer absurdity of the whole thing. I still desperately wanted to believe that human society was, or should be, a sane and just system. The idea that I should spend the prime of my youth building a monstrous packing warehouse, bottling garbage for gullible, depressed people to consume, felt almost insane. My youthful idealism still clung to me. I was certain there had to be a catch in this grand vision of wealth and comfort. I believed that somewhere—perhaps overgrown, but still visible—there was a path of usefulness and honor waiting for me.

As I walked along the Embankment, my urge to refuse the offer grew stronger. When I was with my uncle, he cast a sort of spell that made it impossible to say no outright. Part of it was my lingering affection for him, and part of it was a primal instinct to respect him as my host. But mostly, it was his strange ability to inspire a specific kind of belief—not a belief in his integrity or skill, but a belief in the reciprocal, compliant foolishness of the world. He was silly and chaotic, but somehow his silliness matched the chaos of the universe. And a man has to live somehow. I surprised both of us by stalling.

"No," I had said. "I'll think about it."

As I walked by the river, the environment made my uncle seem insignificant. In my mind, he shrank down to a shabby little man in a filthy back alley, shipping out a few hundred bottles of garbage to fools. The massive buildings to my right—the Inns of Court, the School Board building, Somerset House, the grand hotels, the enormous bridges, and the distant silhouette of Westminster—projected a grey, monumental power that reduced my uncle to the size of a black beetle scuttling across the floor.

But then my eyes caught the illuminated night advertisements on the south side of the river for "Sorber's Food" and "Cracknell's Ferric Wine." They were bright, prosperous signs, and I realized how perfectly they fit in. They were undeniably part of the grand system.

I saw a man rush out of Palace Yard (the courtyard of Parliament), and a policeman touched his helmet in respect. The man’s hat and posture were startlingly similar to my uncle’s. After all, wasn’t Cracknell himself a Member of Parliament?

Tono-Bungay yelled at me from a billboard near Adelphi Terrace. I saw it from afar near Carfax Street. It screamed at me again in Kensington High Street in a perfect uproar. I saw it six or seven times before I reached my lodgings. It certainly felt like it was more than just a pipe dream.

Yes, I thought it over—very thoroughly. Trade rules the world. Or rather, wealth rules the world! It was true. And my uncle was right: the fastest way to get rich is to sell the cheapest possible product in the most expensive bottle. He was terrifyingly right. The Roman emperor said it best: "Money has no smell" (Pecunia non olet). Maybe my great historical heroes were no different from these businessmen; they only seem noble because they lived so long ago. Maybe my flirtation with Socialism was just a foolish dream—foolish because its promises were only conditionally true. Men like William Morris played with it; it gave a spicy, substantial flavor to their aesthetic hobbies. But there would never be enough genuine good faith to make it a reality. They knew it. Everyone knew it, except for a few young fools. 

Lost in thought, I crossed the corner of St. James's Park and barely dodged a pair of prancing grey horses pulling a carriage. A stout, vulgar-looking, but magnificently dressed woman glared at me scornfully from her seat. "Probably a pill-peddler's wife," I thought.

Running through all my thoughts, echoing like a chorus, was my uncle’s masterstroke—his brilliant piece of flattery: "Smooth everything out—and then make it soar. I know you can! Oh, I know you can!"

IV

As a moral compass, my friend Ewart was useless. I had decided to explain my dilemma to him, partly to see his reaction, and partly just to hear how it sounded out loud. I invited him to dinner at an Italian place near Panton Street where you could get a strange, interesting, heavy meal for eighteen pence. He showed up with a nasty black eye that he refused to explain. "It’s not so much a black eye," he said, "as the aftermath of a wild night.... So, what's your problem?"

"I'll tell you when the salad comes," I said.

But I never actually told him. I vaguely mentioned that I was torn between going into business or sticking to teaching, especially given my growing Socialist leanings. Warmed by the rare treat of a sixteen-penny bottle of Chianti, Ewart launched into a monologue without asking for any specifics about my situation.

His thoughts wandered all over the place.

"The reality of life, my dear Ponderevo," I remember him saying dramatically, tapping the table with a pair of nutcrackers, "is the clash of colors... and shapes. Grasp that, and let all these other questions go. The Socialist will tell you one shape and color is right, and the Individualist will tell you another. What does it all amount to? What does it amount to? Nothing! I have no advice for anyone—except to avoid regrets. Be yourself. Seek out whatever your own senses tell you is beautiful. And don't worry about the hangover the next morning.... Because what is a morning, anyway, Ponderevo? It's not like it's the pinnacle of the day!"

He paused for dramatic effect.

"What a load of rubbish!" I cried, struggling to make sense of him.

"Isn't it! And that's my ultimate wisdom on the subject! Take it or leave it, my dear George; take it or leave it." He moved the nutcrackers out of my reach and pulled a greasy notebook from his pocket. "I'm going to steal this mustard pot," he said.

I muttered an objection.

"Just to study the design. I have to design a tomb for some awful old man. A wholesale grocer. I'll put a mustard pot on each corner of his grave. I'm sure the poor devil would love a mustard poultice to cool him down in Hell right now. Anyway—here we go!"

V

In the early hours of the morning, it hit me that the true moral test for this massive dilemma was Marion. I lay in bed, composing speeches about my problem and imagining myself delivering them to her. I pictured her, beautiful and goddess-like, handing down a pure, perfectly worded judgment.

"You see, it means surrendering to the Capitalist System," I imagined saying, using good Socialist jargon. "It means abandoning all my beliefs. We might succeed, we might get rich, but where's the real satisfaction in that?"

Then she would say, "No! That wouldn't be right."

"But the alternative is waiting indefinitely!"

Then, suddenly, she would transform into a goddess. She would turn to me, open and noble, her eyes shining and her arms outstretched. "No," she would say. "We love each other. Nothing dishonorable will ever touch us. We love each other. Why wait to say that, my darling? What does it matter if we are poor and stay poor?"

But of course, the actual conversation didn't go like that at all. The moment I saw her, my late-night speeches felt ridiculous, and all my moral values flipped upside down. I had waited for her outside the "Parsian-robe" dress shop in Kensington High Street and walked her home. I remember how she stepped out into the warm evening light, wearing a brown straw hat that, for once, made her look not just beautiful, but cute.

"I like that hat," I said to start things off. She gave me her rare, delightful smile.

"I love you," I murmured, as the crowd pushed us closer together on the pavement.

She shook her head strictly, but kept smiling. "Be sensible!" she warned.

The pavement on the High Street was too narrow and crowded for a real conversation, and we walked quite a way west before speaking again.

"Look," I said, "I want you, Marion. Don't you understand? I want you."

"Here we go again!" she warned.

I don't know if anyone can understand how a passionate lover, filled with immense admiration and desire, can suddenly feel a flash of pure hatred. But I felt that flash at the serene, self-satisfied tone of her "Here we go again!" It vanished almost instantly. I completely failed to recognize it as a warning of the deep incompatibilities hiding between us.

"Marion," I said. "This isn't a joke to me. I love you; I would die to have you.... Do you even care at all?"

"But what's the point?"

"You don't care," I cried. "You don't care a bit!"

"You know I care," she answered. "If I didn't—if I didn't like you a lot, would I let you meet me and walk with me?"

"Well then," I said, "promise to marry me!"

"If I do, what difference will it make?"

Two men carrying a ladder rudely pushed between us, separating us for a moment.

"Marion," I asked when we were side by side again, "I'm telling you, I want you to marry me."

"We can't."

"Why not?"

"We can't get married—in the street."

"We could take our chances!"

"I wish you wouldn't talk like this. What is the point?"

She suddenly became gloomy. "There's no point in getting married," she said. "I've seen what happens to other girls. When you're single, at least you have a little pocket money; you can go out sometimes. But imagine being married with no money, and maybe having children... you can never be sure...."

She poured out this concentrated philosophy of her social class in jerky, unfinished sentences. She frowned at the sunset, looking dissatisfied, seemingly forgetting I was even there.

"Look here, Marion," I said abruptly. "How much money would you need to marry me?"

"What is the point?" she started to say again.

"Would you marry me on three hundred pounds a year?"

She looked at me for a moment. "That's six pounds a week," she said. "We could manage easily on that. Smithie's brother—no, he only makes two hundred and fifty. He married a typist."

"Will you marry me if I get three hundred a year?"

She looked at me again, this time with a strange gleam of hope.

"If!" she said.

I held out my hand and looked her in the eyes. "It's a deal," I said.

She hesitated, then touched my hand for a second. "It's silly," she said as she did it. "It really means we're—" She stopped.

"Yes?" I said.

"Engaged. You'll have to wait years. What good will it do you?"

"It won't be that many years," I replied.

She thought about it for a moment.

Then she glanced at me with a smile—half sweet, half wistful—that has stayed in my memory forever.

"I like you!" she said. "I would like to be engaged to you."

And, so quietly I could barely hear it, I caught her whisper: "Dear!"

It's strange that, as I write this down, my memory skips over everything that happened between then and now. I feel it all over again, and once more, I am Marion's boyish lover, finding immense joy in those rare, tiny moments of affection.

VI

Finally, I went to the address my uncle had given me in Gower Street, and found my Aunt Susan waiting for him to arrive for tea.

The moment I walked into the room, I saw how much Tono-Bungay had already changed their lives—it was just as obvious as my uncle's new hat. The furniture in the room looked almost majestic to me. The chairs and sofa were covered in a floral chintz fabric that gave the place a faint, distant feeling of the grand Bladesover house. The mantelpiece, the ceiling moldings, and the gas chandelier were all much larger and finer than anything I was used to in London. Furthermore, I was shown in by a real housemaid wearing a cap with trailing ribbons and a mass of reddish hair.

My aunt looked bright and pretty, wearing a blue-patterned tea-gown with bows that seemed to me the height of fashion. She was sitting in a chair by the open window with a large stack of yellow-labeled library books on the table next to her. In front of the large, paper-decorated fireplace stood a three-tiered cake stand filled with assorted cakes, and a tray holding all the tea supplies (except the teapot) sat on the large center table. The carpet was thick, and several dyed sheepskin rugs added a touch of adventure to the room.

"Hel-lo!" my aunt said as I appeared. "It's George!"

"Shall I serve the tea now, Ma'am?" the real housemaid asked, watching our greeting coldly.

"Not until Mr. Ponderevo arrives, Meggie," my aunt said. The moment the housemaid turned her back, my aunt made an incredibly fast, venomous grimace at her.

"She calls herself Meggie," my aunt said when the door closed, giving me the impression they didn't get along.

"You're looking very cheerful, Aunt," I said.

"What do you think of this crazy business he's got going?" she asked.

"It looks promising," I said.

"I assume there actually is a business somewhere?"

"Haven't you seen it?"

"I'm afraid I'd complain about it, George, if I did. So he won't let me go. It all happened so suddenly. He was brooding and writing letters and sizzling inside—like a chestnut about to pop in the fire. Then he came home one day shouting 'Tono-Bungay' until I thought he'd completely lost his mind, and singing—what was it?"

"'I'm afloat, I'm afloat,'" I guessed.

"Exactly. So you've heard him. He said our fortunes were made. He took me out to the Holborn Restaurant, George—for dinner, and we had champagne. You know, the stuff that shoots up the back of your nose and makes you gasp. He finally said he'd achieved things worthy of me—and we moved here the next day. It's a posh house, George. Three pounds a week for the rooms. And he says the business can easily afford it."

She looked at me doubtfully.

"He'll either afford it, or go bankrupt," I said profoundly.

We communicated silently with our eyes for a moment. My aunt patted the pile of library books.

"I've been reading so much, George. You wouldn't believe it!"

"But what do you think of the business?" I asked.

"Well, people have lent him money for it," she said, raising her eyebrows thoughtfully.

"It's been a crazy time," she went on. "All the flapping about! I just sit here doing nothing, and he's rocketing around. He's done wonders. But he wants you, George—he really wants you. Sometimes he's full of hope—talking about when we'll buy a carriage and join high society—he makes it sound so natural, yet so upside-down, that I hardly know if my heels are in the air listening to him, and my head is on the floor.... But then he gets depressed. He says he needs someone to restrain him. He says he can make a big splash, but he can't keep the momentum going. He says if you don't join him, everything will fall apart. But you are joining him, aren't you?"

She stopped and looked at me.

"Well—"

"Don't tell me you aren't joining!"

"But look, Aunt," I said. "Do you fully understand what it is?... It's a quack medicine. It's garbage."

"I don't know of any law against selling quack medicine," my aunt said. She thought for a minute and became unusually serious. "It's our only chance, George," she said. "If it fails..."

Suddenly, a door slammed, and a loud bellowing came from the next room through the folding doors. "Here—a sheer—hulk—lies—poo-oor Tom Bow—owling."

"Silly old accordion! Listen to him, George!" She raised her voice. "Don't sing that, you old walrus! Sing 'I'm afloat!'"

One side of the folding doors opened, and my uncle appeared.

"Hello, George! Finally made it? Got some teacake, Susan?"

He turned to me abruptly. "Thought it over, George?"

"Yes," I said.

"Are you in?"

I paused for one last second, and nodded yes.

"Ah!" he cried. "Why couldn't you have said that a week ago?"

"I had false ideas about how the world works," I said. "Oh, they don't matter now! Yes, I'll join you. I'll take my chances with you. I won't hesitate again."

And I didn't. I stuck to that resolution for seven long years.
