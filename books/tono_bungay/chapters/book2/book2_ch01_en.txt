# Chapter 1: How I Became a London Student and Went Astray

I

I came to live in London, as I will tell you, when I was nearly twenty-two years old., Here, 'Wimblehurst' refers to the small English country village where I used to live., Wimblehurst dwindles in my memory, and in this book, it is now just a distant, small place. Even 'Bladesover', the grand aristocratic country house situated among the distant hills of Kent, seems like nothing more than a faint pinkish dot. The landscape before me broadened out, becoming countless and limitless, filled with a sense of massive movement that had nothing to do with me. I do not remember my second arrival in London as clearly as my first. My early impressions consist mainly of a distinct October memory of soft amber autumn sunshine falling on the grey front of some house I cannot place. Along with that memory, there remains a feeling of immense tranquility.

I think I could fill an entire book with a somewhat imaginative account of how I came to understand London, and how it grew in my mind—first from one perspective, and then from another. Every day, my accumulating impressions were added to, modified, and connected with new ones. They became inseparably fused with other impressions that were purely personal and accidental. I found myself possessing a kind of comprehensive perception of London. It is a whole that is truly complete, yet incurably hazy in certain places; regardless, it is a single entity that began with my first visit and is still maturing and growing richer.

London!

At first, London was undoubtedly a chaos of streets, people, buildings, and folks coming and going without any apparent reason. I do not remember ever trying steadily to understand it, or exploring it for any purpose other than my own personal, adventurous intentions. Over time, however, a kind of theory about London began to form inside me. I believe I can see the outlines of an orderly structure from which it has grown, discovering a process that is more than just a confusion of accidental events—even if it might merely be the process of a spreading disease.

At the beginning of my first book, I mentioned that Bladesover, the grand aristocratic mansion, is the clue to understanding all of England. Well, I certainly imagine it is also the clue to understanding the structure of London. In England, there have been no revolutions or deliberate shifts or abandonments of opinion since around 1688, the era of the fine gentry, the landowning class below the nobility, and the time when Bladesover was built. Of course, there have been changes, like a dying forest being replaced by a new one; but it was during that time that the broad foundations of the English system firmly took root. And whenever I wandered around certain areas of London, the thought constantly occurred to me: "This is Bladesover House; this corresponds to Bladesover House." The fine gentry class may be gone. No, I think they are indeed mostly gone, likely replaced by wealthy merchants, financial adventurers, or others. But that does not matter, because the shape still takes on the structure of Bladesover.

I am most reminded of Bladesover and Eastry, the aristocratic village near Bladesover, when I look at all the regions around the parks of the West End, London's wealthy western commercial and residential district,. For example, the private parks of large estates are each somewhat related to a palace or a group of great houses. The roads and back alleys of Mayfair, London's most exclusive wealthy neighborhood, and everything around St. James's, the historical center of the British royal family, had the very spirit and architectural texture of the corridors and courtyards of Bladesover House, even if they were built later. They had the same smells, the same spaces, and on the largest and cleanest thoroughfares, you could always meet people who looked like unmistakable Olympians, a metaphor for aristocrats holding power, and even more unmistakable valets, butlers, and footmen in plain clothes. Sometimes, looking down into the basement areas of London houses, there were moments when I seemed to catch a glimpse of the white paneled walls and chintz, cotton fabric printed with floral patterns, from my mother’s room again.

Now, looking at a map, I can trace what I would call the 'Great-House' region. It passes southwestward into Belgravia, a wealthy neighborhood, then diffuses and spreads out sporadically to the west, finding its last systematic expression around Regent's Park. The Duke of Devonshire's mansion in Piccadilly, parading its insolent ugliness, pleases me particularly because it is the very essence of this phenomenon. Apsley House, once the home of the Duke of Wellington, fits perfectly into the manner of my theory. Park Lane, an exclusive residential street, has its typical great mansions, and they run along the borders of Green Park and St. James's Park. And one day on Cromwell Road, looking over the Natural History Museum, I suddenly realized a truth. "By Jove," I said, "this is just the small display cases of stuffed birds and animals on the Bladesover staircase, grown enormous. The Art Museum over there corresponds to the antiques and porcelain of Bladesover; and in the little observatories on Exhibition Road is old Sir Cuthbert's Gregorian telescope, which I had hunted out of the storeroom and put together, only grown much larger." Inspired by this thought, I dove into the Art Museum, found a small reading room, and, just as I had guessed, discovered old brown books!

What I did that day was a truly excellent piece of comparative social anatomy. All these museums and libraries dotted between Piccadilly and West Kensington, and indeed the entire global museum and library movement, actually sprang from the elegant leisure of gentlemen of taste. Their great mansions were the first libraries, the first houses of culture. By raiding the drawing rooms of Bladesover House like a rat, I had become, so to speak, the last shrunken representative of a man of letters like Jonathan Swift. But now, these cultural inheritances have completely escaped the Great Houses and taken on a strange, independent life of their own.

This idea that the social system escaped from the old 17th-century system of Bladesover, multiplying and overgrowing from the grand estates, still seems to me the best explanation not only for London, but for all of England today. England is a country of great Renaissance landed aristocracy that has unconsciously outgrown itself and become overgrown. During my early days in London, the high-end shops suitable for Bladesover's patrons could still be found on Regent Street and Bond Street. In those days, they had only been lightly touched by the profaning hands of Americans. The same was true in Piccadilly. Up and down Harley Street, I found the houses of country doctors or small-town physicians multiplied endlessly but otherwise unchanged. Further east, in houses abandoned by previous generations of aristocrats, I saw family solicitors, lawyers exclusively serving a family, settled by the hundreds. And down in Westminster, behind classic Palladian facades, public offices sheltered in large, Bladesover-like rooms looking out over St. James's Park. The Parliament Houses—the assembly of lords and gentlemen that was horrified a hundred years ago when merchants and brewers pushed their way in—stood out upon its terrace, gathering the whole system together into one head.

And the more I compared these things with my Bladesover-Eastry model, the clearer it became that the balance was no longer the same, and the presence of huge, new forces of blind invasion and growth became increasingly evident. The railway terminals on the north side of London were kept far away, just as Eastry had kept the train station far from Wimblehurst; they stopped at the very outskirts of the grand estates. But from the south, the South Eastern Railway rammed its great, stupid, rusty iron head—Charing Cross Station—straight across the river between Somerset House and Whitehall. This was the massive head that came crashing down in 1905. The southern area had no large protective estates. Factory chimneys blew smoke toward Westminster with a careless air of not having permission. To me, industrial London, all of London east of Temple Bar, and the huge, dingy immensity of the London port felt disproportionately large, abnormally expanded without plan or intention, and dark and ominous compared to the clean, clear social reassurance of the West End. And all around the southern, southeastern, southwestern, far western, northwestern, and northern hills of central London, there are similar disproportionate growths. There are endless streets of ordinary houses and ordinary industries, shabby families, second-rate shops, and inexplicable people who, to use a once-fashionable phrase, simply "do not exist." All these sights have often suggested to my mind, and still do today, the uncontrolled, abundant substance of some tumor-like growth process. This process literally bursts through all the outlines of the infected carcass, protruding into massive lumps like the vulgar but comfortable Croydon or the tragically impoverished West Ham. To this day, I ask myself: will these massive lumps ever become structured? Will they truly take on any new shape? Or is that cancerous image their true and ultimate diagnosis?...

Moreover, alongside this hypertrophy, there was an influx of foreign elements that had never understood, and never will understand, the great tradition—wedges of foreign settlements embedded right in the heart of this fermenting English expansion. I remember wandering eastward one day out of pure curiosity during my early student days, and discovering a shabbily bright foreign quarter. There were shops displaying Hebrew signs and bizarre, unfamiliar goods, and a crowd of bright-eyed, hook-nosed people chattering in some incomprehensible language between the shops and the pushcarts. Soon after, I became quite familiar with the indirect, wicked, and dirtily pleasant eroticism of Soho, a bustling commercial and entertainment district in London,. I found that those crowded streets offered immense relief from the dull, grey exterior of Brompton, where I lived my daily life. It was in Soho that I got my first faint inkling of the element of "replacement," which is so important in both the English and American developmental processes.

Even in the West End, around Mayfair and the squares near Pall Mall, Ewart would soon remind me that the outward face of old aristocratic dignity was much finer than its substance. Here were actors and actresses, moneylenders and Jews, and bold financial adventurers. I remembered my uncle's frayed cuffs as he pointed out various houses on Park Lane. That house belonged to so-and-so who monopolized the borax market, and that palace was owned by Barmentrude, a hero among modern adventurers who used to be an I.D.B., an illicit diamond buyer. It was a city of Bladesovers, the capital of a kingdom of Bladesovers. Yet all of this was severely shaken, and much of it was in complete decay, parasitically occupied and slyly replaced by alien, unsympathetic, and irresponsible elements. And with the ruling of this accidental and miscellaneous empire covering a quarter of this intricate earth, there followed complex laws, tangled social necessities, and unsettling, insatiable suggestions. This was the world into which I had arrived—a world where I had to somehow thrust myself and fit in my problems, my temptations, my efforts, my patriotic instincts, all my moral instincts, my physical appetites, my dreams, and my sanity.

London! I came up to it young, with no advisors, rather arrogant, somewhat dangerously open-minded, and very bright-eyed. Inside me, I possessed something fine—something I unblushingly claim as the common gift of imaginative youth—something finer than the world, seeking fine responses. I did not want to simply live, or just to live happily and comfortably. I wanted to serve, to do, and to create, with some degree of nobility. It was inside me. It is in the hearts of half the youth in the world.

II

I had come to London as a scholarship student. I had received the Vincent Bradley scholarship from the Pharmaceutical Society, but I gave it up when my studies in mathematics, physics, and chemistry at the Science and Art Department earned me one of the minor Technical Board Scholarships at the Consolidated Technical Schools in South Kensington. The latter was in mechanics and metallurgy, and I hesitated between the two. The Vincent Bradley scholarship offered £70 a year and was absolutely the best start a pharmaceutical chemist could have. On the other hand, the South Kensington scholarship was worth only about twenty-two shillings a week, and the prospects it opened up were vague. However, it meant engaging in far more scientific work than the former, and I was still gripped by the intense intellectual appetite that men of my type experience during adolescence. Furthermore, it seemed to lead toward engineering, which I imagined—and still imagine today—is where my particular usefulness lies. I accepted its greater uncertainty as a fair risk. I came up very eager, never doubting that the truly hard and steady diligence that had gotten me through Wimblehurst would continue in my new surroundings.

Only, from the very beginning, it didn't....

Looking back now at my Wimblehurst days, I am still sometimes surprised by the grueling amount of steady study and fierce self-discipline I maintained throughout my apprenticeship. In many ways, I think that time was the most honorable period of my life. I wish I could say with certainty that my motives for studying so hard were also grand and honorable. To some extent, they were. There was a fine, genuine curiosity, a desire for the strength and power of scientific knowledge, and a passion for intellectual training. But if Wimblehurst hadn't been so boring, restricted, and closely watched by others, I don't think those forces alone would have kept me studying so stubbornly and intensely. As soon as I entered the atmosphere of London, tasting freedom, irresponsibility, and the pull of entirely new forces, my discipline fell away from me like a discarded piece of clothing. To a young man in my position, Wimblehurst offered no temptations worth mentioning, no interests to conflict with my studies, and no vices. The only vices it offered were coarse drunkenness stripped of any imaginative appeal, or clumsy, sneaky, shameful lust. There wasn't even any social interaction to waste one's time on. On the other hand, being a conspicuously diligent student greatly boosted my self-esteem. People noted that I was "clever," and I played the part. In my own private calculations, my small accomplishments stood out brilliantly against the sunlit, petty ignorance of that pleasant town. I would cross the market square with busy intent, take my exercise with the dramatic sense of an ordered daily routine like an Oxford professor, and burn the midnight oil well past midnight, acutely aware of the occasional awe-struck passerby in the dark. And I stood out magnificently in the local newspaper with my unmatched annual harvest of certificates. Thus, in those days, I was not only a genuinely enthusiastic student but also a bit of a prig and a poseur. And, as London made very clear, it was the latter trait that had kept the former going.

Moreover, Wimblehurst had given me no outlet in any other direction.

But I did not realize all of this when I came to London; I did not notice how the change in atmosphere immediately began to warp and scatter my energies. First of all, I became invisible. If I idled away a day, no one noticed except my fellow students, who clearly had no awe for me,. No one saw my midnight lamp, and no one pointed me out as an astonishing intellectual genius when I crossed the street. Next, I became insignificant. In Wimblehurst, I felt that I represented Science; no one there seemed to know as much as I did, or to know it so fully and completely. In London, I walked through vast ignorance, and it was clear that among my fellow students from the midlands and the north, I was poorly equipped and undertrained. Even with the utmost effort, I would only ever hold a secondary position among them. Finally, thirdly, my mind was distracted by a wealth of new interests. London took hold of me, and Science, which had once been my entire universe, shrank to the size of annoying little formulas compacted into a book. I arrived in London in late September, and it was a very different London from the huge, grey, overcast, smoke-stained wilderness of houses that had been my first impression. I had arrived via Victoria Station rather than Cannon Street, and the center of my life was now Exhibition Road, an area filled with museums and educational institutions,. Under clear autumn skies, London shone in pale amber and blue-grey; it was affectionately spacious and fine. It was a London of massive, handsome buildings, long vistas, and distances—a London of parks, labyrinthine tall museums, old trees, remote palaces, and artificial lakes. I rented a room nearby in a house on a little square in West Brompton.

And so London faced me a second time, making me completely forget for a while the grey, drizzling face of the city that had first looked down upon me. I settled in and went back and forth to my lectures and my laboratory. In the beginning, I worked hard. But only slowly did a curiosity awaken in me—a desire to know more about this huge urban region, a desire to find something beyond mere machinery that I could serve, some purpose other than just learning. Along with this grew a sense of loneliness, a desire for adventure and social interaction. I found myself spending my evenings poring over a map of London I had bought, instead of copying out my lecture notes. And on Sundays, I went on explorations, taking horse-drawn omnibuses east, west, north, and south, devoting myself to enlarging and broadening the...
It was a sense of great, swarming hinterlands of humanity with whom I had no dealings, and of whom I knew nothing....

The whole illimitable place teemed with suggestions of indefinite and sometimes outrageous possibilities, full of hidden but magnificent meanings.

It wasn’t simply that I received a vast impression of space, multitude, and opportunity. Intimate things were also suddenly dragged from neglected, veiled, and darkened corners into an acute, vivid perception. Close at hand in the large art museum, I came upon the beauty of nudity for the first time. Up until then, I had held it to be a shameful secret, but here it was flaunted and gloried in. I was made aware of beauty not only as something permissible, but as desirable and frequent—one of a thousand rich aspects of life I had hitherto unsuspected. One night, in a state of real rapture, I walked around the upper gallery of the Albert Hall, London's famous large concert venue, and listened to great music for the first time. I believe now that it was a performance of Beethoven’s Ninth Symphony, the "Choral" symphony....

My apprehension of spaces and places was reinforced by a quickened apprehension of people. A constant stream of individuals passed by me; eyes met and challenged mine before moving on. More and more, I wanted to stay. If I went eastward toward Piccadilly, the bustling commercial center of London, women who seemed softly splendid and alluring to my boyish inexperience murmured to me as they passed. Life was extraordinarily unveiled. Even the advertising hoardings on the street clamored strangely at one’s senses and curiosities. People bought pamphlets and papers full of strange and daring ideas that transcended one’s boldest thoughts. In the parks, one could hear men discussing the very existence of God, denying the rights of property, and debating a hundred topics that no one dared think about back in my quiet, conservative rural hometown of Wimblehurst. After the ordinary overcast days and dull mornings came the twilight, when London lit up and became a city of white, yellow, and red jewels of light. It was flooded with wonderful golden illumination and stupendous, unfathomable shadows. In that light, there were no longer any mean or shabby people—only a great mysterious movement of unaccountable beings....

I was constantly coming across the queerest new aspects of life. Late one Saturday night, I found myself as part of a great, slow-moving crowd between the blazing shops and flaring market barrows on Harrow Road. I got into a conversation with two bold-eyed girls, bought them boxes of chocolate, and made the acquaintance of their father, mother, and various younger siblings. I sat hilariously with all of them in a pub, buying and being bought drinks, and left them in the early morning hours at the door of their "home," never to see them again. Another time, on the outskirts of a Salvation Army meeting in one of the parks, I was accosted by an eager and serious young man wearing a silk hat. He argued against skepticism with me and invited me home for tea with his clean, cheerful family of siblings and friends. I spent the evening there singing hymns to the harmonium—which vaguely reminded me of my childhood hometown of Chatham—and secretly wishing that all the sisters were not so obviously engaged to be married....

Then, on the remote hill of this boundless city-world, I found my old school friend Ewart.

III

How well I remember that first morning—a bright Sunday morning in early October—when I dropped in on Ewart unannounced! I found my old schoolfellow in bed in a room over an oil-shop in a back street at the foot of Highgate Hill in North London. His landlady, a pleasant but slightly dirty young woman with soft brown eyes, brought down his message for me to come up, and up I went. The room presented itself as spacious and interesting in detail, shabby with a quite commendable shabbiness. I had an impression of brown walls, as they were papered with brown paper. There was a long shelf along one side of the room holding dusty plaster casts and a small, cheap articulated model of a horse. I saw a table, something made of grey wax partially covered with a cloth, and scattered drawings. There was a gas stove in one corner, and some enameled ware that had been used for cooking the night before. The oilcloth on the floor was streaked with a peculiar white dust. Ewart himself was not immediately visible. There was only a fourfold canvas screen at the end of the room, from which shouts of “Come on!” proceeded. Then his wiry black hair, very much rumpled, along with a staring red-brown eye and the stump of his nose, peeked around the edge of the screen about three feet from the ground. “It’s old Ponderevo!” he said, using my last name. “The Early bird! And he’s caught the worm! By Jove, it’s cold this morning! Come around here and sit on the bed!”

I walked around the screen, shook his hand firmly, and we surveyed one another.

He was lying on a small wooden fold-up bed. Its scanty covering was supplemented by an overcoat and an elderly but still cheerful pair of check trousers, and he was wearing wildly clashing pink and green pajamas. His neck seemed longer and stringier than it had been even in our school days, and he had grown a wiry black mustache on his upper lip. The rest of his ruddy, knobby face, his erratic hair, and his general hairy leanness hadn't seemed to have grown or matured at all to my eyes.

“By Jove!” he said. “You’ve gotten quite decent-looking, Ponderevo! What do you think of me?”

“You’re doing all right. What are you doing here?”

“Art, my son—sculpture! And incidentally—” He hesitated. “I ply a trade on the side. Will you hand me that pipe and those smoking things? Great! You can’t make coffee, can you? Well, try your hand at it. Push down this screen—no, fold it up, and we’ll go into the other room. I’ll stay in bed all the same. The fire is a gas stove. Yes. Don’t make it bang too loud as you light it—I can’t stand loud noises this morning. You don't smoke... Well, it does me good to see you again, Ponderevo. Tell me what you’re doing, and how you’re getting on.”

He directed me in the service of his simple hospitality, and presently I came back to his bed, sat down, and smiled at him. I smoked comfortably while he lay with his hands under his head, surveying me.

“How is the morning of your life, Ponderevo? By Jove, it must be nearly six years since we met! We've got mustaches now. We’ve put on a bit of weight, eh? What about you?”

I felt that a pipe was fitting for the occasion, and once it was lit, I gave him a favorable sketch of my career so far.

“Science! And you’ve worked that hard! While I’ve been puttering around doing odd jobs for stonemasons and others, trying to get into sculpture. I have a sort of feeling that the chisel is right for me. I began with painting, Ponderevo, but found I was color-blind—color-blind enough that I had to stop. I’ve drawn a bit and thought a lot—thought more particularly. I give myself three days a week as an art student, and the rest of the time I have a sort of trade that keeps me afloat. And we’re still at the beginning of things, young men just starting out. Do you remember the old times at Goudhurst, our doll’s-house island? The 'Retreat of the Ten Thousand Young Holmes and the Rabbits' game we used to play, based on Xenophon's history? It’s surprising, if you think about it, to realize we are still young. We used to talk about what we would be, and we used to talk about love! I suppose you know all about that now, Ponderevo?”

I finished speaking and hesitated, almost telling a vague, foolish lie. “No,” I said, a little ashamed of the truth. “Do you? I’ve been too busy.”

“I’m just beginning—just like we were back then. Things happen.”

He sucked on his pipe for a moment and stared at a plaster cast of a flayed hand hanging on the wall.

“The fact is, Ponderevo, I’m beginning to find life a most extraordinarily queer setup. The things that pull at you, and the things that don’t. The wants—this whole business of sex. It’s a net. There is no end to it, no way out of it, and no sense in it. There are times when women take possession of my thoughts, when my mind is like a painted ceiling at the grand Hampton Court Palace, with the pride of the flesh sprawling all over it. Why? ... And then again, sometimes when I actually have to interact with a woman, I am overwhelmed by a terror of tantalizing boredom—I flee, I hide, I will do anything to escape. You might have your scientific explanations; what is Nature and the universe up to in that regard?”

“I gather it's Nature's way of securing the continuity of the species.”

“But it doesn’t work like that,” said Ewart. “That’s just it! No. I have succumbed to dissipation down the hill there, in the red-light district toward Euston Road. And it was damned ugly and mean, and I hate myself for having done it. And the continuity of the species—Lord! ... And why does Nature make a man so infernally ready to drink alcohol? There’s no sense in that, anyway.” He sat up in bed to ask this question with greater earnestness. “And why has she given me a most violent desire to sculpt, and an equally violent desire to quit working the moment I begin? Let’s have some more coffee. I tell you, these things puzzle me, Ponderevo. They dishearten me. They keep me in bed.”

He had the air of having saved up these difficulties to discuss with me for some time. He sat with his chin almost touching his knees, sucking on his pipe.

“That’s what I mean,” he went on, “when I say life is striking me as extraordinarily queer. I don’t see my purpose, nor why I was invited into this world. And I don’t understand anything about the world outside, either. What do you make of it?”

“London,” I began. “It’s—so enormous!”

“Isn’t it? And it’s all for nothing. You find guys running grocery stores—why the devil, Ponderevo, do they run grocery stores? They all do it very carefully, very steadily, and very meanly. You find people running around doing the most remarkable things, being policemen or burglars, for example. They go about these businesses quite gravely and earnestly. But somehow, I can’t go about mine. Is there any sense in it at all—anywhere?”

“There must be sense in it,” I said. “We’re young.”

“We’re young—yes. But one has to ask. The grocer is a grocer, I suppose, because he sees that he fits in there. He feels that, on the whole, it amounts to a calling.... But the problem is, I don’t see where I fit in at all. Do you?”

“Where you fit in?”

“No, where you fit in.”

“Not exactly, not yet,” I said. “I want to do some good in the world—something—something effectual before I die. I have a sort of idea that my scientific work—I don’t know.”

“Yes,” he mused. “And I’ve got a sort of idea about my sculpture. But how it fits in and why—I have no idea at all.” He hugged his knees for a moment. “That’s what puzzles me to no end, Ponderevo.”

He became animated again. “If you look in that cupboard,” he said, “you will find an old, respectable-looking bread roll on a plate, a knife somewhere, and a small pot containing butter. Give them to me and I’ll make my breakfast. Then, if you don’t mind watching me splash around during my simple morning wash, I’ll get up. After that, we’ll go for a walk and talk more about this affair of life. And about Art and Literature, and anything else that crops up along the way.... Yes, that’s the butter pot. A cockroach got in it? Throw him out—damned interloper....”

And so, in the first five minutes of our talk, as I seem to recall it now, old Ewart struck the note that ran through our entire interaction that morning....

To me, it was a most memorable conversation because it opened up entirely new horizons of thought. I had been working rather closely and was out of touch with Ewart’s free, expressive way of living. He was pessimistic that day, and skeptical to the very root of things. He made me clearly feel—what I had not felt at all before—the general adventurousness of life, particularly life at the stage we had reached, as well as the absence of definite goals or any coordinated purpose in the lives going on all around us. He also made me realize how ready I was to accept commonplace assumptions. Just as I had always imagined that somewhere within the social hierarchy there was a Headmaster who would intervene if someone went too far, I had always held an implicit belief that somewhere in England there were people who understood what we, as a nation, were doing. That naive belief crumpled into his pit of doubt and vanished.

He brought out, sharply cut and certain, the immense sense of purposelessness in London that I was already vaguely feeling. We eventually found ourselves returning through the historic Highgate Cemetery and Waterlow Park in North London, and Ewart was still talking.

“Look at it down there,” he said, stopping and pointing to the great valley of London spreading far and wide. “It’s like a sea—and we swim in it. And at last we go under, and then we wash up here.” He swung his arms toward the long slopes around us, indicating the tombs and headstones stretching out in limitless rows.

“We’re young, Ponderevo, but sooner or later our whitened memories will wash up on one of these beaches, on some beach just like this. George Ponderevo, Fellow of the Royal Society. Sidney Ewart, Rest In Peace. Look at the rows of them!”

He paused. “Do you see that hand? The hand pointing upward on top of that blunted obelisk, I mean. Yes. Well, that’s what I do for a living—when I’m not thinking, drinking, prowling, making love, or pretending I’m trying to be a sculptor without either the money or the morals to hire a model. See? I carve those flaming hearts and those pensive guardian angels holding the palm of peace. I do them damned well, and damned cheap! I’m a victim of sweatshop labor, Ponderevo...”

That was how it went, anyway. I drank deeply of our conversation that day; we delved into theology and philosophy, and I had my first glimpse of socialism. I felt as though I had been locked in silence ever since he and I had parted ways. At the thought of socialism, Ewart’s mood changed for a time to a sort of energetic optimism. “After all, all this confounded vagueness might be altered. If you could just get men to work together...”

It was a good talk that rambled through the entire universe. I thought I was refreshing my mind, but in truth, it was a distraction. Even now, all sorts of ideas carry me back to the source: Waterlow Park and my newly reacquainted friend Ewart. South of us lay long garden slopes, white gravestones, and the wide expanse of London. Somewhere in that picture was an old, sun-warmed red wall, and a bright blaze of late-blooming, golden sunflowers and autumn Michaelmas daisies, set off by a drift of mottled, blood-red fallen leaves. That day, I felt as though I had suddenly lifted my head from dull, immediate concerns and looked at life as a whole.... But it played absolute havoc with catching up on my backlog of notes, to which I had vowed to dedicate the latter half of that day.

After that reunion, Ewart and I met frequently and talked a lot. In our subsequent encounters, his monologues were interrupted as I began to take my share of the conversation. He had stimulated my mind so greatly that I would lie awake at night thinking about him, and I would discourse and answer him in my head as I went about my day.
In the morning, I went to the College. I am by nature a man of action, and only secondarily a critic. His philosophical assertion regarding the immeasurable vagueness of life suited his innate laziness, but it provoked my impatient and energetic nature into active protest. “It’s all so pointless,” I said, “because people are lazy, and because this is an era in decline. But you are a socialist. Well then, let’s make it happen! And there’s a purpose. You are right there!”

Ewart instilled in me all my initial concepts of socialism. Before long, I became an enthusiastic socialist, while he became a passive dissenter to the practical application of the theories he had taught me. “We must join some organization,” I said. “We must take action.... We should stand at street corners and give speeches. People just don’t know.”

You must imagine me as a young man, dressed somewhat unfashionably but with intense earnestness, standing in his shabby studio and saying these things, perhaps even throwing in a few gestures. And you must picture Ewart with clay smeared on his face, likely wearing a flannel shirt and trousers, a pipe in his mouth, squatting philosophically at a table as he tinkered with a lump of clay that never went beyond a vague suggestion of a shape.

“I wonder why people don’t want to,” he said.

It took me a very long time to fully understand how deliberately and completely he detached himself from moral condemnation and responsibilities—the very responsibilities that played such a magnificent role in his conversations. At heart, his nature was that of an artistic appreciator. He could find interest and beauty even in the endless facets of things I considered evil, or at least unacceptable. As for the self-deceptive impulse and sustained, consistent self-devotion I possessed—which, though unstable, disconnected, and meaningless at the time—he offered a kind of admiration, but never any true empathy. Like many highly imaginative and talkative people, he was inherently secretive, and throughout our interactions, he subjected me to a series of little shocks of discovery.

The first of these shocks came when I realized he had absolutely no intention of doing anything practical to reform the worldly evils he so easily and skillfully exposed. The second was the sudden appearance of someone named “Milly”—I have forgotten her last name. One evening, I saw her in his room, simply wrapped in a blue garment, the rest of her clothes were behind a folding screen, smoking a cigarette, and sharing a bottle of “Canary Sack,” a very cheap and potent grocery-store wine from the Canary Islands that Ewart favored. “Hello!” Ewart said as I walked in. “This is Milly, you know. She’s modeling for me—she is a real model, I mean...., calm down, Ponderevo!, Would you like some wine?”

Milly was a woman of about thirty, with a broad and quite pretty face, a gentle disposition, a poor accent, and beautiful blonde hair that cascaded in waves, exuding a variety of irrepressible charms. Whenever Ewart spoke, she beamed brightly at him. Ewart was always sketching her hair and starting clay statues of her, but none were ever finished. I now know that she was a woman of the streets whom Ewart had met purely by chance, and she had fallen in love with him. However, my lack of experience at the time was too great for me to figure out her true identity, and Ewart offered no explanation. She came to him, he went to her, and they took country holidays together, where she certainly covered a substantial portion of the travel expenses. I now suspect he might have even accepted money from her. Odd old Ewart! It was a relationship so far removed from my orderly concept of honor, or from anything I could ever imagine a friend of mine doing, that I failed to properly see it even when it was happening right in front of me. But now, I can see it, and I think I understand it....

Before I fully grasped the haphazard way Ewart committed to his particular lifestyle, and as the broad, constructive ideas of socialism took hold of me, I tried to convince him to work alongside me as a socialist in some definitive way.

“We must join forces with other socialists,” I said.

“They have something.”

“Let’s go and observe some of them first.”

After much effort, we located the office of the Fabian Society, an early British socialist organization, hidden away in a basement at Clement's Inn, a historic center for law and commerce in London. There, we were interviewed by a rather intimidating secretary who stood with his legs apart in front of a fire, questioning us strictly and showing deep suspicion of our intentions. He advised us to attend the next open meeting at Clifford’s Inn, a historic building in central London, and provided us with the necessary details. We both managed to attend the event, where we listened to a rambling, abrasive paper on trusts, monopolies, and one of the most inconclusive discussions imaginable. Three-quarters of the speakers seemed driven by a joking compulsion to act conceited. It felt like an inside family joke, and as outsiders to that family, we did not enjoy it.... As we exited the narrow passageway leading from Clifford’s Inn to the Strand, a major bustling street in London, Ewart suddenly approached a wrinkled, bespectacled little man wearing a large felt hat and an oversized orange tie.

“How many members do you have in this Fabian Society of yours?” he asked.

The little man instantly became defensive.

“About seven hundred,” he said. “Maybe eight hundred.”

“Are they... are they like the people here?”

The little man let out a nervous yet self-satisfied laugh. “I suppose they are above average,” he said.

The little man vanished from sight, and we stepped out onto the Strand. Ewart twisted his arm into a strangely eloquent gesture that seemed to gather up the tall facades of the banks, commercial buildings, protruding clocks, towers of the Law Courts, billboards, and glowing signs, condensing this massive, invincible capitalist system into one giant social entity.

“These socialists have no sense of balance,” he said. “What can you possibly expect from them?”

IV

Ewart, the very embodiment of conversation, was undoubtedly the primary reason for my obvious failure to focus on my studies. The early, crude form of social theory known as democratic socialism gripped my intellect more and more powerfully. I debated with the man who shared my bench in the laboratory until we argued and stopped speaking to each other entirely. And also, I fell in love.

Anxiety regarding sex had been slowly creeping into my being like a rising tide throughout my days in Wimblehurst, and the stimulation of London was like the wind blowing in from the ocean, driving the waves in fast and high. Ewart played a part in that, too. My perception of beauty, form, and sound, my craving for adventure, and my desire for human interaction became increasingly focused, sharp, and clear on this central, dominant task of individual life: the need to find a mate.

I began to fall faintly in love with girls I passed on the street, women sitting across from me on trains, female classmates, ladies riding in carriages, lingerers on street corners, skillful waitresses in shops and teahouses, and even pictures of girls and women. During my rare visits to the theater, I was always elated, finding not only the actresses but also the audience members around me to be mysterious, attractive subjects of deep interest and desire. The feeling grew stronger and stronger that somewhere among these glancing, passing crowds, there was one person meant just for me. And despite all the hostile forces in the world, something deep in my bones kept insisting: “Stop! Look at this one! Think about her! Isn't she nice? This is what matters—this matters above all else! Stop! Why are you rushing by? She might be the one destined for you before anyone else.”

It is strange that I cannot remember how I first saw Marion. She would later become my wife—the woman I would make miserable, who would make me miserable, and who would pluck that magnificent, generalized possibility of love from my early youth and turn it into a personal conflict. I came to recognize her as one of the many interesting, attractive figures moving through my world, meeting my eyes, and passing by with a kind of watchful avoidance. I would encounter her as I walked through the Art Museum, which was my shortcut to the Brompton Road, or I would see her sitting in a corner of the Education Library, supposedly reading. But as I found out later, she never actually read. She simply went there to quietly eat her bread. At the time, she was a very plainly dressed girl with dark brown hair who moved with incredible grace. I remember her hair was tied low at the back of her neck, revealing the pretty shape of her head and harmonizing perfectly with the lovely lines of her ears and cheeks, as well as the earnest calmness of her mouth and brow.

She stood out distinctly among the other girls because they dressed better, wore bolder colors, and tried to surprise people with novelties like hats and ribbons. I have always hated the rustling of women's clothes, the jarring color contrasts, and the smart yet unnatural angles of their outfits. Her simple black dress gave her a certain purity....

However, I do remember one afternoon when I discovered the unique appeal her form held for me. Feeling restless with my work, I had finally left the laboratory and wandered into the Art Museum to stroll among the paintings. In an odd corner of the Sheepshanks gallery, I found her intensely focused, copying a painting hanging high on the wall. I had just come from the gallery of ancient statues, and my mind was fully alive with a newly awakened sense of line. There she stood, her face turned upward, her upper body leaning slightly forward from the hips—memorably graceful and thoroughly feminine.

After that, I actively tried to see her, felt special emotions when she was near, and started imagining various things about her. I no longer thought about generalized femininity or casual people here and there. I thought only of her.

A chance event brought us together. One Monday morning, I found myself in a rattling omnibus carriage heading west from Victoria Station in central London. I was returning from a Sunday spent in Wimblehurst, thanks to the unprecedented hospitality of Mr. Mantell. She was the only other passenger inside. When it came time to pay the fare, she realized she had left her purse at home and instantly became an extremely flustered, embarrassed, and panicked young woman.

Fortunately, I had some money on me.

She looked at me with startled, anxious brown eyes. With a somewhat ungracious attitude that seemed to stem from her shyness, she allowed me to pay the conductor for her. As she stood up to leave, she forced a casual demeanor and thanked me.

“Thank you very much,” she said in a sweet, soft voice. And then, a bit less gracefully, she added, “That was very kind of you.”

I think I made some polite noises in response. But at that moment, I had no desire to be critical. I was completely overwhelmed by her presence. As she walked past me, her arm reached over me, and the graceful slender lines of her body were so close. The words we exchanged didn't seem to matter much. I had a vague thought of getting off the carriage with her—but I didn't.

That encounter undoubtedly tormented me immensely. I lay awake at night replaying the event in my mind, imagining what the next stage of our relationship would be. It took the form of me getting my two pence back. I was in the Science Library, looking up something in the *Encyclopædia Britannica*, when she appeared beside me. She placed a thin, obviously prepared envelope on my open book, its bulging shape revealing the coins inside.

“You were so kind the other day,” she said. “I wouldn't have known what to do if it weren't for you, Mr.—”

I told her my name. “I knew you were a student here,” I said.

“Not exactly a student. I—”

“Well, anyway, I knew you came here often. And I am a student myself at the Consolidated Technical Schools.”

I poured out my life story and barrage of questions, drawing her into a conversation that took on a strange intimacy because we had to speak in low voices out of consideration for our fellow readers. I am sure the content of that conversation was incredibly cliché. In fact, my impression is that all our early conversations were unbelievably ordinary. We met several times in ways that were half-accidental, half-secretive, and entirely awkward overall. Mentally, I never managed to grasp her. I never formed a mental bond with her at all. I can see so clearly now that her conversation was shallow, pretentious, and evasive. Yet—even to this day—I do not remember it as being vulgar. It was very obvious that she wanted to exaggerate or hide her actual social status; she somewhat wanted to be thought of as an art school student and was slightly ashamed that she wasn't. I gathered that she came to the museum to “copy things,” and that this was related to some method of partially earning a living that I wasn't supposed to pry into. I told her vain things about myself that I felt might impress her, only to learn a very long time later that they made her think I was “conceited.” We talked about books, though she was very guarded and secretive about them, and she spoke a bit more freely about paintings. She “liked” paintings. From the very beginning, I realized that her mind was utterly ordinary, that she was the unconscious keeper of something that had captured my deepest instincts, and that she was the careless possessor of physical traits that embodied the hope of possibility and intoxicated my reason like strong wine. And I never felt the slightest bit angry about it. I felt I had to maintain this relationship, no matter how monotonous it was. I believed that before long, we would break through these irrelevant exterior things and reach the true love beneath.

In my dreams, I saw her freed from herself, appearing, so to speak, beautiful, worthy of worship, and glowing. And sometimes when we were together, when a silence fell between us simply because we had nothing to say, my eyes would feast on her. That silence felt like the lifting of a veil—the veil of her superficial self. I confess it was strange. It is truly bizarre how powerfully certain features of hers gripped me—such as the slightly round, dusky tone of her skin, a certain perfect dimensionality of her lips and brow, and the magnificent slope of her shoulders. To many people, she would not have been beautiful at all—these things belong to the realm of the unexplainable. She had obvious flaws in her figure and facial features, but they didn't matter in the slightest. Her skin was poor, but I don't think it would have mattered even if it looked completely unhealthy. I harbored an abnormally limited and intensely painful craving. I had an unbearable desire to kiss her lips.

V

This affair was immensely serious and all-consuming for me. I don't recall ever having the slightest thought of turning back during this early stage. It was clear that she viewed me with a much more critical eye than I did her; she obviously disliked my scholar-like untidiness and my lack of even the most basic sense of style. “Why do you wear collars like that?” she asked, sending me on a quest for gentlemanly neckties. I remember one day when she rather abruptly invited me to come to her home for tea the following Sunday and meet her mother...
Thinking of meeting her father, mother, and aunt, I immediately doubted whether wearing my best clothes would create the impression she wanted to make on her family. So, I postponed the encounter until the following Sunday to get my appearance in order. I had a morning coat made and bought a silk hat, and as a reward, I received the first glance of admiration she ever gave me. I wonder how many men of my age would act as preposterously as I did. As you can see, I was abandoning all my beliefs and conventions without anyone asking me to do so. I was immensely forgetting myself. And I felt a conscious shame throughout all of this. I did not breathe a single word of what was going on to Ewart, or to any living soul.

Her father, mother, and aunt struck me as the gloomiest of people. Her home in Walham Green in West London was notable for its black and amber tapestry carpets, curtains, and tablecloths, and mostly for the old, irrelevant books with faded gilt on their covers. The windows were blocking outside prying eyes with cheap lace curtains and an "art pot" placed on an unstable octagonal table. Several of Marion's Art School drawings, bearing the official certification marks of South Kensington Art School—a prestigious art education institution in London at the time—were framed and adorned the room. Above them was a black and gilt piano with a hymn-book placed on it. Draped mirrors were placed over all the mantels, and above the cupboard in the dining room where we drank tea hung a portrait of her father, which was terribly realistic, as such portraits usually are. I could not see a single trace of the beauty I found in her in either of her parents, yet surprisingly, she contrived to look like them both.

These people put on airs in a way that reminded me of the Three Great Women in my mother's room, but they did not have as much social knowledge, nor did they pull it off nearly as well. Also, I noticed that they were acting this way while keeping an eye on Marion. They explained their unusual invitation by saying they wanted to thank me for my kindness to their daughter regarding the bus fare. They pretended to be simple gentlefolk who slightly disliked the hustle and bustle of London, preferring a secluded and unpretentious quiet.

When Marion pulled out a white tablecloth from the cupboard drawer for tea, a card bearing the word "APARTMENTS" fell to the floor. I picked it up and handed it to her, but seeing her face quickly turn red, I realized I shouldn't have seen it. It seemed they had previously removed it from the window to welcome my arrival.

Her father once spoke in a grand and vague manner about having business appointments, but it was only a long time later that I realized he was a temporary supernumerary clerk at the Walham Green Gas Works and a useful errand boy at home. He was a large, slightly fat, and loose man with dry brown eyes magnified by thick, large-lensed spectacles. He wore an ill-fitting frock-coat and a paper collar, and he showed me, as his greatest treasure and interest, a large Bible that he had grangerised—a technique of elaborately decorating a book by adding related pictures and photographs to it. He also tended a garden in the small yard behind the house and had a small greenhouse where he grew tomatoes. "I wish I had some heat," he said. "Because you can do a lot with heat. But I guess you can't have everything you want in this world."

Both of her parents treated Marion very respectfully, which I thought was the most natural thing in the world. Marion's own manner changed as well, becoming more authoritative and attentive, and her shyness disappeared. I guessed that she had taken her own approach—draping the mirrors, bringing in a second-hand piano, and taming her parents.

Her mother must have been a beauty once. She had clear-cut features and Marion's hair, though without its luster, but she looked thin and filled with care. Her aunt, Miss Ramboat, was a large, abnormally shy person who strongly resembled her brother, and I cannot remember a single word she said during this meeting.

To begin with, there was a considerable amount of tension. Marion was terribly nervous, and everyone had to behave in a mysteriously unreal manner until I jumped into the conversation, becoming talkative and making the atmosphere relaxed and interesting. I told them about school, the boarding house where I stayed, my days in Wimblehurst, and my apprenticeship. "There is certainly a lot of talk about science these days," Mr. Ramboat said thoughtfully. "But sometimes I wonder what use it all is."

I was young enough to be drawn into what he called "a bit of a discussion," which Marion cut off before our voices could be unfairly raised. "Perhaps," she said, "there is a lot to say on both sides."

I remember Marion's mother asked me which church I attended, and I answered evasively. After tea, we played music and sang hymns. When this was proposed, I doubted whether I could sing well, but that was considered a trivial objection, and I found that sitting right next to the hair flowing down from Marion's forehead was a great compensation. I found her mother sitting in a horsehair armchair, watching us sentimentally. I went for a walk with Marion toward Putney Bridge, and after returning, we sang some more and had a dinner of cold bacon and pie, after which Mr. Ramboat and I smoked. During the walk, I remember her telling me the meaning of her sketching and copying work at the museum. A cousin of a friend she called Smithie had started an original business making what she called a "Persian Robe"—a plain wrap-style tea-gown, comfortable clothing worn by women at home while drinking tea, with an elaborately embroidered yoke, the fabric piece attached to the shoulders or chest,. Marion went there to work when they were busy. When she wasn't busy, she diligently used her eyes and notebook at the museum to invent new designs for yokes, and then she would return home and trace the captured shapes onto the base fabric. "I don't make much money," Marion said. "But it is interesting, and during busy times, we work all day. Of course, the female workers we work with are terribly common, so we don't talk to them much. And Smithie chatters enough for ten people."

I fully understood that those female workers were terribly common.

I do not remember that the household situation of the Walham Green family, the level of these people, or any light, influence, they threw on Marion detracted even the slightest bit from my firm resolve to make her mine at that time. I did not like them. But I accepted them as a part of this affair. Rather, I think on the whole, they made her stand out through an effect of contrast. She was noticeably controlling them, consciously superior to them.

I devoted more and more of my time to this passion that possessed me. I began to think primarily about ways to please Marion, acts of devotion, treats, lavish gifts for her, and appeals she could understand. At times, when it became indisputably clear that she was obviously foolish and ignorant, I told myself that her simple instincts were more valuable than all the education and intelligence in the world. And even to this day, I do not think I was completely wrong about her. Like a tongue flicking in and out of a snake's mouth, flickering and appearing and disappearing through her ignorance, commonness, and limitations, there was definitely something very fine, something simple yet noble about her...

One night, I had the privilege of meeting her and taking her home from an event held at the Birkbeck Institute, an adult education institution in London that provided evening classes for working people. We returned on the underground railway, riding first-class—the highest class available. We were entirely alone in the carriage, and for the first time, I gathered the courage to put my arm around her.

"You mustn't do this," she said weakly.

"I love you," I whispered suddenly, feeling my heart beat wildly, and I pulled her toward me, embracing all her beauty in my arms and kissing her cool and unresisting lips.

"You love me?" she said, pulling her body away from me. "Don't do this!" And as the train pulled into a station, she added: "You must not tell anyone.... I don't know.... You shouldn't have done this...."

Then, two people got in with us, and my courtship was interrupted for a time.

When we were left alone again as we walked toward Battersea, she decided to be offended. I parted from her unforgiven and in terrible distress.

When we met again, she told me I must never say "such" words again.

I had dreamed that kissing her lips would be the ultimate satisfaction. But it was in fact merely the beginning of desire. I told her my only ambition was to marry her.

"But," she said. "You are not in a position to do so. What is the use of talking like that?"

I stared intently at her. "I intend to do so," I said.

"You cannot," she replied. "It will take years."

"But I love you," I insisted.

I was standing not a yard away from the sweet lips I had kissed. I stood within arm's reach of the inanimate beauty I wanted to bring to life, but between us, I saw the yawning abyss of years, hard labor, waiting, disappointment, and immense uncertainty.

"I love you," I said. "Don't you love me?"

She stared at my face with serious, unresponsive eyes.

"I don't know," she said. "Of course, I 'like' you.... One has to be realistic..."

I still remember the frustration I felt at her inflexible reply. I should have realized then that my passion could not be the spark to bring her to life. But how could I have known? I had allowed myself to want her, and my imagination bestowed infinite possibilities upon her. I simply wanted her and wanted her, foolishly and instinctively....

"But," I said. "Love—!"

"You must think of reality," she replied. "I like going around with you. Can't we just stay the way we are right now?"

VI

Well, you will now begin to understand how I came to break down. I have spun out these excuses long enough. My studies gradually lost their vigor, my attitude worsened, and my punctuality declined. I fell further and further behind my classmates, who studied steadily. The moral energy I still had at my disposal was now being formed toward serving Marion rather than science.

I became terribly depraved, increasingly evading and slacking off. The hunched men from the North, the pale men with sharp, hardened minds, and the fiercely focused students who had breathed heavily as they studied eventually stopped viewing me as a fierce rival and began looking at me with moral contempt. Even a female student surpassed me on the grade list. Then, indeed, I considered it a matter of honor to show that I wasn't even pretending to try by openly disregarding every rule.

So one day, I found myself sitting in Kensington Gardens, overwhelmed by a feeling of considerable astonishment. This was the aftermath of a recent heated interview with the school Registrar, where I had revealed more emotion than reason. I was primarily astonished at the fact that I had fallen so far from the militant ideal of unyielding study that I had brought with me from Wimblehurst. To borrow the Registrar's expression, I had exposed myself as an "unmitigated rotter, a hopeless, pathetic loser,." My failure to score marks on the written exam was no different from the insufficient results of my practical evaluations.

"I want to ask you," the Registrar had said. "What do you think will become of you when your scholarship runs out?"

It was indeed an interesting question. 'What' in the world was going to become of me?

It was clear that there would be no position for me at the school, as I had once dared to hope. Truly, there seemed to be nothing in the world for me except an assistant position with meager pay at some systematic provincial science school or grammar school. I knew that doing such work without a degree or qualifications meant barely scraping by, with almost no leisure time to struggle for a better position. If only I had even 50 pounds, I could hold out in London, earn my Bachelor of Science, B.Sc., degree, and quadruple my chances! At this thought, a wave of bitterness toward my uncle washed over me again. After all, he still had some of my money, or at least he ought to have it. Why shouldn't I act within my rights and threaten to 'take legal proceedings'? I pondered that thought for a moment before returning to the Science Library and writing him a very long and occasionally scathing letter.

That letter sent to my uncle marked the absolute bottom of my failure. The surprising consequences that completely ended my days as a student will be told in the next chapter.

I say "my failure." Yet, there are times when I doubt whether that period was a failure at all. That happens when I become defensively critical of those demanding courses I didn't follow—the encyclopedic process of scientific exhaustion that distracted me. Even if my mind was feeding on forbidden food, knowledge, it did not stop being active. I did not learn what my professors and teaching assistants decided I should learn, but I learned many things. My mind learned to swing wide and move on its own.

In any case, those other friends who received high grades on college exams and were the professors' model students have not achieved incredibly remarkable results either. Some became professors themselves, and some became technical experts; but not a single one can show achievements like those I accomplished by following my own interests. I built a boat that runs across the water, striking it like a whip. Before I built it, no one had even dreamed of such a boat. And from the unexpected hiding places of nature, I surprisingly discovered three secrets that hold significance beyond mere technical discoveries. I have come closer to flying than anyone else. If I had possessed a talent for obeying those rather ordinary professors at the university who proposed to train my mind, would I have been able to achieve this much? If I had been 'trained' in research—which is a contradiction in terms—could I have done anything more than add something to the existing repository of small papers filled with dull conclusions, of which there are already too many? I see no reason to feign false modesty on this matter. Even by the standards of worldly success, I am by no means a failure compared to my peers. I became a Fellow of the Royal Society, F.R.S., the most prestigious scientific society in the UK, at the age of thirty-seven, and even if I am not vastly wealthy, I am as far from poverty as I am from the Spanish Inquisition. If I had trampled on my curiosity as it tried to reach out in various directions, locked my imagination in a box when it wanted to grow out of things, and worked according to this or that excellent method and instruction, where would I be now?

I might be entirely wrong in this thought. If I had blocked off all those diversions of energy, plugged up my curiosity about society with currently accepted rubbish, abandoned Ewart, and avoided Marion instead of chasing her while focusing, I might have become a far more competent person than I am now. But I do not believe that!

However, on that afternoon when I sat dejectedly in Kensington Gardens, reflecting on my first two years in London in light of the Registrar's appropriate questions, I certainly believed it completely and was filled with remorse.
