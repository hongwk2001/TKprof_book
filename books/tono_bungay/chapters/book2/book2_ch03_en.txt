CHAPTER 3

HOW WE MADE TONO-BUNGAY A MASSIVE SUCCESS

I

So I made peace with my uncle, and we jumped into this brilliant business of selling slightly harmful trash. We sold it for 1 shilling and 3.5 pence—about the cost of a cheap daily meal for ordinary people back then—and 2 shillings and 9 pence a bottle, including the government tax stamp. We made Tono-Bungay a massive success! It brought us wealth, influence, respect, and the trust of countless people. Everything my uncle promised me turned out to be true. In fact, his promises were an understatement. Tono-Bungay gave me enormous freedom and power that I could never have achieved through a lifetime of scientific research or passionate service to humanity.

It was my uncle's genius that made it happen. Without a doubt, he needed me. Admittedly, I was his indispensable right-hand man. But he was the brain that conceived it all. He wrote every advertising slogan, and he even sketched some of the ads himself. You must remember that this was before the famous British newspaper, The Times, got into business and started loudly selling outdated encyclopedias. At that time in London, his style of newspaper advertising was almost entirely new. It grabbed the reader's attention, pulled them in, and whispered, "Let me tell you something you really need to know." Suddenly, catchy phrases would jump out in capital letters. "Many people who think they are QUITE healthy are actually just barely getting by" was one of his early masterpieces. Other capitalized slogans included "YOU DON'T NEED MEDICINE OR MEDICAL TREATMENT" and "ALL YOU NEED IS A PROPER LIFESTYLE TO REGAIN YOUR VITALITY." There were also warnings to watch out for "heavily advertised fake medicines" recommended by pharmacists. He claimed that such garbage would only ruin your health. What people truly needed was a proper regimen—which meant Tono-Bungay!

I also remember that bright, short advertisement that usually appeared in the evening papers in the very early days. "VITALITY—Tono-Bungay. Like mountain air flowing through your veins." And there were three penetrating questions: "Are you bored with your business? Are you bored with your dinner? Are you bored with your wife?" These ads also came out when we were running our business from Gower Street in central London. We used both of these ads in our first campaign targeting south-central and western London. That was also when we produced our first poster: the "Health, Beauty, and Strength" poster. It was my uncle's design. By chance, I still have the very first sketch he drew for it. I have included that sketch here, along with a few others, to help readers understand the kind of mental brilliance that created these familiar decorations that once covered the streets of London., The second poster was created about eighteen months later. It became the foundation for the famous "Fog" poster, which featured London's infamous thick fog. The third poster was designed in preparation for a flu epidemic, but it was never actually published.,

These things were just secondary tasks in my department. I had to polish the sketches for the artist and arrange the printing and distribution. Later, my uncle had an unnecessarily huge argument with the advertising manager of the Daily Regulator newspaper over how large to print his brilliant ideas. After that, I also had to take over the job of negotiating newspaper advertisements.

At first, we discussed and planned product distribution together in the drawing room of the Gower Street house, with my aunt sometimes offering very sharp advice. Later, we moved to my uncle's cozy study in their first real home in Beckenham, southeast London. There, we would smoke increasingly expensive cigars, drink older and older whiskey, and work late into the night—sometimes until dawn.

I remember that we worked incredibly hard, and both my uncle and I had tremendous passion. It was a game—an absurd but incredibly fun game—and we kept score by counting the boxes of medicine bottles. People think that one brilliant idea is enough to make you rich, and that you can make a fortune without any hard work. But as every millionaire can prove, except for one or two lucky gamblers, that is just a dream. Even John D. Rockefeller, the founder of the giant American oil company Standard Oil, probably didn't work harder in his early days than we did. We worked late into the night, and we also worked all day. We made it a rule to drop into the factory completely unannounced. At first, we couldn't afford to hire responsible managers, so we did this to make sure everything was running smoothly. We also traveled all over London, pretending to be our own sales representatives and making all sorts of special deals.

But none of this was my main job. As soon as we could hire other staff, I stopped working outside the office. My uncle, however, found the work especially fascinating and continued doing it for years. "George, it feels good to see the boys standing behind the counter, just like I used to do," he explained. My specific, unique duty was to give Tono-Bungay real substance and put it into visible bottles. I had to translate my uncle's great imagination into countless boxes of strangely labeled bottles. I had to make sure they were shipped by train, road, and steamship exactly on time, heading toward their ultimate destination: the Great Stomach of the Public. By any modern standard, the business was, as my uncle would say, "absolutely genuine." We sold our product and made money, and we honestly spent that money on lies and exaggeration to sell even more product. Step by step, we spread our product all over the UK. First, we targeted the middle-class suburbs of London, then the outer suburbs, and then the counties surrounding London. After that, armed with new flyers and a more serious style of advertising, we expanded into Wales, which is a fantastic market for new patent medicines. From there, we moved into Lancashire in northwest England.

My uncle hung a massive map of England in the inner office. Whenever we started shipping to a new area and advertising in the local newspapers, he would mark our progress with little flags for ads and pink underlines for orders.

Rubbing his hands together and drawing breath through his teeth, my uncle would say, "This is the romance of modern business, George! The romance of modern business, right? Conquest. Taking over one province at a time. Just like soldiers."

We conquered England and Wales, and then we marched over the Cheviot Hills—on the border of Scotland—with a specially modified product containing 11 percent pure alcohol: "Tono-Bungay: Thistle Brand." We also adapted the "Fog" poster for this market, showing an Englishman wearing a traditional Scottish kilt against the misty Scottish highlands.

Under the shadow of our fantastic main product, we soon launched secondary specialty items. "Tono-Bungay Hair Stimulant" was our first addition. Then came "Concentrated Tono-Bungay" for the eyes. That one didn't sell well, but the hair stimulant was quite successful. I remember we introduced it with a short Q&A advertisement: "Why does hair fall out? Because the hair follicles are exhausted. What are hair follicles?..." The ad led up to the ultimate claim that the hair stimulant contained "the essential ingredients of Tono-Bungay, the most revitalizing tonic, along with a soft, nutrient-rich oil extracted from the hooves and horns of cattle through a process of refining, separating, and deodorizing." It claimed, "...Anyone with scientific knowledge will clearly see that neat's-foot oil, extracted from the hooves and horns of beasts, is a natural skin and hair lubricant."

We also achieved amazing results with our next supplementary products: "Tono-Bungay Lozenges" and "Tono-Bungay Chocolate." We heavily promoted their extraordinary nutritional and recovery value for fatigue and stress. We provided posters and illustrated ads showing mountain climbers hanging from vertical cliffs, champion cyclists racing on the track, messengers riding from Aix-la-Chapelle to Ghent, and soldiers lying low in battle under the hot sun. We declared, "You can GO for twenty-four hours on Tono-Bungay Chocolate!" But we didn't mention whether you could make the return trip on the same product. We also showed a very stereotypical lawyer—complete with a wig, sideburns, and buck teeth, looking frighteningly like every existing lawyer—giving a speech at a table. Below him was the caption: "A four-hour speech fueled by Tono-Bungay Lozenges, and he feels as fresh as when he started." After that, we brought in crowds of school teachers, revivalist ministers, and politicians. I honestly believe that the early formula for those lozenges contained a "kick" of strychnine stimulant. I say this because, as sales skyrocketed, we constantly and drastically weakened the formula.

Before long—or so it seems to me now—we were hiring traveling salesmen and opening up the UK at a rate of a hundred square miles a day. My uncle sketched out the entire organizational structure in a rough, tangled, half-inspired way. It was my job to turn all of that into a practical plan for quantities and expenses. We had a lot of trouble finding our salesmen. In the end, more than half of them were Irish-Americans, who had an incredible talent for selling medicine. We had even more trouble finding a factory manager because of the secrets hidden in the inner office. Eventually, we hired Mrs. Hampton Diggs, a very capable woman who had previously managed a large hat workshop. We could trust her to keep everything running smoothly without discovering anything that wasn't put right under her loyal, energetic nose. She had a very high opinion of Tono-Bungay and consumed it in all its forms and in large quantities for as long as I knew her. It didn't seem to do her any harm. And she managed the young female workers wonderfully.

My uncle's final addition to the Tono-Bungay product line was the Tono-Bungay Mouthwash. You have probably read his inspired question hundreds of times: "You are still young, but are you sure your gums haven't aged?"

After that, we took over the exclusive distribution rights for three or four American products that could be sold alongside our own. The most notable ones were 'Texan Embrocation' for muscle pain and '23,' which was claimed to cleanse toxins from the body.

I am simply recording these bare facts. For me, all of this is forever tied to the image of my uncle. In some of the old seventeenth or early eighteenth-century Anglican prayer books at the Bladesover estate, there were illustrations of woodcut figures with long scrolls pouring out of their mouths. I wish I could write this entire last chapter on a scroll pouring out of my uncle's head. I want to show you all these stories constantly unfolding and pouring out from a short, slightly plump, short-legged man with stiff, closely cropped hair, glasses perched precariously on his perky little nose, and eyes wide open behind them. I wish I could show you how he breathed heavily through his nose as he scribbled down bizarre and eccentric ideas for posters or pictorial pages with his pen. And I wish you could hear his voice, full of solemn meaning like a squeaky prophet, saying, 'George! Listen to me! I just had an idea. It's a brilliant thought! George!'

I would like to put myself in that same picture. The setting that would suit us best is probably the cozy study in Beckenham, a suburb in South London. That was where we worked the hardest. It would be a dimly lit room in the early 1890s, and the clock on the mantelpiece would be pointing to midnight or later. We would be sitting on either side of the fireplace, me smoking a pipe and my uncle smoking a cigar or cigarette. There would be glasses resting inside the brass fender. Our expressions would be incredibly serious. My uncle always used to lean deep into his armchair, and whenever he sat down, his toes pointed inward, making his legs look curved. They looked like the legs of a stuffed doll filled with sawdust, without any bones or joints.

'George, what do you think about using Tono-Bungay for seasickness?' my uncle would say.

'I don't think it would have any effect at all.'

'Hmm! It wouldn't hurt to try, George. All we can do is try.'

I puffed on my pipe. 'It wouldn't be easy to reach people. Unless we specifically sold the medicine at the docks. Maybe we could run a special ad in Cook's travel agency offices, or in the Bradshaw guide, the continental train timetable.'

'That would give people confidence, George.'

My uncle clicked his tongue, and the glowing red light from the coal fire reflected in his glasses.

'We can't just let our talents go to waste,' my uncle added.

I could never quite tell if my uncle considered Tono-Bungay a complete scam, or if he had convinced himself to believe in it to some extent by repeating his own claims. To me, his overall attitude seemed simply friendly and almost as tolerant as a parent's. I remember saying to him once, 'But you don't actually think this medicine will do even the slightest bit of good for a single person, do you?' In response, his face took on a defensive look, as if he were scolding me for being harsh and dogmatic.

'You have a cold personality, George,' my uncle said. 'You're too quick to tear things down. Who knows? How can anyone dare to say for sure?…'

Perhaps back then, any game that involved creating and developing something would have caught my interest. At the very least, I poured just as much passion into this Tono-Bungay business as a young naval officer suddenly handed command of a ship. It was fascinating for me to calculate the benefits of shortening a certain process and weigh that against the capital cost required to make the change. I invented a kind of machine for applying labels and patented it, and even now, I still earn a nice little bit of royalties from it. I also figured out a way to turn our medicine mixture into a concentrate. I set it up so that bottles sliding down a ramp with safety guards would be almost entirely filled with distilled water from the first tap, and then the magical ingredients would drip in from the next tap. This saved an enormous amount of space in our innermost, secret work area. We needed a special tap to fill the bottles, which I also invented and patented.

We had a sort of endless track, like a conveyor belt, that slid down a sloped glass trough, and running water in the trough helped the bottles slide easily. At one end, a female employee held the bottles up to the light, removed any defective ones, and placed the rest in the trough. The filling process was automatic, and at the other end, another employee inserted corks and tapped them in with a small wooden mallet. Each tank—a small one for the energizing ingredients and a large one for the distilled water—had a water level indicator. Inside the tanks, I installed a float device that would automatically stop the slide whenever the water level got too low. Another employee stood by with my machine, labeled the corked bottles, and handed them over to three packers. The packers wrapped the bottles in outer packaging, placed corrugated cardboard padding between pairs of bottles, and dropped them into a small groove that slid them neatly into their designated spots inside a standard packing box. It might sound absurd, but I believe I was the first person in central London to pack patent medicines through the side of a packing box, discovering that this was a better method than packing from the top. In a way, our boxes practically packed themselves. All we had to do was place the bottles in their spots on a small wheeled tray, and when the box was full, we pulled it to the elevator and sent it down to the workers below. Those workers would fill the empty spaces with padding and nail the top and sides shut. Furthermore, our female employees used corrugated cardboard and matchbox wood partitions for packaging. At the time, everywhere else was paying high wages to young men to pack items by stuffing straw through the top of the box, which caused a lot of broken bottles, generated a lot of waste, and was a chaotic method.

II

Looking back now, those vibrant years seem compressed into little more than a single year. It stretches from our reckless start on Farringdon Street, armed with barely a thousand pounds’ worth of goods and credit—obtained through a risky method not much different from snatching—to the days when my uncle confidently and proudly asked investors for the massive sum of 150,000 pounds. He did this on behalf of himself, my share, which was a one-tenth stake, and our silent partners: the drug wholesalers, the printing press, and the owners of a specific magazine and newspaper group. When the deposits started pouring in, I know those silent partners deeply regretted not taking a larger share and extending our credit period. My uncle clearly secured half the shares, including the one-tenth considered to be mine, to manage freely.

150,000 pounds! Just think about it! A valuation for the goodwill of a business built on selling bottles of plain water and a series of lies! Can you grasp the madness of a world that tolerates such a thing? Maybe you can't. To be sure, sometimes the familiar routines blinded me as well. If it hadn't been for my friend Ewart, I wouldn't have even guessed how utterly absurd this incredible stroke of luck really was. I probably would have gotten used to it and fallen completely into all these illusions, just as my uncle eventually did. My uncle was incredibly proud of the company going public. 'No one has offered investors this kind of value in over ten years,' he said. But Ewart, with his hairy hands and bony wrists exaggerating every gesture, took it upon himself to be the sole chorus for this whole situation echoing in my memory. Throughout this entire astonishing time, he made sure I was aware of the fundamental absurdity of what I was doing.

'It fits perfectly with the way the world works,' Ewart said. 'It's just a bit more extreme. You don't need to think you're walking some extraordinarily special path.'

I vividly remember one particular long speech he gave one day. It was right after Ewart had returned from Paris, where he had gone for the dubious purpose of 'sketching out a draft' for an upcoming American sculptor. The young sculptor had been commissioned to create an allegorical statue of Truth, clothed, of course, for his state capitol building and needed help. Ewart came back with his hair cut short on top in what they call a 'brosse' style, and his clothing completely changed to French fashion. As I recall, he was wearing a purplish-brown cycling suit that was overly baggy, beyond just being old—the only thing you could praise about the suit was that it clearly wasn't made for him in the first place. On top of that, he wore a voluminous black tie, a soft felt hat with a decadent vibe, and muttered a few eerie-sounding French curses. Seeing my startled look, he said, 'These clothes look pretty stupid, don't they? I don't even know why I bought them. They looked pretty good over there.'

He came down to our factory on Raggett Street in the London slums to discuss a well-intentioned project for a poster he was going to draw for us. And there, over the heads of the female employees bottling the medicine, he poured out a remarkable speech., I sincerely hope those stories were too difficult for the employees to understand.,

'Ponderevo, what I like about all this is the poetry in it…. That's exactly where we are superior to animals. No animal could ever run a factory like this. Think about it!... Sure, the beaver comes to mind. A beaver might, on a very rare occasion, put something in a bottle, but would it stick a label on it and sell it? I'll admit, the beaver is a dreaming fool, and so are its dams. But still, the beaver has a kind of protective shield, a sort of muddy practicality! Those things protect the beaver from annoyances. And this isn't just your poetry. It's the customer's poetry too. It's a poet responding to a poet—soul meeting soul. Health, strength, and beauty packed into a bottle—it's a magic potion! It's like a fairy tale….

'Think of the people who buy the bottles of nonsense you sell!, Ponderevo, I'm calling it nonsense right now as a compliment,' he added as an aside.,

'Think of the ordinary, low-level clerks, the exhausted women, and the people suffering from overwork.

"People overworked with a desire to do something, overworked with a desire to become something.... Actually, people have reached their limits.... Ponderevo, the true headache of life isn't that we exist. That is a common illusion. The real headache is that we want to exist while 'not actually existing.' That is exactly what this symbolizes, in the noblest sense! That hunger to feel truly alive, right down to our fingertips, even if just for once!...

Nobody wants to be the way they are or do what they do now. Nobody. You wouldn't want to supervise this bottling work either. And I don't want to wear these terrible clothes and be dragged around by you. Nobody wants to keep pasting labels on silly bottles for ridiculously low pay. That isn't existing! That is merely the foundation, a means to make a living. None of us want to be who we are now or do what we do now. Except purely as a basis for life. What do we want? You know. I know. It's just that no one confesses it. What we all want to become is something forever young and beautiful—like the young Jupiter from Roman mythology, becoming young gods chasing after nymphs who act shy but secretly welcome us, through eternal forests, Ponderevo." His voice grew loud, rough, and declamatory.

There was a slight, listening pause in the workshop around us.

"Let's go downstairs," I interrupted. "It's better to talk there."

"I prefer talking here," he answered.

He was about to continue, but fortunately, the ruthless face of Mrs. Hampton Diggs appeared in the aisle where the bottling machines were.

"Alright," he said. "Let's go."

Downstairs in the small private room, my uncle was resting to digest his lunch and did not look alert at all. His presence made Ewart return to the topic of modern commerce while smoking an excellent cigar my uncle had given him. He acted with the elaborate politeness an unknown man should rightfully show to a business tycoon.

"What I was pointing out to your nephew, sir," Ewart said, resting both elbows on the table, "was the poetic side of commerce. As you know, your nephew doesn't seem to see it at all."

My uncle nodded brightly. "That's exactly what I told him," he said with the cigar in his mouth.

"We are artists. You and I, sir, can speak as one artist to another, if you permit me. The thing that made it happen is advertising. Advertising has revolutionized trade and industry. Advertising will revolutionize the world. In the old days, merchants carried their goods around on their backs, but the new merchant creates value. He doesn't need to carry things around. He takes something that has no value, or no special value, and turns it into something valuable. He takes mustard that is exactly the same as anyone else's mustard, and he goes around shouting, singing, writing with chalk on walls, writing inside people's books, and pasting it everywhere: 'Smith's mustard is the best.' And behold, it becomes the best!"

"That's right," my uncle said with his chubby face, expressing a feeling of dreamy mysticism. "That's right!"

"It is just like an artist. He takes a block of white marble right before it goes into the lime kiln, carves and refines it, and makes a monument for himself and others. A monument the world will not willingly let be forgotten. Speaking of mustard, sir, a few days ago I went to Clapham Junction, a busy transit station in London, and the entire railway bank was covered with horseradish that had spread from a garden somewhere. You know what kind of plant horseradish is. It breeds and spreads wildly like wildfire. I stood at the end of the platform, looking at the plant and thinking. 'It's like fame,' I thought. 'Growing wild and thick in places it isn't wanted. Why don't the truly good things in life grow like horseradish?' I thought. Then my mind branched out in its own peculiar way and reached the thought that a tin of mustard costs one penny. I had bought some a few days ago to spread on my ham. The thought crossed my mind that it would be a tremendously good business to mix horseradish into mustard as an adulterant. I had this sort of idea that I could jump into business with that, get rich, and then return to my real profession of monumental art. But then I said to myself. 'But why mix impurities? I don't like the idea of adding impurities.'"

"That's a despicable thing to do," my uncle said, nodding his head. "It's bound to be found out!"

"And it's completely unnecessary, too! Why not make a mixture of three-quarters ground horseradish and one-quarter mustard, give it a plausible name, and sell it for twice the price of mustard. Do you see? I almost started that business right away, but something happened. The train I was supposed to catch arrived."

"That's a very excellent idea," my uncle said. He looked at me. "That really is a wonderful idea, George," he said.

"Take wood shavings as another example! You know Longfellow's poem, sir. The one that sounds like a Latin first-declension noun. What was it? 'People say, Marr is a creator!'"

My uncle nodded and muttered some fading quote in his throat.

"It's a very good poem, George," he said to me like an aside.

"Well, it's a poem about a carpenter, a romantic Victorian child, and some wood shavings. That child made incredible things out of wood shavings. You could do that too, sir. Grind them into powder. They could become anything. Soak them in salty meat broth, and it becomes Xylo-tobacco! Grind them into powder and add a bit of tar and a sharp turpentine smell. It becomes a natural wood bath additive for hot baths, a sure cure for a terrible flu! There are all sorts of patented grain foods out there, what the Americans call cereals. I believe I wouldn't be wrong, sir, to say that they are actually sawdust."

"No!" my uncle said, taking the cigar out of his mouth. "From what I found out, they are real grains. Spoiled grains.... I've looked into that part."

"See, that's exactly it!" Ewart said. "Let's say they are spoiled grains. That supports my argument just as well. Modern commerce, like sculpture, is no longer just the simple act of buying and selling. It is mercy and salvation. It is rescue work! It takes all sorts of fallen commodities by the hand and lifts them up. The miracle of Cana, turning water into wine, doesn't even compare. You, sir, are turning water into Tono-Bungay."

"Tono-Bungay is perfectly fine," my uncle said, suddenly turning serious. "We are not talking about Tono-Bungay right now."

"Your nephew, sir, is stubborn. He wants everything to head towards a predestined end. He is a Calvinist of commerce. If you give him a trash can full of something, he calls it garbage and walks past it on the other side. But you, sir, you are someone who would make even cinders respect themselves."

My uncle looked at him half in doubt for a moment. But there was a hint of admiration in his eyes.

"We could make them into a sort of sanitary brick," he pondered, looking at the end of his cigar.

"Or what about crumbly biscuits? Why not? You could advertise it like this. 'Why are birds so energetic? Because they digest their food perfectly! Why do they digest their food so perfectly? Because they have a gizzard! Why don't humans have a gizzard? Because you can buy Ponderevo's crumbly asphalt-crushing biscuits. This is much better!'"

He spat out the last words like a shout, waving his hairy hand in the air....

"He's a damn smart fellow," my uncle said after he left. "I know how to judge people. That fellow will make it. Even though he was drunk. But that just makes some people smarter. If he wants to draw that poster, let him do it. Tsk. His idea about the horseradish. There's something to that, George. I'll have to think about that...."

To tell you right away, my poster project ended up bearing no fruit at all in the end, even though Ewart poured an interesting week into the matter. He let his unfortunate cynical temperament overpower his reason. He drew a picture of two beavers with a strange resemblance to me and my uncle—the part resembling my uncle was certainly more than half decent. And those beavers were bottling Tono-Bungay in countless rows, along with the legendary phrase "Modern Commerce." One cheerful evening, he strongly recommended the drawing to me, saying it would "arouse curiosity," but it certainly wouldn't have sold a single box. Furthermore, he produced a very shocking study of my uncle, excessively and unnecessarily naked, which in my judgment looked surprisingly similar, showing off feats of enormous giant-like strength in front of an audience of debauched and ruined women. The caption below it, "Health, Beauty, Strength," provided the necessary point for his parody. He hung this picture in the studio above the oil shop, draping a piece of brown paper over it like a curtain to further emphasize its defamatory unpleasantness.
