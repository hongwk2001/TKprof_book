# Chapter 4: Marion

I

Looking back at the days when we built the massive Tono-Bungay pharmaceutical empire out of people’s hopes and credit for bottles, rent, and printing, my life seems divided into two parallel columns of unequal width. One is an ever-expanding, eventful, and diverse column that represents the business side of my life. The other is a narrow, increasingly dark column, occasionally pierced by a gleam of happiness, which represents my home life with Marion. Because, of course, I married Marion.

In truth, I didn’t marry her until a full year after the Tono-Bungay business was completely established, and even then, only after fierce conflicts and arguments. I was twenty-four at the time—an age that, in hindsight, feels barely removed from childhood. We were both exceptionally ignorant and simple-minded in certain respects; our temperaments were exact opposites, and we shared absolutely no common thoughts, in fact, I don't think we were even capable of doing so,. She was young and extremely conventional. She seemed to possess no original thoughts, only the collective mindset of her social class. I, on the other hand, was young, skeptical, adventurous, and passionate. The only two ties binding us together were the intense physical attraction her beauty held for me, and the fact that she thoroughly enjoyed how important she was in my mind. There is no doubt about my passion for her; in her, I had found the woman I desired. I remember the countless nights I stayed awake because of her, writhing and biting my wrists in a fever of longing! ...

I have already mentioned how I dressed up in a silk hat and a black coat to please her on a Sunday—braving the mockery of my fellow students—and how we came to be engaged. But that was only the beginning of our differences. For her, an engagement merely meant the start of a pleasant little secret, an excuse to exchange sweet words now and then, and perhaps share a kiss. It was something that could go on indefinitely without disrupting her daily routine of chatting while working at Smithie’s dressmaking shop. For me, however, an engagement was a solemn vow to achieve the ultimate intimacy of soul and body as quickly as we could possibly arrange it....

I don't know if readers will feel I am treating this strange, foolish romance and my failed marriage with too much solemnity. But to me, this issue extends far beyond our small, personal anecdote. I have reflected deeply on my life. Over the past few years, I have tried to extract at least a little wisdom from it. And I have thought especially hard about this particular chapter of my life. It shocks me to realize how blindly and ignorantly we tangled ourselves up with each other, without any guidance whatsoever. When an individual confronts society—this web of misunderstandings, false statements, and flawed, flimsy conventions—the strangest part of it all is how accidentally and blindly we are forced to meet. We were merely typical examples of a common fate. Love is not only the most critical event in an individual's life but also the most important concern for the community as a whole. Ultimately, the way the youth of this generation pair off determines the nation's destiny; all other state affairs are secondary to that. Yet, we leave it to blushing, stumbling young people to accidentally discover its meaning on their own, guided by nothing more than shocked expressions, sentimental nonsense, vulgar whispers, and hypocritical examples.

In the previous chapter, I tried to outline my own sexual development. No one was ever honest or decent with me about this subject. No person, and no book, ever came to me and clearly explained that this is how the world works, and these are the things you need to know. Everything came to me in vague, unclear, and confusing ways. Whatever I knew about the laws or conventions regarding this matter took the form of threats and prohibitions. Aside from the shameful, secretive conversations among my classmates at Goudhurst and Wimblehurst, I was never warned—not even about the most genuinely horrific dangers. My understanding was pieced together from instinct, romantic imagination, and a random jumble of suggestive fragments that happened to reach me. I had read widely and chaotically: the Gothic novel _Vathek_, the romantic poet Shelley, the radical thinker Tom Paine, the ancient historian Plutarch, the historian Carlyle, the evolutionary biologist Haeckel, the designer and poet William Morris, the Bible, the freethinking magazine _The Freethinker_, the socialist weekly _The Clarion_, and Grant Allen's feminist novel _The Woman Who Did_—just to list the ingredients that immediately come to mind. All sorts of ideas were scrambled inside me, without a single clear explanation. However, one thing I did perceive clearly was that the world viewed someone like Shelley, for example, as both heroic and beautiful. I came to believe that defying social conventions and surrendering magnificently to passion was exactly what one should do to earn the respect and affection of all decent people.

Marion’s mindset on the matter was equally irrational. Her upbringing was characterized not just by silence, but by active suppression. The overwhelming force of suggestion had molded her so intensely that a young girl's natural fastidiousness had warped into a complete perversion of instinct. When it came to this fundamental, essential aspect of life, she had one inseparable label for it: "horrid." Without that kind of upbringing, she might have simply been a shy lover; instead, she had become an impossible one. I assume the rest of her romantic ideals came partly from the fiction she checked out from the public library, and partly from the gossip in Smithie’s workroom. Through her novels, she had developed the idea that love was a state where the man worshipped and served, while the woman gracefully bestowed her favors. There was nothing "horrid" about love in any of the fiction she read. The man gave gifts, performed acts of service, and tried to be delightful in every way. The woman "went out" with him, smiled at him, allowed him to kiss her in polite secrecy, and if he happened to offend her, she withdrew her presence and refused to see him. Usually, she would do something "for his own good," like making him go to church, forcing him to quit smoking or gambling, or polishing up his appearance. The story always ended with a wedding, and after that, the interest simply evaporated.

That was the theme of Marion’s fiction, but I think the chatter at Smithie’s worktable altered those ideas slightly. At Smithie’s, it was generally agreed that having a "fellow", a boyfriend, was a prized possession; that being engaged to a fellow was better than not; and that fellows had to be closely guarded—because they could be misplaced, or even stolen. There had actually been a case of boyfriend-stealing at Smithie’s, accompanied by many tears.

I met Smithie before we were married, and afterward, she became a frequent guest at our house in Ealing. She was a thin, bright-eyed, hawk-nosed woman in her early thirties, with prominent front teeth, a high, eager voice, and a tendency to dress in aggressively smart outfits. Her hats were startling and varied, but always unnerving. She spoke in a rapid, nervous rush of words that was more boisterous than witty, frequently interrupted by little shrieks of "Oh, my dear!" and "You never did!" She was the first woman I ever met who wore perfume. Poor old Smithie! She was genuinely a harmless, kind-hearted soul, yet I despised her with all my heart! Using the profits from selling Persian robes, she supported her sister's family of three children, "helped" a worthless brother, and even extended her charity to the girls working under her. But none of that mattered to my youthful, narrow mind. It was one of the major minor irritations of my married life that Smithie’s whirlwind chatter seemed to hold far sway over Marion than anything I had to say. More than anything, I fiercely coveted the grip she had on Marion's inaccessible mind.

I gathered that in Smithie’s workroom, they always referred to me demurely as "A Certain Person." I was rumored to be terrifyingly "clever," and there were doubts—which were not entirely unfounded—about whether I had a sweet temper.

II

Well, these broad explanations will help the reader understand the agonizing times we endured together when I finally felt I was on equal footing with Marion, and clumsily tried to draw out through conversation the intellect and wonderful passion that I stubbornly and foolishly believed must exist inside her. I think she viewed me as the maddest of sane men. In Smithie’s workroom, being "clever" was probably considered the next worst thing to insanity—a word implying motives that were incomprehensible and unpredictable. She could be shocked by absolutely anything, she misunderstood everything, and her ultimate weapon was a sulky silence that knit her brows, ruined her mouth, and stripped her face of its beauty. "Well, if we can’t agree, I don’t see why you should go on talking," she would say. That remark always enraged me beyond measure. Or she would say, "I’m afraid I’m not clever enough to understand that."

Silly little people! I can see it all clearly now, but back then I was no older than she was, and all I could see was that Marion, for some inexplicable reason, simply refused to come alive.

We used to plan semi-secret walks on Sundays, only to part in silence, filled with unspeakable frustration over vague offenses. Poor Marion! The things I tried to explain to her—my burning ideas about religious doctrines, the equality principles of Socialism, and the philosophy of beauty—the mere words terrified her. They gave her a faint chill, as if she were approaching something improper, and filled her with the dread of an impossible intellectual challenge. Then, with an enormous effort, I would suppress myself for a while and keep up a conversation that made her happy—talking about Smithie’s brother, the new girl at the workroom, or the house we would soon live in. 

But on the subject of the house, we had a slight disagreement. I wanted to live somewhere with easy access to central London train stations like St. Paul’s or Cannon Street, while she had her mind firmly set on the suburban neighborhood of Ealing. You must understand, we didn’t just argue all the time. She liked it when I played the role of the affectionate lover. She enjoyed the experience of going out together; we went out for lunches, visited the exhibitions at Earl’s Court, the botanical gardens at Kew, and attended theaters and concerts. We didn’t go to concerts very often, though, because while Marion "liked" music, she didn’t like "too much of it," and the same applied to art shows. Furthermore, there was a kind of childish baby talk I had picked up from somewhere—I forget where exactly—that acted as a very powerful peacemaker between us.

For me, her worst offense was when she occasionally tried to dress in the "Smithie" style—a cheapened version of wealthy West Kensington fashion. She had absolutely no sense of her own natural beauty. She completely lacked an understanding of the physical beauty of the body, and she would ruin her elegant figure by wearing wide hat brims and excessive trimmings. Thank heavens! Her innate refinement, her natural shyness, and her extremely tight budget prevented her from fully plunging into the extravagant tastelessness of the true Smithie style. Poor, simple, beautiful, and kindly but limited Marion! Now that I am forty-five, I can look back at her with all my old admiration and a new sense of affection, completely free of my old bitterness and without a trace of passion. I can take her side against the equally foolish, fiercely energetic, sensual, and intellectually chaotic young man that I used to be. I was too much of a young beast for her to have married—a hound-like beast. When I was with her, it was my responsibility to understand and control the situation, yet I demanded companionship and intense passion.

As I’ve mentioned, we became engaged, broke it off, and then got back together. We went through a continuous cycle of these phases. We had absolutely no idea what was wrong with our relationship. Eventually, we became formally engaged. I had an incredible interview with her father, who was tremendously solemn and dropped his 'h's—a common trait of working-class London accents. He wanted to know about my family background and acted exasperatingly tolerant because my mother had been a servant. Afterward, her mother began showering me with kisses, and I bought a ring. However, I gathered that the quiet aunt did not approve, as she had doubts about my religious devotion. Whenever we drifted apart, we could stay away from each other for days, and initially, every separation brought a sense of relief. But then I would start wanting her again; a relentless longing would take over me. I would think about the curves of her arms and the soft, graceful lines of her body. I would lie awake or dream of a radiant Marion made of light and fire. In reality, it was Mother Nature pushing me toward women in her blind, inescapable way, but I believed it was the specific need for Marion that was tormenting me. So, in the end, I always went back to Marion, made peace, and generally compromised on or ignored whatever had driven us apart, increasingly begging her to marry me.

Over time, marrying her became an obsession. It tangled up my willpower and my pride; I told myself I refused to be defeated. I became emotionally hardened to the whole process. The truth is, my genuine passion for Marion had likely faded immensely long before we actually married, completely worn down by her sheer unresponsiveness. Once I felt secure about earning three hundred pounds a year, she demanded a delay—a twelve-month wait to "see how things would turn out." There were moments when she simply seemed like an irritating opponent standing in the way of a matter I needed to resolve. In addition, I started to become heavily distracted by the thrill and excitement of the Tono-Bungay fake medicine business taking off, the constant changes, and the busy traveling. I would forget about her for days at a time, only to suddenly desire her with an annoying intensity. Finally, one Saturday afternoon, after spending the whole morning brooding, I aggressively decided that these delays had to end.

I went over to her small house in the west London neighborhood of Walham Green and made Marion come with me to the park at Putney Common. When I arrived, Marion wasn’t home, so I had to wait anxiously and chat with her father. He explained he had just returned from his office and was enjoying his free time in the greenhouse.

"I’m going to ask your daughter to marry me!" I announced. "I think we’ve waited long enough."

"I don't approve of long engagements either," her father replied. "But Marion will do what she wants anyway. Have you seen this new powdered fertilizer?"

I went inside to speak to Mrs. Ramboat. "She’ll need time to pack her things," Mrs. Ramboat said.

Marion and I sat down together on a small bench under some trees at the top of Putney Hill, and I got straight to the point.

"Look here, Marion," I said, "are you going to marry me or not?"

She smiled at me. "Well," she said, "we’re engaged, aren’t we?"

"This can't go on forever. Will you marry me next week?"

She looked me directly in the face. "We can't," she replied.

"You promised to marry me once I was making three hundred pounds a year."

She remained silent for a moment. "Can't we just stay as we are for a little longer? We *could* get married on three hundred a year, but it would mean living in a very small house. Take Smithie's brother, for example. They manage on two hundred and fifty, but it's very tight. She says they live in a semi-detached house right on the road, with barely any garden. The wall between them and the neighbors is so thin they can hear everything. When her baby cries, the neighbors bang on the wall. And people lean against the front railings to talk... Can't we just wait? You're doing so well right now."

An overwhelming bitterness consumed me at the thought of the profound, beautiful act of love being ruined by the ugly necessity of money. I answered her with immense self-restraint.

"What if," I suggested, "we could afford a detached, double-fronted house—say, in Ealing—with a square lawn in the front, a garden in the back, and... a tiled bathroom?"

"That would cost at least sixty pounds a year in rent."

"Which means we would need an income of five hundred pounds a year... Well, you see, I told my uncle that was what I wanted, and I got it."

"Got what?"

"Five hundred pounds a year."

"Five hundred pounds!"

I let out a laugh that was heavily laced with bitterness.

"Yes," I said, "really! And *now* what do you think?"

"Yes," she said, her face slightly flushed. "But be sensible! Do you truly mean you got a raise of two hundred pounds a year all at once?"

"Enough to get married on—yes."

She studied me for a moment. "You did this to surprise me!" she exclaimed, and she began to laugh along with my laughter. She became absolutely radiant, and her radiance made me feel the same way.

"Yes," I agreed. "Yes." I was no longer laughing bitterly.

She clasped her hands together and looked deeply into my eyes.

She was so thrilled that I completely forgot the disgust I had felt just a minute earlier. I forgot the fact that she had effectively raised her asking price by two hundred pounds a year, and that I had simply bought her at that higher price.

"Come on!" I said, standing up. "Let's walk toward the sunset, my dear, and talk about everything. Do you know—this is a most beautiful world, an..."

It is an amazingly beautiful world. And when the sunset falls upon you, you become shining gold. No, not gold. You turn into golden glass... Into something better than glass or gold."...

And for that entire evening, I wooed her and made her happy. She asked me to repeat my words of assurance over and over, yet still doubted a little.

We furnished that double-fronted house from the attic, it was a house with an attic, to the cellar, and created a garden.

"Do you know Pampas Grass, an ornamental reed with silvery feather-like leaves,?" Marion said. "I like Pampas Grass... if there is space."

"Let us make sure to have Pampas Grass," I asserted. And whenever we wandered around the house in our imagination together, there were moments when my whole being cried out that I wanted to hold her in my arms right then. But I held back. Since I had already learned my lesson, I touched upon such aspects of life very lightly in our conversation. She promised to marry me within two months.
Shyly, and reluctantly, she set a date, and the next afternoon, amidst anger and wrath, we "called off the engagement" for the final time. We disagreed on procedural matters. I firmly refused to have a normal wedding involving a wedding cake, white favors, white ribbons or decorations signifying a wedding, and carriages. Suddenly, during a conversation with her and her mother, I realized that these things were implicitly demanded. I immediately spat out my opposition, and this time it was not a normal disagreement. It was a 'massive fight.' I cannot remember even a quarter of the words we poured out during that argument. I only remember her mother repeating in a gentle tone of admonition, "But George, dear George, we *must* have a cake to send home." I think we all repeated our own words. I remember echoing this refrain myself: "Marriage is too sacred and private an affair to make such a display." Her father came in and stood behind me against the wall, and her aunt appeared next to the cupboard, crossing her arms and looking back and forth from one speaker to the other. She looked like a highly satisfied prophetess. I failed to consider it then! How painful it must have been for Marion to watch people witness my rebellion.

"But George," her father said. "What kind of wedding do you exactly want? You wouldn't want to go to a place like that registry office, a government office handling simple marriage registrations, over there, would you?"

"That is exactly what I want to do. Marriage is such a personal matter that..."

"I don't think I would feel married," Mrs. Ramboat said.

"Look here, Marion," I said. "We are going to get married at the registry office. I do not believe in all these formalities and superstitions, and I will not surrender to them. I have agreed to all sorts of things just to please you."

"What did he agree to?" the father asked, but no one answered.

"I cannot marry at a registry office," Marion said, turning pale.

"Very well," I said. "I will not marry anywhere else."

"I cannot marry at a registry office."

"Very well." I stood up, pale and tense. I was surprised at myself, but on the other hand, I felt a strange exhilaration. "Then let us not marry at all."

She leaned forward over the table and stared blankly ahead. But soon, her half-turned face sitting at the dining table, her arm, and her long drooping shoulder began to torment me.

III

The next day, I committed an unprecedented act. I sent a telegram to my uncle saying, "I am not feeling well and will not come to work," and set off to find Ewart, the protagonist's artist friend, in Highgate. He was actually working on a bust of Millie, and seemed to welcome any interruption.

"Ewart, you foolish friend," I said. "Quit working and let's go chat all day. I feel awful. You have a kind of madness that I somehow relate to. Let's go to Staines and row a boat up to Windsor."

"A girl problem?" Ewart said, putting down his chisel.

"Yes."

That was all I told him about my situation.

"I don't have money," he added to clear up the ambiguity of my invitation.

At Staines, we bought a bottle of shandygaff, a beverage mixing beer and ginger beer, some food, and, at Ewart's suggestion, two Japanese parasols. We requested extra cushions at the boat house, anchored the boat in a shady spot on this side of Windsor, and spent an incredibly comforting day amidst conversation and meditation. I remember Ewart lying down with a cushion at the front, so only his heels, parasol, and the tips of his black hair were visible. Only his voice could be heard against the mirror-like surface of the water, reflecting the sparkling, smoothly flowing trees and bushes.

"It's not worth it." That was the gist of that voice. "You'd better find yourself a woman like Millie, symbolizing a simple relationship without overthinking, Ponderevo. Then you wouldn't be so upset."

"No," I said firmly. "That's not my way."

For a while, a wisp of smoke rose from Ewart, like smoke rising from an altar.

"Everything is a complete mess, but you are the only one who thinks it's not. No one knows where we are. Because, in fact, we are nowhere. Are women possessions, or are they fellow humans? Or some kind of exclusive goddesses? So clearly, they are fellow humans. Do you believe in the goddess?"

"No," I said. "That is not my thought."

"Then what is your thought?"

"Well."

As I hesitated, Ewart said, "Hmm."

"My thought is," I said, "to meet someone who will belong to me, and one person to whom I will belong with all my body and mind. No half-gods! I will wait until she comes. If she comes at all... We must meet when we are both young and pure."

"There is no such thing as a pure person or an impure person... Everything is mixed from the start."

This was such an obvious truth that I was left completely speechless.

"And if you belong to her and she belongs to you, Ponderevo... who takes the head, the initiative,?"

I gave no answer other than an impatient sigh of "Oh!"

We smoked in silence for a while...

"Have I ever told you about an amazing discovery I made, Ponderevo?" Ewart soon brought it up.

"No," I said. "What is it?"

"It's that there is no such thing as Mrs. Grundy, a fictional character who strictly insists on England's conventional morality and dignity,."

"No?"

"No! Practically none. I just thought about the whole issue. She is just an instrument, Ponderevo. She has been taking the blame. Grundy is a man. Grundy's true identity has been revealed. A rather skinny, sullen-tempered man. In his early middle age. He has bushy black sideburns and a worried look in his eyes. He has been behaving well so far, and that's what makes him anxious! It's his mood! Let's say, for example, there is a Grundy in a state of sexual panic. 'Please cover it up! People are gathering, gathering! It's too stimulating! Terrible things are happening!' He runs around, waving his long arms like a windmill. 'We must tear everyone apart!' He attempts absolute separation, trying to absolutely eliminate everything. He divides one side of the road for men and the other for women, and sets up an empty screen without a single poster in between. He sews up every boy and girl in a sack until they are 21, leaving only their heads, hands, and feet exposed. He bans music, and makes lower animals wear cotton clothes!"

"We should eliminate the sparrows entirely—ab-so-lutely."
I let out an abrupt laugh.
"Well, that represents one of Mr. Grundy's moods—the imaginary figure representing strict British puritanism. And because of it, Mrs. Grundy—who, Ponderevo, is heavily misunderstood and actually has a libertine streak at heart—finds herself thrown into an agonizingly painful flutter. An absolutely painful one! She is such a compliant creature. Whenever Mr. Grundy declares that things are scandalous, she becomes scandalized, turning flushed and breathless. She scurries about, attempting to hide her profound sense of guilt behind a facade of haughtiness....
"Meanwhile, Mr. Grundy is in a state of complete turmoil. Pointing and gesturing with his long, bony, knobby hands! 'They are still thinking about things—unwholesome things! It's dreadful. They pick these ideas up from books. I cannot fathom where they find them! I have to keep watch! There are people whispering over there! No one should be whispering!—There is something implicitly indecent in the very act of it! And then, the paintings! The things in the museum—too horrendous to even describe. Why can't we just have pure art—the kind where the human anatomy is entirely inaccurate but wholesome and nice—and why can't we have pure fiction and pure poetry, instead of all this garbage filled with suggestive allusions?... Hold on, excuse me! There's something suspicious happening behind that locked door! Through the keyhole! For the sake of public morality—yes, sir, as a pure and righteous man—I insist—I will look for myself—it won't corrupt me—I insist on looking as my duty—hm, yes—the keyhole!'"
He kicked his legs around in a wildly exaggerated manner, making me laugh again.
"That is just one of Grundy's moods, Ponderevo. It isn't Mrs. Grundy. That is one of the falsehoods we spread about women. They are far too simple. Simple! Women truly ARE simple! They simply adopt exactly what men tell them to."
Ewart paused to meditate for a moment. "Just exactly the way it's fed to them," he said, before resuming his imitation of Mr. Grundy's moods.
"Then you catch old Grundy in a completely different mood. Have you ever caught him snooping around, Ponderevo? Driven mad by the thought of mysterious, unknown, wicked, and delicious things. Things that are not respectable. Wow! The very things he is strictly forbidden to do!... Anyone familiar with such matters knows there is just as much mystery and delight in Grundy's forbidden sins as there is in eating a piece of ham. It is wonderfully pleasant if it's a bright morning, you feel healthy, you're hungry, and you're eating breakfast outdoors. It is horribly unappealing if you are feeling sick. But Grundy has covered all of it up, hidden it away, and thrown filthy shades and blankets over it until he has forgotten about it. Then it begins to fester in his mind. He engages in dreadful internal battles over his impure thoughts.... And then you see Grundy with burning ears, intensely curious about low whispers. A Grundy let off his leash, speaking in a hoarse whisper with shifty eyes and twitching movements—making everything seem obscene. Evolving obscenity out of a dense fog!
"Grundy commits sins. Oh, yes, he is a total hypocrite. He sneaks around corners and sins in the ugliest of ways. It is Grundy and his dark corners that turn vice into true vice! We artists—we don't have any vices.
"And after that, he becomes frantic with repentance. He suddenly wants to be cruel to fallen women and to decent, harmless sculptors of simple nudes—like myself—and then he spirals right back into his panic."
"I assume Mrs. Grundy is unaware that he sins," I noted.
"Unaware? I wouldn't be so sure.... But, bless her heart, she is a woman.... She's a woman. And then, at other times, you catch Grundy with a massive, greasy smile plastered all over his face—looking like a disaster in a butter churn. He believes he is being incredibly liberal and open-minded—this is Grundy in his anti-puritan moments, 'trying his hardest not to see the harm in it'—Grundy acting as the champion of innocent pleasure. He makes you physically sick with the amount of harm he is trying so hard not to see...
"And that, Ponderevo, is why everything is wrong. Grundy, damn him, is blocking the light, and we young people are left in the dark. His shifting moods infect us. We catch his sudden gusts of panic, his disease of snooping, his greasiness. We don't know what we are allowed to think or say. He does his foolish best to prevent us from reading, seeing, or discussing the single topic that we—quite naturally and rightfully—find supremely interesting. As a result, we don't have a proper adolescence; we just blindly stumble into the concept of sex. If you dare—dare to even look—he might tarnish your reputation forever! Young girls are terrified into silence by his meaningful sideburns and by that bleary, judging look in his eyes."
Suddenly, Ewart sat bolt upright, popping up like a Jack-in-the-box.
"He is everywhere around us, Ponderevo," he said with deep solemnity. "Sometimes... sometimes I think he is literally in our blood. In mine, at least."
He looked at me very earnestly, waiting for my opinion, with his pipe hanging from the corner of his mouth.
"You are the most distant relative he could possibly have," I replied.
I took a moment to reflect. "Look here, Ewart," I asked, "how would you choose to run things differently?"
He scrunched up his peculiar face, stared off into the distance, and made his pipe gurgle for a while as he thought deeply.
"I admit there are complications. We have all grown up under the terror of Grundy and his innocent, docile, yet formidable wife. I am not entirely sure to what extent these complications are actually a disease—a sort of bleaching effect from living under Grundy's shadow.... It is quite possible that I still have things to learn about women.... Humanity has eaten from the Tree of Knowledge. Our innocence is gone. You cannot have your cake and eat it too. We are stuck with this knowledge, so let's deal with it plainly and directly. I think I would begin by completely abolishing the concepts of decency and indecency...."
"Grundy would have a total fit!" I interjected.
"Grundy, Ponderevo, would require public cold showers—if the spectacle weren't too agonizing to watch—three times a day.... But mind you, I do not think I would let men and women roam around freely together. No. The ultimate reality behind the two sexes is sex itself. There is no point in pretending otherwise. It trails along everywhere—even in the most refined mixed company. It tugs at your ankles. The men start showing off and bickering—and so do the women. Or else, they just get bored. I suppose our ancestral males have been competing over our ancestral females ever since they were all just grubby little reptiles. You are not going to change that instinct, even in a thousand years.... You should never have mixed company, never—except perhaps situations with only one man or only one woman. How would that work?...
"Or perhaps only duets?...
"How would we even manage it? Maybe through some strict rule of etiquette."... He became portentously serious.
Then he reached out his long hand in a series of bizarre gestures.
"I seem to envision—I seem to envision a sort of City of Women, Ponderevo. Yes.... A walled enclosure—built with excellent stonemasonry—a city wall as high as the walls of Rome, surrounding a massive garden. Dozens of square miles of gardens, trees, fountains, pavilions, and lakes. Lawns where the women can play, avenues where they can gossip, and boats on the water.... Women enjoy that sort of thing. Any woman who has attended a good, eventful girls' boarding school lives on the memory of it for the rest of her life. It is one of the rather pathetic realities about women—that their school and college days are vastly superior to anything they experience afterward. And this garden city of women would have beautiful spaces for music, spaces for gorgeous dresses, and spaces for artistic work. Everything a woman could possibly want. Nurseries. Kindergartens. Schools. And no man would ever be allowed inside—except, perhaps, to do the heavy manual labor. The men would live in their own separate world where they could hunt, engineer, invent, mine, manufacture, sail ships, drink heavily, practice the arts, and fight—"
"Yes," I said, "but—"
He silenced me with a wave of his hand.
"I am getting to that. The women's homes, Ponderevo, will be built directly into the wall of their city. Each woman will have her own unique house and home, furnished exactly to her heart's desire and in her own personal style—complete with a little balcony on the outside of the wall. Built right into the wall—with a small balcony. Whenever she feels in the mood, she will step out onto it and look down. All around the outside of the city, there will be a broad road lined with benches and massive, shady trees. Men will stroll up and down that road whenever they feel the need for feminine company; for instance, when they want to talk about their souls, their characters, or any of those things that only women will tolerate listening to.... The women will lean over their balconies, look down at the men, smile, and converse with whomever they please. Every woman will have this; she will

she would have a little silken ladder she could let down if she chose—if she wanted to talk more intimately..."

"Men would still compete with each other."

"Perhaps there, yes. But they would have to obey the women's decisions."

I brought up one or two difficulties, and we played around with this idea for a while.

"Ewart," I said, "this is like Doll's Island.

"Suppose," I pondered, "a man who lost the competition laid siege to a balcony, preventing his rival from coming near?"

"Move him away," Ewart replied, "through a special regulation. Just like people move away organ-grinders who cause a nuisance. It wouldn't be difficult at all. And you could forbid it by making it against etiquette. A life without etiquette has no dignity. Besides, people follow etiquette much faster than they follow laws..."

"Hmm," I said, struck by an idea quite distant from a young man's world. "What about children?" I asked. "In that female-centric city? Girls are all fine. But boys, for instance—they grow up."

"Ah!" said Ewart. "Yes, I forgot about that. Boys shouldn't grow up inside the city.... When they turn seven, they must be sent out. The father must arrive with a little pony, a small gun, and men's clothes to take the boy away. Later on, he could visit his mother's balcony.... Having a mother must be a wonderful thing. The father and the son..."

"This is all very pretty in its own way," I finally said, "but it's just a dream. Let's return to reality. What I really want to know is, what are you going to do right now in a wealthy area like Brompton, or an ordinary neighborhood like Walham Green?"

"Oh, damn it!" he exclaimed. "Walham Green! What a guy you are, Ponderevo!" With that, he abruptly ended his speech. For a while, he wouldn't even respond to my attempts to keep the conversation going.

"While I was talking just now," he eventually spoke up,

"I had a completely different idea."

"What is it?"

"It's for a masterpiece. A series of sculptures. Like the busts of the Roman Caesars. But not heads, you know. These days, we don't actually see the faces of the people who affect our lives..."

"How will you make it, then?"

"Hands—a series of hands! The hands of the twentieth century. I'm going to do it. Someday, someone will discover them—they'll go there, see what I've done, and understand what it means."

"Where will they see it?"

"On the tombs. Why not? I will be the unknown master of the Highgate Slope cemetery! All the small, soft, feminine hands, the nervous and ugly hands of men, the hands of the failures, and the hands of the greedy snatchers! And then there's Grundy's loose, bony, knuckly hand—the hand of that terrifying moral authority! Complete with its little wrinkles and its thumb! But that one hand should be holding all the others together—gripping them in a slightly disturbing squeeze.... Just like Rodin's great Hand of God—you know the piece!"

IV

I forget exactly how many days passed between our final breakup and Marion's surrender. But I still remember the sharp rush of emotion, the concentrated feeling of tears and laughter in my throat when I read her unexpected letter: "I have thought everything over, and I was being selfish...." I rushed straight to Walham Green that evening to give back everything she had given me, determined to outdo her completely in giving. She was incredibly gentle and generous that night, and when I finally left, she gave me a very sweet kiss.

And so we got married.

We married amidst all the usual mismatched elements. I gave—and perhaps after a short while, not entirely unwillingly—and Marion took what I gave with obvious satisfaction. Ultimately, I was being reasonable. We rode to the church in three rented carriages, one pair of horses actually matched, driven by coachmen wearing very shabby top hats with white favors tied to their whips, giving an improvised feel. My uncle grandly intervened, insisting on having a wedding breakfast catered all the way from Hammersmith. The table was splendidly decorated with chrysanthemums, orange blossoms placed in significant spots, and a wonderful cake. We handed out more than twenty slices of the cake, accompanied by silver-printed cards showing Marion's maiden name, Ramboat, crossed out with an arrow and replaced with Ponderevo. A small group of Marion's relatives gathered, and several friends—and friends of friends—from her workplace at Smithie's showed up at the church and drifted toward the vestry. My side consisted of a select group of exactly two: my aunt and uncle. The sheer number of people created an exhilarating sense of congestion in that shabby little house. The sideboard, which usually held the tablecloth and the "Apartments Available" sign, was repurposed to display the wedding gifts, with the leftover silver-printed cards filling the empty spaces.

Marion wore a white bridal outfit—white silk and satin that didn't really suit her, making her look large and unfamiliar to me. She prominently displayed ribbons and strange contours. She went through the entire unfamiliar ritual of an English wedding with a solemn, sacramental gravity that I was far too young and self-absorbed to understand. To her, it was something profoundly central and important. To me, however, it felt like an offensive, complicated, and bewildering intrusion by a world I was already beginning to criticize very bitterly. What was all this fuss about? It felt like an indecent, public advertisement of the simple fact that I was passionately in love with Marion! I believe, though, that Marion was only faintly aware of my suppressed frustration at having to behave "nicely" in the end. I had played along to the point of dressing the part perfectly: I wore a beautifully tailored frock coat, a new top hat, the lightest-colored trousers I could tolerate, lighter than I preferred, actually, a white vest, a dark tie, and light-colored gloves. Noticing my gloom, Marion took the unusual initiative to whisper that I looked lovely. I knew all too well that I didn't look like myself. I felt like I belonged in a special color supplement of "Men's Wear" or "The Tailor and Cutter," posing for "Full Dress For Ceremonial Occasions." I even had to endure the uncomfortable sensation of an unfamiliar collar. I felt lost inside a strange body, and when I looked down at myself for reassurance, the straight white vest over my stomach and those alien legs only confirmed the feeling.

My uncle was my best man, looking for all the world like a little banker buried in flowers. He wore a white rose in his buttonhole. I don't think he was particularly talkative that day. At least, I remember very little of what he said.

"George," he said a couple of times, "this is a great occasion for you—a very great occasion." He sounded a bit doubtful as he spoke.

You see, I hadn't told him anything about Marion until about a week before the wedding. Both he and my aunt were taken completely by surprise. As people say, they just couldn't "make it out." My aunt was far more interested than my uncle. I think that was the very first time I truly saw how much she cared for me. I remember she pulled me aside right after I made the announcement. "Now, George," she demanded, "tell me everything about her. Why didn't you tell—at least ME—before?"

I was surprised by how hard it was to explain Marion to her. I only ended up confusing her.

"Well, is she beautiful?" she finally asked.

"I don't know what you'll think of her," I dodged. "I think—"

"Yes?"

"I think she might be the most beautiful person in the world."

"And isn't she? To you?"

"Of course," I nodded. "Yes. She is..."

While I can't recall anything specific my uncle said or did during the ceremony, I do remember certain small details very clearly—intense little moments of observation,

I saw anxiety in my aunt's eyes, a curious and rare flicker of intimacy. I realized I couldn't hide anything from her. She dressed very neatly, wearing a large feathered hat, a glamorous women's hat popular in the late Victorian era, that made her neck look longer and thinner than ever. As she walked down the church aisle with her characteristic waddle, staring only at Marion, the bride, in a panic that made her forget herself, somehow it didn't seem funny. I believe my aunt was thinking more about my marriage than I was, and was worried beyond words about my dark anger and Marion's ignorance, blindness to the situation,. She was searching for love with eyes that knew what love is.

In the vestry, the room in the church where the marriage vows are signed, when we were signing, my aunt turned her head. To this day I don't know why she had to cry, but I firmly believe she was crying. Also, when we parted, she squeezed my hand and was on the verge of tears. She didn't say a single word to me, didn't even look at me, just squeezed my hand tightly....

If my heart hadn't been so gloomy, I would have found much of my wedding quite amusing. In my memory, there are many ridiculous details that still don't seem funny to me. The officiating clergyman had a cold and made a nasal "d" sound instead of an "n", e.g., a lisp caused by a blocked nose, and when we signed the marriage register, he gave the most mechanical compliment imaginable about the bride's age. Everyone could tell that every bride he ever married must have received that same compliment. And two middle-aged spinsters stood out, Marion's cousins who worked as dressmakers in Barking, an outer area in East London,. They wore amazingly bright and colorful blouses with faded old skirts, and had immense respect for Mr. Ramboat, Marion's father,. They threw rice, a traditional custom of throwing rice at newlyweds wishing them fertility and prosperity,. They brought a whole sack and handed it out by the handful to unknown little boys at the church door, causing a Lilliputian riot, a chaotic situation where small children swarmed to get the rice,. And one of them intended to throw a slipper, an old English wedding custom for good luck,. It was a very warm, worn silk slipper, and I know this because she dropped it out of her pocket in the aisle, there was a bit of a tangle in the aisle, and I picked it up for her. I don't think she actually threw it. Because as we were leaving the church in the carriage, I saw her in a terrible and seemingly hopeless struggle with her pocket. And later, my eyes caught that missile of good luck, the slipper, or its mate, clearly misplaced and lying behind the umbrella stand in the hallway....

The whole affair was much more absurd, inconsistent, and more human than I had expected. But I was too young and serious to let the latter characteristic, being human, compensate for its earlier shortcomings. Because I am so far removed from this period of my youth, I can now look back at it all very coldly, as if looking at a photograph. It is like an inexhaustible, wonderful, perfect kind of photograph. But at the time, all of this filled me with unspeakable anger. Now I look around at all of it, look into the details, and generalize about its aspects. For example, I am interested in piecing it together with my 'Bladesover theory', a social system centered around traditional aristocratic mansions, of the British social structure. Under the pressure of tradition, in the boiling chaos of London, we were all trying to carry out the marriage rituals of a tenant farmer on the Bladesover estate or of chubby middle-class people in some dependent country town. In those places, the countryside, marriage is a public event with public meaning. There, the church is largely the gathering place of the community, and the fact that you are getting married is important to everyone you meet on the road. It is a very natural and legitimate change in status that attracts the attention of the whole neighborhood. But in London, there are no neighbors, nobody knows, and nobody cares. A complete stranger in a government office received my notice, and our wedding announcement was proclaimed to the ears of people who had never heard our names before. Even the clergyman who married us had never seen us before, and gave no hint of wanting to see us again. Neighbors in London! The Ramboat family didn't even know the names of the people living on either side of them. I remember when we were waiting for Marion before leaving for our honeymoon, Mr. Ramboat came and stood next to me, staring out the window. Nodding toward the house opposite as if to make conversation, he said, "There was a funeral over there yesterday. It was quite a grand affair with a glass hearse...."

And our small procession of three carriages, led by horses and drivers decorated with white ribbons, a sign of celebrating a wedding, passed through the huge, noisy, indifferent traffic jam like a lost porcelain doll dropped into the coal chute of an ironclad, a warship,. No one made way for us, and no one paid attention to us. The omnibus driver mocked us, and we had to crawl behind an unpleasant garbage truck for a long time. The irrelevant rattling and commotion added a bizarre and vulgar atmosphere to this public union of lovers. We felt like we had shamelessly overstepped our bounds. The crowd gathered outside the church would have gathered with the exact same mindset, and even more quickly, for a street traffic accident....

At Charing Cross station, a major train station in London, we were going to Hastings, the experienced eyes of the train conductor spotted the meaning of our unusual attire, that we were newlyweds, and secured a compartment for us. "Well," I said as the train pulled out of the station, "_Now_ it's all over!" And I turned to Marion, still in her unfamiliar clothes and looking a bit unfamiliar herself, and smiled. She looked at me seriously and timidly. "You're not angry?" she asked. "Angry! Why?" "About doing everything properly, according to custom,." "Oh, my dear Marion!" I said, and instead of answering, I took her white-gloved, leather-scented hand and kissed it....

I don't remember much else about the journey. It was about an hour of featureless time, because we were both confused and a little tired, and Marion had a mild headache and didn't want any caresses, physical affection,. I fell into a reverie about my aunt, and realized as if it were a new discovery that I cared for her very much. I deeply regretted not telling my aunt about my marriage sooner. But you wouldn't want to hear the history of my honeymoon. I've told you everything I need to accomplish my present purpose. Thus and so, the will in all things, the providence of the universe or instinctive urge, pushed me around at its whim. Driven by forces I couldn't understand, completely separated from the science, curiosity, and work I had once devoted myself to, I struggled through a tangle of tradition, custom, obstacles, and nonsense, became enraged, suppressed myself, and surrendered myself to things that, in my clear vision, were dishonorable and vain. And so I finally achieved the goal of half-blind nature, its relentless and immediate desire, and held Marion, who was crying reluctantly, in my arms, far from happiness.

V

Who can tell the story of the slow alienation between a married couple, the weakening first of this bond and then of that bond in this complex contact? The people who can least tell it are probably one of the two parties involved. Even now, after a lapse of fifteen years to clarify it, I still find countless impressions of Marion as confusing, unharmonious, unsystematic, and self-contradictory as life itself. Thinking of one thing I love her, thinking of another I hate her. There are now hundreds of aspects from which I can view her with dispassionate sympathy. As I sit here trying to express some perspective on this infinitely confusing process, I recall moments of cold and fierce alienation, moments of clouded intimacy, and the transitional process that has been completely forgotten. We became "friends" and used a little secret language between us; I was "Mutney" and she was "Ming." And we kept up such a good outward pretense that until the very end, Smithie, a friend, thought our family was the most harmonious in the world.

I cannot fully explain how Marion frustrated me and failed in the life of intimate emotion that is the core of love. That life of intimate emotion is made up of tiny things. A beautiful face is distinguished from an ugly face by differences in surface and proportion, and those differences are sometimes almost infinitesimally small. I find myself writing down little things, tiny things. None of it does more than prove the fundamental temperamental dissonance I have already tried to make clear. Some readers will understand, but to others I will seem like nothing more than a heartless brute who couldn't be tolerant.... It's easy to be tolerant now. But to be tolerant when you are young and passionate, to see your married life unfold before you, a life that at its dawn seemed like glory, a rose garden, a place of deep, sweet mystery, heart-pounding excitement, and wonderful silence, only to see it turn into a landscape of endurance and childish baby talk. That is compromise, and the most useless thing in a person's life.

Every romance novel I read seemed to mock our boring relationship, and every poem, every beautiful picture reflected the dry, uneventful succession

I will tell you about the gray hours we spent together. I think our real difference was a difference in aesthetic sensibility.

What I still remember as the absolute worst and most miserable aspect of all that time was her total disregard for her own beauty. It is the most trivial thing I know, but she would wear curl-papers in her hair in my presence. It was also her idea to "wear out" her old, ruined clothes at home when "no one was around to see her"—and that "no one" was me. She allowed me to accumulate unappealing and untidy memories of her...

All our concepts of life were different. I remember how we disagreed about furniture. We spent three or four days on Tottenham Court Road, a commercial street in London full of furniture shops, and she selected the things she liked with relentless determination, dismissing my suggestions with, "Oh, _you_ want such weird things." She pursued a limited, clearly visible, and already experienced ideal that excluded all other possibilities. Over every mantelpiece there was a draped mirror, and our sideboard was made of splendid and wonderful beveled glass—glass with decoratively angled edges. We had lamps on long metal stalks, cozy corners, and plants potted in grog-tubs. Smithie approved of all of this. In the entire house, there wasn't a single place where one could sit and read a book. My books were placed on a shelf in a corner of the dining room. And even though Marion's piano playing skills were at an elementary level, we still brought a piano into the house.

As you can tell, my insisting on marrying her, full of insecurity, skepticism, and constantly evolving ideas as I was, was the cruelest misfortune for Marion. She had no capacity for growth or change, and she was rigidly set in the limited ideas of a particular social class. With immense and unimaginative inflexibility—like a tailor-bird building its nest or a beaver building a dam—she maintained her concepts of what was right regarding drawing-room chairs, wedding ceremonies, and every relationship in life with simple, clear honesty and conviction.

I will quickly pass over this history of disappointment and separation. I could talk about the waxing and waning of love between us, but overall, it was waning. Sometimes she would do things for me, like making me a tie or a pair of slippers, and even though the items were absurd, they filled my heart with gratitude. She managed our home and our single maid with a cold, bright efficiency. She was excessively proud of the house and garden. She always fulfilled her duty to me in her own way.

Soon, the rapid development of the business around Tono-Bungay—the sham cure-all medicine my uncle created—began to take me to the provinces, and I would sometimes be away from home for a week at a time. She didn't like this. She said my absence made her "depressed," but it wasn't long before she started going back to Smithie's house and cultivating her independence from me. At Smithie's house, she was now a woman of status, and she had her own money to spend. She would take Smithie to the theater, buy her lunch, and talk endlessly about the business, making Smithie a sort of permanent weekend guest in our home. Marion also started raising a spaniel and dabbling in minor hobbies like poker-work, wood-burning art, taking photos with a Kodak camera, and growing hyacinths in glasses. She only visited the neighbors exactly once. My parents-in-law left Walham Green—my father-in-law had severed his ties with the gas-works—and came to live in a small house I rented for them nearby, and they spent a lot of time with us.

It's strange how trivial things can infuriate a person when the wellspring of life turns bitter! My father-in-law would constantly catch me in a gloomy mood and urge me to try gardening. He annoyed me to the extreme.

"You think too much," he would say. "If you picked up a spade and dug a little, you could soon make that garden of yours a 'Vision of Flowers.' That's better than just thinking, George."

Or he would say, with extreme irritation, "George, I simply cannot understand why you don't put some greenhouse glass up here. If you put glass in this sunny corner, you could work miracles."

And in the summer, he never entered the house without performing a sort of magic trick in the hallway, pulling cucumbers and tomatoes from unexpected places on his body. "All from 'my' little patch," he would say in an exemplary tone. He left trails of vegetables in the most bizarre places—on the mantelpiece, the sideboard, on top of picture frames. Good heavens! How a sudden, unexpected tomato could annoy me!...

The fact that Marion and my aunt failed to become friends, and instead became hostile toward each other out of a sort of instinct, played a large role in widening the gap between us.

First of all, my aunt visited quite frequently because she really wanted to get to know Marion. At first, my aunt would arrive like a whirlwind, filling the whole house with a boisterous atmosphere of greetings. My aunt was already dressed in that cheerful, extravagant abandon that signaled she had come into wealth, and she dressed in her absolute best for these visits.

I think my aunt wanted to act as a mother figure to me, and she wanted to tell Marion my hidden secrets, like how I wore out my boots or why I never thought to wear thicker clothes in cold weather. But Marion received my aunt with the suspicion typical of a defensive and shy person, conscious only of potential criticism directed at her. And my aunt, noticing this, became nervous and resorted to using slang...

"She says very strange things," Marion once said while discussing my aunt. "But I suppose it's meant to be witty."

"Yes," I said. "She is witty."

"If I spoke like she does—"

The strange things my aunt said were nothing compared to the strange things she *didn't* do. I remember my aunt in our drawing room one day, and I can never forget the expression—there's no other way to describe it—when she cast a sidelong glance at the India-rubber plant in a Doulton-ware pot, a famous British ceramic brand, that Marion had placed on the corner of the piano.

My aunt was just about to say something. Then suddenly she caught my expression, and she shrank back like a cat caught stealing milk.

Then a mischievous impulse took hold of my aunt.

"I didn't say a word, George," she insisted, looking me straight in the eye.

I smiled. When Marion scowled and entered the room to welcome my aunt, I said, "You're a dear for holding it in." But even though nothing had been said, I felt like a traitor—to the India-rubber plant, perhaps...

"Your aunt turns people into a Game, a mockery," was Marion's verdict, and she added, pretending to be open-minded, "I suppose... that's all right for her."

We went to their house in Beckenham, a wealthy neighborhood where my aunt and uncle lived, for lunch several times, and had dinner there once or twice. My aunt did her best in her own way to be friends, but Marion was stubborn. Also, I know Marion was terribly uncomfortable; she adopted a silence that exhausted people as her social weapon, answering everything said to her firmly and without leaving any openings.

The gaps between my aunt's visits grew wider and wider.

My married life eventually became like a narrow, deep pit within the broad world of interests I was living in. I traveled the world, met countless people of various personalities, and endlessly read books on trains. At my uncle's house, I developed social relationships that Marion could not share. The seeds of new ideas poured into me and grew inside me. A person's early and mid-twenties, their third decade, is probably the period of greatest mental growth. Those years are restless and filled with vague ambition.

Every time I returned to Ealing, where Marion and I lived, the life there felt more alien, narrow, and unappealing, and Marion seemed less beautiful, more limited, and harder to deal with. Finally, she was stripped of every fragment of the magic she once possessed. I think she always welcomed me coldly, and eventually she seemed completely indifferent. At the time, I never asked myself what heartbreak she might be hiding or what her complaints might be.

I would come home wishing for nothing and expecting nothing.

This was my fated life, and I had chosen it. I became more sensitive to the flaws I had once completely ignored. I began to associate her sallow skin with a temperamental deficiency, and the heavy lines around her mouth and nostrils with her dissatisfied moods. We slowly drifted apart...

The gap between us widened more and more. I had grown tired of her childish way of talking and her predictable, shallow shows of affection. I was also weary of the latest updates from her workplace—those supposedly wonderful workrooms of Smithie's—and I let my boredom show far too obviously. We hardly spoke when we were alone together. All that was left was the lingering, unreturned physical frustration of my passion, which only fueled the irritation between us.

No children came along to save our marriage. During her time working at Smithie’s, Marion had developed a deep disgust and fear of motherhood. To her, having children represented the ultimate realization of all the "horrid" aspects of life—a disgusting fate and a final humiliation that struck down careless women. Honestly, I doubt a child would have saved us anyway, as we would have argued bitterly over how to raise one.

Looking back, I remember my life with Marion as a prolonged period of distress, sometimes harsh and sometimes surprisingly tender. It was around that time that I first started critically examining my life, weighed down by a heavy sense that I had made a terrible mistake and ended up in the wrong place. I would lie awake at night questioning the purpose of everything. I reviewed my unfulfilling and awkward domestic life and the days I wasted on shady business ventures selling garbage medicine. I contrasted my current reality with my teenage ambitions and the dreams I had back in Wimblehurst. My situation felt inescapable, and I fruitlessly asked myself why I had ever forced myself into this life.

VI

Our unbearable situation came to a sudden and unexpected end, though I suppose it was almost bound to happen eventually.

My disconnected affections began to wander, and I was unfaithful to Marion.

I will not try to make excuses for my behavior. I was a young and fairly energetic man. My desire for love had been awakened and sharpened, yet neither my past romance nor my marriage had satisfied it. I had chased a fleeting illusion of beauty while ignoring everything else, and it had let me down. It faded exactly when I had hoped it would grow brighter. I became embittered and hopeless about life. And so, things unfolded just as I am describing. I am not trying to draw any moral conclusions from this, and I will leave the social solutions to the social reformers. I have reached an age where the only theories that interest me are general observations about reality.

To reach our private inner office on Raggett Street, I had to walk through the room where the typists worked. They handled our correspondence; our accounting and invoicing departments had long since expanded into the buildings we had luckily acquired on either side of our original office. I must admit I was always vaguely and emotionally aware of this group of mostly stooped-over young women. But soon, one of the girls stood out from the rest and completely captured my attention. At first, I simply noticed her small, straight back, which was much neater than the others. Then I noticed her soft, round neck adorned with a smiling string of fake pearls, her meticulously styled chestnut hair, and a quick sideways glance. Eventually, I recognized her as the face that would quickly turn to look for me.

My eyes naturally sought her out whenever I passed through on business. I dictated a few letters to her, which is how I noticed her pretty, soft-looking hands and pink fingernails. Once or twice, as we passed casually, our eyes met and held for a brief, intense second.

That was all it took. But in the mysterious, unspoken language of attraction, it was enough to communicate everything essential. We shared a secret.

One day, I came into the Raggett Street office around lunchtime, and she was sitting alone at her desk. She glanced up as I walked in, then suddenly froze, keeping her face lowered and her hands tightly clasped on the table. I walked right past her to the door of the inner office, stopped, turned back, and stood over her.

Neither of us spoke for a long, noticeable moment. I was trembling violently.

"Is that one of the new typewriters?" I finally asked, just to break the silence.

Without saying a word, she looked up at me. Her face was flushed, and her eyes were bright. I bent down and kissed her lips. She leaned back, wrapped an arm around me, pulled my face closer, and kissed me over and over. I lifted her up and held her tightly in my arms. She let out a soft, muffled cry as she felt herself being held.

I had never known such passionate kisses before.

We suddenly heard someone moving around in the front shop.

We sprang away from each other, our faces flushed and our eyes burning brightly.

"We can't talk here," I whispered with bold familiarity. "Where do you go at five o'clock?"

"Along the Embankment to Charing Cross," she replied just as intimately. "None of the other girls go that way..."

"Around half-past five?"

"Yes, half-past five..."

The door from the shop opened, and she quickly sat back down.

"I'm glad," I said in a normal, everyday voice, "that these new typewriters are working out well."

I went into the inner office and dug through the payroll sheets just to find her name—Effie Rink. I didn't get a single bit of work done that afternoon. I paced around that dingy little office like a caged animal.

When I eventually walked back out, Effie was working with an incredibly calm expression, and she didn't so much as glance in my direction.

We met and had our talk that evening, whispering in a place where no one could overhear us. We reached an understanding. The whole experience was strangely different from any romantic fantasy I had ever imagined.

VII

After being away for a week, I returned home a changed man. I had burned through my initial rush of passion for Effie and had taken time to reflect on my situation. I figured out where Effie fit into my life and had temporarily parted ways with her. She had returned to her desk at Raggett Street after a brief "illness." As I opened the small cast-iron gate that protected Marion's front garden and pampas grass from stray dogs, I felt absolutely no regret or shame. If anything, I felt as though I had reclaimed some personal right that had been denied to me. I came back to Marion without a shred of guilt, and actually felt a renewed sense of friendliness toward her. I don't know if that's how one is supposed to feel in these situations, but that is exactly how I felt.

I followed her into our drawing-room. She was standing next to the tall lampstand that took up half the bay window, looking as though she had just turned away from watching for me outside. Something about her pale face stopped me in my tracks. She looked like she hadn't slept at all. She didn't step forward to greet me.

"You've come home," she said.

"Just as I wrote you I would."

She stood perfectly still, a dark silhouette against the bright window.

"Where have you been?" she asked.

"On the East Coast," I lied smoothly.

She paused for a second. "I *know*," she said.

I stared at her. It was the most shocking moment of my life.

"By Jove!" I finally said. "I believe you do!"

"And then you came back to me!"
I walked over to the hearth rug and stood completely still, considering this new situation.
"I never even imagined it," she began. "How could you do such a thing?"
It felt like a long time passed before either of us said another word.
"Who knows about this?" I finally asked.
"Smithie’s brother. They were at Cromer."
"Damn Cromer! I see."
"How could you let yourself—"
I felt a surge of sheer irritation at this unexpected disaster.
"I'd like to wring Smithie’s brother’s neck," I said.
Marion spoke in dry, fragmented sentences. "You... I always believed that, no matter what, you wouldn't deceive me... I guess all men are awful about this sort of thing."
"I don't think it's awful. To me, it seems like the most inevitable consequence—the most natural thing in the world."
I heard someone moving in the hallway, so I went and closed the room's door. Then, I returned to the hearth rug and turned around.
"It's hard on you," I said. "But I didn't intend for you to find out. You've never really cared for me. I've been going through hell. Why should you even care?"
She sat down in an upholstered armchair. "I have cared for you," she said.
I shrugged.
"I suppose," she said, "she cares for you?"
I had nothing to say.
"Where is she now?"
"Oh! What does it matter to you?... Look, Marion! I didn't foresee this. I didn't mean for this to crash down on you like this. But, you know, something was bound to happen. I'm sorry—I'm truly sorry from the bottom of my heart that things between us have come to this. But honestly, I'm just as surprised. I don't know where I stand, or how we even ended up here. Everything took me by surprise. One day, I found myself alone with her. I kissed her. And I kept going. Turning back seemed foolish. Besides, why should I have turned back? Why? From beginning to end, I barely even thought about how it would affect you. Damn it!"
She studied my face closely and tugged at the ball fringe of the small table next to her.
"When I think about it," she said, "I don't think I can ever touch you again."
We sat in a long silence. I was only just beginning to grasp, in the most superficial way, the sheer scale of the disaster that had unfolded between us. Massive issues had suddenly crashed into our lives. I felt completely unprepared and entirely inadequate. I was irrationally angry. A flood of foolish things to say rushed into my mind, but my growing realization of how crucial this moment was kept me from saying them. The silence stretched out, threatening to leave a permanent mark—a space where any one of a thousand thoughtless remarks might forever poison our relationship.

Our young maid knocked on the door—Marion always insisted that the servants knock—and came in.
"Tea is ready, ma'am," she said, and then disappeared, leaving the door open.
"I'll go upstairs," I said, pausing. "I'll go upstairs," I repeated, "and leave my bag in the spare room."
We stood motionless and silent for a few seconds.
"Mother is having tea with us today," Marion finally remarked. She let go of the frayed ball fringe and slowly stood up.
And so, with the massive weight of our changed relationship hanging over us, we soon sat down for tea with the completely oblivious Mrs. Ramboat and the spaniel. Mrs. Ramboat was far too polite to acknowledge our gloomy mood. She kept up a steady stream of light chatter, mentioning, as I recall, that Mr. Ramboat was "distressed" over his cannas—a type of showy flowering plant.
"They won't bloom, and they're never going to bloom. He went and had words with the man who sold him the bulbs—he's very angry and upset."
The spaniel was incredibly annoying, begging and performing little tricks for each of us in turn. Neither of us used his name. We had always called him Miggles, forming a silly trio in our baby talk: Mutney, Miggles, and Ming.

VIII
Not long after, we resumed our heavy, momentous conversation. I can no longer remember exactly how long that dialogue lasted. I know it dragged on in heavy, fragmented stretches over three or four days. I recall sitting with Marion on the bed in her room, or standing in the dining room, trying to sort things out. We took two long walks. We also spent one long evening alone together, our nerves frayed and our hearts swinging between a cold, dreary acceptance of reality and—at least on my part—a strange, uncharacteristic tenderness. Oddly enough, this crisis had shattered our mutual indifference and made us truly feel for each other again.
The conversation was disjointed, breaking into chunks of talk that failed to connect with what had just been said. We would start over on a different level—sometimes higher, sometimes lower—taking on new angles in the pauses and absorbing new considerations. We talked about the fact that we were no longer lovers; we had never openly faced that before. It feels strange to write this, but looking back, I can clearly see that those few days were the closest Marion and I ever were. For the first and last time, we looked honestly and steadily into each other's souls. For those days alone, there were no pretenses. I made no concessions to her, and she made none to me. We hid nothing and exaggerated nothing. We were done with faking it. We dealt with each other plainly and soberly. Moods shifted rapidly, each one expressed with raw honesty.

Naturally, we argued—bitterly—and hurled words at each other: long-suppressed things that bruised, crushed, and cut. But above it all, what I remember most is the sense of a deliberate, direct confrontation. In my mind, Marion stands tall—pale, melancholic, tear-stained, deeply hurt, unforgiving, yet full of dignity.

"Do you love her?" she asked once, suddenly throwing that doubt into my mind.
I wrestled with my tangled thoughts and emotions. "I don't know what love is. It's a mixture of things—a dozen threads twisted together in a thousand different ways."
"But do you want her? Do you want her right now, when you think of her?"
"Yes," I admitted after a moment. "I definitely want her."
"And what about me? Where do I fit in?"
"I suppose you fit in right here."
"Well, but what are you going to do?"
"Do!" I exclaimed, my frustration with the whole situation boiling over.

"What do you want me to do?"

Looking back on all those times across a gap of fifteen active years, I find myself looking at them with understanding and judgment. I can judge the events objectively, without emotion, as if they were someone else's affair—or rather, the affair of two people I know very well. I now realize that the shock, this immense and sudden disillusionment, actually drew out the mind and soul hidden within Marion. For the first time, she broke free from her routines, timidity, imitations, clichés, and somewhat narrow impulses, emerging as an independent personality.

Her initial driving motive seemed to be an indignant, outraged pride. This situation simply had to end. She firmly demanded that I give up Effie, my affair partner, but filled with fresh and intense memories, I absolutely refused.

"It’s too late, Marion," I said. "It doesn't work like that."

"Then we can hardly continue living together," she said. "Can we?"

"Very well," I said after careful consideration, "if you insist."

"Well, can we?"

"Could you stay in this house? I mean—if I were to leave?"

"I don’t know.... I don't think I could."

"Then—what is it you want?"

Slowly, we worked our way through each issue until the word 'divorce' finally lay before us.

"If we can't live together, we must be set free," Marion said.

"I know nothing about divorce," I said, "if that's what you mean. I don't know how it is done. I'll have to ask someone or look it up.... Perhaps, in the end, it's what we have to do. We might as well face it."

Through our conversation, we began to realize the reality of our diverging futures. That evening, I returned having gotten answers to my questions from a solicitor, a British lawyer.

"As things stand, we can't actually get divorced," I explained. "Apparently, the law requires you to just endure this sort of thing. It's ridiculous, but that is the law. However, arranging a divorce is easy. In addition to adultery, there has to be desertion or cruelty. To prove cruelty, I would have to strike you or do something similar in front of witnesses. That’s impossible. But legally deserting you is simple. All I have to do is leave you. That’s it. I can continue sending you money, and you file a suit—what was it called?—a suit for the Restitution of Conjugal Rights. Then the court will order me to return. I disobey. After that, you can proceed with a divorce suit. You receive a Decree Nisi, the first stage of an English divorce, and the court tries one more time to make me return. If we don't reconcile within six months and you don't behave scandalously, the decree becomes absolute. That’s the end of the fuss. That is how people get divorced. As you can see, it is easier to marry than to unmarry."

"And then—how am I supposed to live? What becomes of me?"

"You'll have an income. They call it alimony. About a third to a half of my current income—or more if you wish, I don't mind. Let's say 300 pounds a year, which is a considerable amount. You have elderly parents to support, and you'll need all of it."

"And then—then you'll be free?"

"Both of us."

"And all this life you have hated..."

I looked up at her deeply distressed and bitter face. "I didn't hate it," I lied, my voice almost breaking from the pain of it all. "Did you?"

IX

The perplexing thing about life is the unsolvable complexity of reality, of both things and relationships. Nothing is simple. There is a certain justification in every wrongdoing, and remnants of evil in every good deed. As for us, still young and lacking in self-awareness, hundreds of discordant notes echoed from the harsh corners of that shock. We were fiercely angry at each other, yet tender; we were ruthlessly selfish, yet generously self-sacrificing.

I remember Marion saying countless disjointed things that didn't align and contradicted one another, yet were deeply sincere and true in their own contexts. I now see them as numerous vain experiments in her effort to grasp the crumpled chaos of our moral landslide. I found some of them endlessly irritating. Sometimes, I answered her quite dreadfully.

"Of course," she would repeat again and again, "my life has been a failure."

"I laid siege to you for three years," I would retort, "hoping it wouldn't be. You did as you pleased. And now that I've finally turned away—"

Or she would bring up all the stress from before we were married.

"How you must hate me! I made you wait. Well now—it seems you have your revenge."

"Revenge!" I echoed.

Then she would try to envision aspects of our new, separate lives.

"I must earn my own living," she would insist.

"I want to be completely independent. I've always hated London. Perhaps I'll try running a poultry farm and keeping bees. You won't mind me being a burden at first. Afterwards—"

"We've settled all that," I said.

"I suppose you'll hate me anyway..."

There were times when she seemed entirely complacent about our separation, planning all sorts of freedoms and unique interests.

"I will go out a lot with Smithie," she said.

And once, she said something so ugly that it truly made me hate her, something I still cannot entirely forgive even now.

"Your aunt will rejoice at all this. She never cared for me..."

Amidst these memories of pain and stress, the image of Smithie comes to mind—so overwhelmed with emotion and breathless in the presence of the terrible villain of the story, me, that she couldn't articulate a sound. I know she had long, tearful, heart-to-heart talks with Marion, clinging to her in deep sympathy. There were moments when only her absolute inability to speak stopped her from giving me a massive lecture; I could see it in her eyes. What dreadful things she would have said! I also remember Mrs. Ramboat, Marion's mother, slowly sensing that something was going on. The expression of concern grew in her eyes, and only her well-trained fear of Marion kept her from speaking out.

Finally, through all this turmoil, parting came to Marion and me, like something fated and entirely beyond our control.

I had to harden my heart, or I wouldn't have been able to leave. Because, in the end, it dawned on Marion that she was parting from me forever. That realization overpowered everything else and turned our final hour into agony. For a moment, she forgot about moving to a new house, and she forgot the insult to her possessiveness and pride. For the first time in her life, she showed truly strong emotions toward me, and perhaps, for the first time, those emotions genuinely reached her. She began to cry slow, reluctant tears. When I walked into her room, I found her sprawled on the bed, weeping.

"I didn't know," she cried out. "Oh! I didn't understand!"

"I have been a fool. My life is completely ruined!
"I will be alone!... Mutney, an affectionate nickname a wife calls her husband,! Mutney, do not leave me! Oh! Mutney! I did not understand."
I had to harden my heart at her words. This was because, during the last few hours we spent together, there were moments when it seemed that what I had longed for so much had finally happened and Marion had come back to life, even though it was too late. A newly bloomed desire for me made her eyes shine.
"Do not leave me!" she said. "Do not leave me!" She clung to me, and kissed me with lips that tasted salty from tears.
Since I had already made a promise and a pledge to Effie, my new lover, I firmly made up my mind in the face of this impossible new beginning. However, there were certainly moments when it seemed like only a single cry, just a single word, would be enough to reunite us for the rest of our lives. Could we have reunited? Would that brief moment have given us an eternal realization, or would we have gone back to our old alienation and old temperamental opposition, personality differences that do not match, in just about a week?
Now there is no way to know that. Our own resolve led us down the already determined path of separation. We acted more and more like lovers parting coldly, but all the preparations for separation that we had started continued to progress like a machine, and we did not try to stop them. My large luggage bags and boxes were sent to the train station. I packed a small bag with Marion standing in front of me. We were like children who had given each other terrible wounds purely out of foolishness, and now did not know how to reverse it. We belonged to each other tremendously, oh so tremendously. A carriage came in front of the small iron gate.
"Goodbye!" I said.
"Goodbye."
For a moment, we held each other in our arms and kissed. Incredibly, there was no malice at all. We heard the sound of the young maid going to open the door in the hallway. For the last time, we embraced each other. We were neither lovers nor enemies, just two human beings honestly sharing pain. I pulled myself away from her.
"Go away," I said to the maid, seeing that Marion had followed me downstairs.
I felt her standing behind my back as I spoke to the carriage driver.
I got into the carriage, determined never to look back, but as the carriage started, I jumped up, stretched my neck, and looked at the door.
The door was wide open, but she had disappeared....
I wonder. Perhaps she ran upstairs.

Chapter 10, X,

Like that, I parted with Marion in extreme confusion and regret, and went to Effie, who was waiting for me in an apartment near Orpington, a village on the outskirts of London, just as I had promised and planned. I remember her bright and nimble figure turning her gaze along the train looking for me on the train station platform, and our walk across the fields at dusk. I had expected to feel a massive sense of relief when the stress of parting was finally over, but now I realized that I was filled with a deep conviction that I was unfathomably miserable and confused, and that I had made an irreversible mistake. The dim dusk and the gloomy Marion were so similar that it felt as if her sadness was hovering all around me. I had to stick to my plans, remembering that I must keep my faith with Effie, who had thrown herself into my arms without setting any conditions or demanding any guarantees.
We walked across the evening fields in silence toward the deepening gold and purple sky, and Effie was always by my side, very close, occasionally looking up at my face.
Clearly, she knew that I was grieving because of Marion, and that our reunion was no longer joyful. However, she showed no anger or jealousy. Surprisingly, she did not try to compete with Marion. During all the time we spent together, she never once said a negative word about Marion....
Before long, she tried to shake off the dark shadow that was weighing me down with the same instinctive skill that some women show when dealing with a child's problems. She made herself my joyful and pretty slave and maid, and finally made me rejoice because of her. But behind all of that, the foolish, tearful, and endlessly sorrowful Marion remained, so I was almost unbearably unhappy when I thought of her, and when I thought of the dead love of our marriage.

All these things I am telling you now are inexplicable even to me. I try to go back to this distant past, which is like the unfrequented highlands and remote wastelands of memory, but it still feels like a strange land. I thought I would be going to a sensual paradise with Effie, but desire, which filled the universe before it was satisfied, completely disappears like the daylight vanishing once the goal is achieved. All the facts and forms of life remain dark and cold. It was a highland full of melancholy questions, and from there I came to look at the entire world from a completely new angle and a new perspective. I had bypassed and passed by the aspects of passion and romance.

I fell into a state of massive confusion. For the first time in my entire life, at least that is how it feels looking back now, I looked at my entire existence.

If this is nothing, what am I doing? What do I exist for?

I wandered here and there for the 'Tono-Bungay' business, a business I started to support Marion, which held me back despite our intimate separation, and used up my remaining weekends and nights to go to Orpington, all while wrestling with these persistent questions. I used to fall into thought on the train, and became even a little inaccurate and forgetful about business matters. I most clearly remember sitting lost in thought on a grassy hill overlooking the wide countryside toward Sevenoaks, a town in Kent, agonizing over my destiny in the evening sunshine. I could even write down the thoughts that came to me that afternoon right now. Effie, a restless London native, was rustling and wandering around the hedges below, picking flowers she had never seen before. I had a letter from Marion in my pocket. I had even made a few attempts to go back for reconciliation. Only Heaven knows what I wrote in the letter! But her cold, poorly written letter pushed me away. I realized that I could never again face that old, inconclusive boredom of life, that stagnant disappointment. Anyway, it was impossible. But what was possible? I could not see an honorable or excellent path of life laid out before me at all.

"What should I do with this life?" That was the question that persistently tormented me.

I wondered if all the people in the world were like me, creatures driven by this motive and that motive, dragged around by chance, impulse, and meaningless traditions. Did I really have to bear the consequences of the things I had said, done, and chosen? Was there no honorable path for me other than to support Effie, return penitently to Marion, and keep my job selling junk, or find a new job and live out the rest of my life? I did not accept that for a single moment. But what else could I do? I wondered if my case was the same as the cases of many other people, and whether people in past eras were also so guideless, mapless, and reliant on chance in their journey into life. In the Middle Ages, in the old Catholic days, when people went to a priest, the priest would speak with the finality of natural law, saying that you are this kind of person, so you must do this. I wondered if I would have accepted that verdict without any questions even if I had lived in the Middle Ages.

I also remember very clearly Effie coming and sitting next to me on a small box. It was in front of the casement window in our room.

"Gloomkins, a playful nickname for someone gloomy," she said.

I was looking out the window, resting my head on my hand and smiling, forgetting about her.

"Did you love your wife that much?" she whispered softly.

"Oh!" I shouted, coming back to reality. "I do not know. I do not understand these things. Life is something that hurts, my love! Life hurts without logic or reason. I made a mistake! I did not understand. Anyway... there would be no need to hurt you, right?"

And I turned around, pulled her to me, and kissed her ear....

Yes, I went through a very difficult time. I still remember it vividly. I suppose I suffered from an ennui of the imagination, a kind of lethargy where I wandered without a clear goal to picture in my mind. I had no objective to hold my will together. I tried to find that goal. I read this and that restlessly and ramblingly. I also turned to my friend Ewart for support, but received no help from him. Looking back now, it seems that I truly discovered myself for the first time during those days filled with disgust and abandoned aims. Before that, I had only seen the world and the things within it, pursuing them while completely forgetting myself in favor of my impulses. But now, I found myself integrated into a system of endless desires and satisfactions. There was a mountain of work to do, but it felt as though no passion remained inside me.

There were moments when I considered suicide. At times, my life was exposed under a cold, relentless light as a continuous string of ignorance, crude mistakes, degradation, and cruelty. I experienced what the old theologians called a "conviction of sin." I longed for salvation. It might not have been the formal salvation a Methodist minister would recognize, but I nevertheless needed spiritual redemption and liberation.

Today, people seek salvation in various ways. I don't think the name or the form matters much. What we truly need is something we can hold onto, and something that holds onto us in return. I know a man who found that crucial element in a dry-plate factory, making glass plates for photography, and another who found it while writing the history of a manor. As long as it can hold a person steady, it doesn't matter what it is. Nowadays, many men and women throw themselves into specific aspects of socialism or social reform. But for me, socialism has always been too human, too cluttered with people's personalities and foolishness. It doesn't suit my temperament. I don't like things that are excessively human. That doesn't mean I think I am blind to the joys, the surprises, the pleasant little roughnesses, the insufficiencies of life, what people often call the 'humor of life,' or to adventure. But those are not the fundamental issues for me. There is no humor in my blood. I am earnest from head to toe in everything I do. I may stumble and flounder, but I know that beyond all these pleasant, immediate things, there exist things that are great, serene, incredibly high, and beautiful—the true reality. I haven't grasped it, but it undeniably exists. I am like a spiritual guttersnipe, a street urchin, who has fallen in love with unimaginable goddesses. I have never seen these goddesses and never will, but that love drains all the joy from this muddy reality. Sometimes, I fear it drains away all kindness as well.

But I am rambling about things I cannot expect my readers to understand, because I only half-understand them myself. There is something that connects various things for me: an occasional sunset, a certain mood, the air at high altitudes, something that resided in Marion's form and color, something I find and then lose in the paintings of Mantegna, the Italian Renaissance artist, and something in the lines of these boats I build., You simply must see my latest and greatest creation, the X2!,

I know I cannot explain myself well. Perhaps all this simply boils down to the fact that I am a mean, morally limited scoundrel with a mind that exceeds his station. Naturally, I resist accepting that as the complete answer. In any case, I had a relentless need, an unbearable sense of agony and deprivation, and for a while, this aeronautical engineering soothed it.

At the end of this peculiar crisis I am clumsily describing, I came to idealize science. I decided that my life's salvation lay in power and knowledge, and that they held the secret to fulfilling my needs. I resolved to dedicate myself to these things.

I finally emerged like a man who had dived into the dark depths and surfaced clutching a new resolve he had been desperately groping for over a long time.

Suddenly, one day, I walked into the inner office. It must have been right before Marion filed her lawsuit for the restitution of conjugal rights. I sat down in front of my uncle.

"Look here," I said. "I am sick of this."

"Whoa!" my uncle replied, pushing some documents aside.

"What's the matter, George?"

"Everything is wrong."

"How so?"

"My life," I said. "It's a total mess. It's an endless cesspool."

"She was a bit foolish, George," my uncle said. "I understand to some extent. But practically speaking, you are done with her now, and there are plenty of fish in the sea—"

"Oh! It's not about that!" I shouted. "That's just what shows on the surface. I am sick of it. I am sick of this whole damn scam."

"Huh? What?" my uncle said. "What scam?"

"Oh, you know what I mean. I need something 'real.' Something I can hold onto. If I don't get it, I'll go crazy. I am a different kind of animal from you. You float around in all this nonsense. But I feel like a man thrashing about in a universe full of soapy water, lost up and down, east and west. I can't stand it. I need to plant my feet on something solid, or else—I don't know what will happen to me."

I burst out laughing at the bewildered look on my uncle's face.

"I mean it," I said. "I've thought about it deeply. I've made up my mind. There is no use arguing. I am going to start working. Real work. No! This isn't work. It's just exhausting fraud. But I have an idea! It's an old idea. I thought of it years ago, but it came back to me. Look here! Why should I argue with you like this? I believe the time has come for flight to be possible. True flying!"

"Flying!"

I clung to that idea, and it helped me survive the most horrific period of my life. After some mild resistance and a consultation with my aunt, my uncle acted like the father of a spoiled son. He arranged a deal that provided me with plenty of capital to use and freed me from worrying too much about our new business expansions—this was during what we might call the later Moggs era of our corporate history. So, I immediately set to work with terrifying focus.

But I will talk about my gliders and airplanes at the proper time. I have neglected my uncle's story for far too long. I merely wanted to explain how I ended up plunging into this work. I threw myself into these experiments after finding something that Marion seemed to promise in some indescribable way. I worked hard, forgot myself for a while, and accomplished many things. Science has been like an unresponsive lover ever since, but I have devoted myself to science more than I ever devoted myself to Marion. Yet, at that time, science, with its order and its inhuman detachment, but also its steely certainty, rescued me from despair.

Well, I haven't managed to fly yet. But as a byproduct, I have invented the lightest engines in the world.

I am trying to recount everything that happened to me. It is incredibly difficult to record them with even a shred of accuracy. But this is a novel, not a dissertation. Please do not imagine that I am about to arrive at some solution to my struggles. Even now, buried here among blueprints and the sound of hammering, I still question unanswerable problems. My entire life has been a process of 'seeking' from the very bottom, always doubting, always dissatisfied with what is seen and what is believed, searching for something through hard labor, through force, and through danger. I sought something whose name and true nature I do not clearly understand, something beautiful, worthy of worship, enduring, profoundly and fundamentally mine, and the ultimate salvation of myself. I don't know. All I can say is that it is something I have never once managed to find.

Chapter 11

But before I wrap up this chapter and Book Two entirely and move on to the great adventure of my uncle's career, I think I will finish telling the remaining story of Marion and Effie, and then set my private life aside for a while.

For some time, Marion and I kept in touch fairly regularly, writing friendly but rather dry letters about minor business matters. That clumsy divorce process was also finalized.

She left the house in Ealing and moved to the country with her aunt and parents, renting a small farm near Lewes in Sussex. She covered a greenhouse in glass, installed a heating system for her father—a happy man!—and talked about figs and peaches. Throughout the spring and summer, everything seemed to be going well, and

The Sussex, a rural area in southern England, winter proved too harsh for the Ramboats after their life in London. They became covered in mud and lost their energy. Mr. Ramboat accidentally killed a cow by feeding it improperly, which disheartened everyone. After a year, their farm business was struggling. I had to help her out of that situation, and afterward, they returned to London. She went into business with Smithie in Streatham, a residential area in south London, running an enterprise branded as "Robes" on the company stationery. Her parents and aunt were tucked away in a small country cottage somewhere. Letters became infrequent after that. However, I remember a postscript in one letter that brought a painful reminder of our old intimacy: "Poor old Miggles died."

Nearly eight years passed. I matured. I grew into a fully formed adult in both experience and capability, living on a grander scale in a wider world than I could have ever imagined during my days with Marion, and I developed many new interests. Her letters became rare and meaningless. Eventually, a period of complete silence made me curious. For over eighteen months, I received nothing from Marion except her quarterly bank receipts. Finally, blaming Smithie in frustration, I wrote a postcard to Marion.

"Dear Marion," I wrote, "How are you doing?"

She surprised me greatly by revealing that she had remarried—to "a Mr. Wachorn, a leading agent in the paper-pattern, paper templates used in dressmaking, industry." Yet, she still wrote on the Ponderevo & Smith, Robes, stationery from the Ponderevo and Smith address.

Aside from a minor disagreement over the continuation of alimony payments that caused me some anger, and the company's continued use of my name which annoyed me, that is the end of Marion’s history for me. She vanishes from this story. I have no idea where she is or what she is doing. I do not even know if she is alive or dead. It feels entirely grotesque to me that two people who were once as close as she and I were should be so thoroughly separated, but that is how it is between us.

I also parted ways with Effie, though I still see her occasionally. There was never any intention of marriage or spiritual connection between us. We shared a sudden, fierce, and hot-blooded passion for each other, but I was neither her first lover nor her last. She lived in a completely different world from Marion. She had a strange and delightful personality; I have no memory of ever seeing her sullen or malicious. She was—quite remarkably—eupeptic, originally meaning having good digestion, but representing a bright and optimistic disposition,. I believe that was the central secret to her agreeableness, and furthermore, she was endlessly kind-hearted. Eventually, I helped her secure a position she had wanted, and she amazed me with a sudden display of business acumen. She now runs a typewriting agency in Riffle's Inn with brisk energy and considerable success, even though she has gained some weight. And she still loves her own kind. About a year ago, she married a boy half her age—a hopeless, wretched poet who was addicted to drugs, a creature with thin, light hair falling into his blue eyes and limp legs. She said she did it because he needed nursing....

But that is enough about the disaster of my marriage and my early love affairs. I have told you everything necessary for this picture to explain how I became involved in airplane experiments and engineering science. Let me return to my core story, to Tono-Bungay, my uncle's promotional schemes, and the vision of the world that these things have given me.
