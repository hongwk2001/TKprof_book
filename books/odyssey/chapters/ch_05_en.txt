Book 5

Calypso and Odysseus: Reaching Scheria on a Raft

As Dawn, with her rosy fingers, rose from her bed beside Tithonus, bringing light to both mortals and immortals, the gods gathered for a council. Among them sat Zeus, the lord of thunder and king of all gods. Then Athena began to speak of Odysseus's many trials, suffering far away in the nymph Calypso's home. She felt deep pity for him, so far from home.

Athena spoke. "Father Zeus, and all you other gods who live in eternal happiness, I hope there will never again be a kind, good ruler, or a king who governs fairly. Let them all be cruel and unjust from now on. This is because not one of his people remembers Odysseus, who ruled them like a loving father. Now, Odysseus lies in terrible pain on the island where the nymph Calypso lives. She refuses to let him go. He cannot return to his homeland. He can find no ships or sailors to carry him across the sea. What's more, wicked men are now trying to murder his only son, Telemachus. Telemachus is on his way home from Pylos and Lacedaemon, where he went to seek news of his father."

Zeus replied to his daughter. "Athena, what are you talking about, my dear? Didn't you send him there yourself, thinking it would help Odysseus return home and punish the suitors? Besides, you are perfectly capable of protecting Telemachus and bringing him safely home. The suitors will have to flee in a panic without having killed him."

After speaking, Zeus turned to his son, Hermes. "Hermes, you are our messenger. Go tell Calypso that poor Odysseus must return home. This is our decree. He will have no escort, neither gods nor men. After a perilous twenty-day voyage on a raft, he will reach fertile Scheria. That is the land of the Phaeacians, who are close to the gods. They will honor Odysseus as if he were one of us. They will send him home on a ship and give him more bronze, gold, and fine clothes than he would have brought back from Troy, even if he had returned safely with all his spoils. This is how we have decided he will return to his country and his friends."

As Zeus finished speaking, Hermes, the gods' guide, guardian, and slayer of Argus, set off to obey. He immediately fastened on his glittering golden sandals. These sandals allowed Hermes to fly like the wind over land and sea. He took his wand, which could seal men's eyes in sleep or wake them at his will, and flew with it over Pieria. Soon, he swooped down through the sky until he reached the surface of the sea. He skimmed across the waves, like a cormorant hunting fish in every nook and cranny of the ocean, its thick feathers drenched in spray. He flew on and on over countless weary waves. Finally, he reached the island, his journey's end. He left the sea and traveled by land until he came to the cave where the nymph Calypso lived.

Hermes found Calypso at home in her cave. A large fire blazed on the hearth, and the fragrant scent of burning cedar and sandalwood drifted far and wide. Calypso herself sat at her loom, weaving with a golden shuttle and singing a beautiful song. Around her cave grew a thick forest of alder, poplar, and sweet-smelling cypress trees. All sorts of large birds nested there: owls, hawks, and noisy sea-crows busy by the water. A lush vine, heavy with grapes, grew abundantly around the cave's entrance. Four small streams flowed through narrow channels, winding this way and that to water beds of violets and rich green grass. Even a god would be enchanted by such a lovely place. Hermes paused, taking in the scene. Only after he had admired it sufficiently did he step inside the cave.

Calypso recognized Hermes at once, for gods always know each other, no matter how far apart they live. But Odysseus was not inside. As usual, he sat on the seashore, gazing at the desolate ocean with tear-filled eyes, groaning with a broken heart. Calypso offered Hermes a seat and spoke. "Hermes, honored and always welcome, why have you come to see me? You don't visit often. Tell me what you wish. If I can do it, and if it's possible, I will do it at once. But first, come inside and let me offer you some refreshment."

As she spoke, Calypso placed a table laden with ambrosia, the food of the immortals, beside Hermes and mixed him some red nectar. After Hermes had eaten and drunk his fill, he began to speak.

"We are speaking as god and goddess, and you ask why I have come. I will tell you the truth, as you wish. Zeus sent me. It was not my own choice. Who would want to travel all this way across the sea, where there are no cities full of people to offer me sacrifices or grand offerings? But I had to come, for none of us gods can defy Zeus or disobey his commands. Zeus says that the man you hold here is the most unfortunate of all those who fought for nine years before King Priam's city of Troy, then plundered it in the tenth year and sailed for home. On their way home, they sinned against the goddess Athena, who stirred up fierce winds and waves against them. All his brave companions perished, and he alone was carried here by the wind and waves. Zeus commands you to let this man go at once. It is decreed that he will not die here, far from his people, but will return to his home and country and see his friends again."

Calypso trembled with rage when she heard this. She cried out, "You gods should be ashamed of yourselves! You are always consumed by jealousy, unable to stand seeing a goddess fall for a mortal man and live openly with him. When rosy-fingered Dawn, Eos, fell in love with Orion, all you precious gods were furious, until Artemis went and killed him in Ortygia. And when the goddess Demeter fell in love with Iasion and lay with him in a thrice-plowed field, Zeus soon found out and struck Iasion dead with his thunderbolts. Now you're angry with me too, just because I have a man here. I found the poor creature sitting alone on a ship's keel. Zeus had struck his ship with lightning and sunk it in the middle of the ocean. All his crew drowned, and he himself was driven by wind and waves onto my island. I grew fond of him and cherished him. I had hoped to make him immortal, so he would never grow old. But I cannot defy Zeus or bring his plans to nothing. So, if he insists, I will send the man back across the seas. However, I cannot send him myself, for I have neither ships nor men to take him. Nevertheless, I will gladly give him honest advice that will help him return safely to his homeland."

"Then send him away," Hermes said, "or Zeus will be angry with you and punish you."

With that, Hermes departed. Calypso, having heard Zeus's message, went to find Odysseus. She found him sitting on the beach, his eyes always full of tears, dying of sheer homesickness. He had grown weary of Calypso. Though he was forced to sleep with her in the cave at night, it was her will, not his. During the day, he spent his time on the rocks and seashore, weeping, crying out in despair, and always gazing at the sea. Calypso approached him and said,

"My poor man, you don't have to waste your life here grieving and suffering any longer. I am going to send you away of my own free will. Go, cut some wood, and build yourself a sturdy raft. Make it large, with an upper deck, so it can carry you safely across the sea. I will put bread, wine, and water on board to keep you from starving. I will also give you clothes and send a fair wind to take you home. That is, if the gods in heaven wish it, for they know more about these things and can settle them better than I can."

Odysseus shuddered at her words. He replied, "Goddess, I sense there's something else behind all this. You can't truly mean to help me home by telling me to do such a terrible thing as put to sea on a raft. Not even a sturdy ship with a fair wind could attempt such a long voyage. I will not board a raft, no matter what you say or do, unless you first solemnly swear that you intend me no harm."

Calypso smiled at this and caressed him with her hand. She said, "You know a great deal, but you are quite wrong about this. Let the heavens above, the earth below, and the waters of the river Styx be my witnesses—this is the most solemn oath a blessed god can take—that I intend you no harm whatsoever. I am only advising you to do exactly what I would do if I were in your place. I am being completely straightforward with you. My heart is not made of iron, and I truly feel sorry for you."

After she spoke, Calypso quickly led the way, and Odysseus followed her steps. The goddess and the man walked on until they reached Calypso's cave, where Odysseus took the seat Hermes had just vacated. Calypso set meat and drink before him, the kind of food mortals eat. Her maids, however, brought ambrosia and nectar for Calypso herself, and they all enjoyed the delicious food laid before them. Once they had eaten and drunk their fill, Calypso began to speak.

"Odysseus, noble son of Laertes, you truly wish to return to your homeland at once? Good luck to you, but if you only knew how much suffering awaits you before you reach your country, you would stay here and live with me, letting me make you immortal. No matter how much you long to see your wife, whom you think of day after day. Honestly, I flatter myself that I am no less tall or beautiful than she is. A mortal woman cannot truly compare in beauty to an immortal goddess."

Odysseus replied, "Goddess, please don't be angry with me about this. I am well aware that my wife, Penelope, is not as tall or beautiful as you. She is merely a mortal woman, while you are an immortal. Nevertheless, I long to return home, and I can think of nothing else. If some god wrecks me while I am at sea, I will endure it and do my best. I have already suffered countless hardships on both land and sea, so I will accept this as part of them."

The sun set, and darkness fell. The two of them went into the inner part of the cave and settled down for the night.

When rosy-fingered Dawn, child of morning, appeared, Odysseus put on his shirt and cloak. Calypso, the goddess, wore a light, delicate, and elegant silk dress. She had a beautiful golden girdle around her waist and a veil covering her head. Calypso immediately began to think about how she could help Odysseus prepare for his journey. She gave him a large, sturdy bronze axe that fit perfectly in his hands. It was sharpened on both sides and had a beautiful olive-wood handle firmly attached. Calypso also handed him a sharp adze. Then, she led Odysseus to the farthest part of the island, where towering alder, poplar, and pine trees grew, reaching for the sky. These trees were very dry and well-seasoned, perfect for floating lightly on the water. After showing him where the best trees grew, Calypso returned home, leaving Odysseus to cut them. He quickly finished the task. He cut down twenty trees in all and smoothed them with the adze, squaring them perfectly like a master craftsman. Meanwhile, Calypso returned with some augers. Odysseus used them to bore holes and securely fastened the timbers together with bolts and rivets. He built the raft as broad as a skilled shipwright would make the hull of a large vessel. He fixed a deck on top of the framework, like ribs, and built a railing all around it. He also made a mast with a yardarm and a rudder to steer with. For protection against the waves, he fenced the raft all around with wicker hurdles. Then he loaded a quantity of wood onto it. Soon after, Calypso brought him some linen cloth for the sails. Odysseus crafted these excellently too, fastening them securely with ropes and sheets. Finally, with the help of levers, he pulled the raft into the water.

In four days, he had completed all the work. On the fifth day, Calypso washed him, gave him clean clothes, and sent him off from the island. She gave him a goatskin full of dark wine and a larger one filled with water. She also provided a pouch full of provisions and plenty of good meat. Moreover, Calypso made a fair and warm wind blow for him. Odysseus gladly spread his sail before it, sitting at the rudder and skillfully steering the raft. He never closed his eyes. He kept them fixed on the Pleiades, on late-setting Bootes, and on the Great Bear. People also call the Great Bear the Wagon; it circles in place, facing Orion, and never dips into the ocean's waters. Calypso had told him to keep this constellation to his left as he sailed. He sailed over the sea for seventeen days. On the eighteenth day, the faint mountain peaks of the nearest Phaeacian coast appeared, rising like a shield on the horizon.

But King Poseidon, returning from visiting the Ethiopians, spotted Odysseus from afar, from the mountains of the Solymi. Seeing him sailing on the sea made Poseidon furious. He shook his head and muttered to himself, "By the gods! It seems the gods have changed their minds about Odysseus while I was away in Ethiopia. Now he's nearing the land of the Phaeacians, where it's fated for him to escape the disasters that have struck him. But he'll still face plenty of hardship before it's over."

Thereupon, Poseidon gathered the clouds and gripped his trident. He stirred the sea with his trident, unleashing the fury of every wind. Earth, sea, and sky vanished behind clouds, and it seemed as if night itself burst forth from the heavens. Winds from all four directions—east, south, north, and west—struck him at once, raising colossal waves. Odysseus was plunged into despair. He cried out in despair to himself, "Oh, what will become of me? Calypso was right when she warned me I'd face trouble at sea before reaching home. It's all coming true. Look how Zeus darkens the sky with clouds, and how the winds from every direction whip up monstrous waves! Now I'm surely doomed to die. How blessed, how truly blessed, were the Danaans who fell before Troy fighting for the sons of Atreus! I wish I had died on that day when the Trojans fiercely attacked me over Achilles' body. Then I would have had a proper burial, and the Achaeans would have honored my name. But now, it seems I'm destined for the most miserable end."

As he spoke, a furious wave crashed over him. The raft reeled once more, and Odysseus was swept far out into the sea. He lost his grip on the rudder. The storm's power was so immense that the mast snapped in half, sending both the sail and yardarm tumbling into the sea. Odysseus was submerged for a long time. The clothes Calypso had given him weighed him down, and it was all he could do to fight his way back to the surface. But at last, he got his head above water and spat out the salty seawater streaming down his face. In spite of all this, he didn't lose sight of his raft. He swam as fast as he could, grabbed hold of it, and climbed back on board to avoid drowning. The sea tossed the raft around like autumn winds swirling thistle seeds on a road. It was as if winds from all four directions were playing battledore and shuttlecock with the raft.

When he was in this plight, Ino, daughter of Cadmus, also known as Leucothea, a goddess, saw him. She had once been a mortal woman, but was now a goddess of the sea. Seeing Odysseus's immense suffering, she felt pity for him. She rose from the waves like a seagull and settled on the raft.

"My poor soul," she said, "why is Poseidon so furiously angry with you? He's causing you much trouble, but for all his rage, he won't kill you. You seem sensible, so do as I tell you. Take off your clothes, let the raft drift with the wind, and swim to the Phaeacian coast, where better fortune awaits you. Here, take my veil and wrap it around your chest. It's enchanted, and you'll come to no harm as long as you wear it. As soon as you reach land, take it off, throw it as far as you can back into the sea, and then leave." After saying this, she took off her veil and gave it to Odysseus. Then, like a seagull, she plunged back into the water and vanished beneath the dark blue waves.

But Odysseus didn't know what to do. He wrestled with his thoughts, speaking to himself in despair, "Oh, this must be some god trying to trick me into ruin by telling me to abandon my raft. I won't do it, not yet. The land where she said I'd be free from all troubles still seems so far away. I know what I'll do. This will be best. No matter what, I'll cling to the raft as long as its timbers hold together. But if the sea smashes it to pieces, then I'll have no choice but to swim. I don't see how I can do any better than that."

While Odysseus was still wrestling with these thoughts, Poseidon sent a terrible, massive wave that seemed to rear up over his head before crashing down on the raft. The raft shattered into pieces, like dry chaff scattered by a whirlwind. Odysseus straddled a single plank, riding the waves as if on horseback. He then took off the clothes Calypso had given him, tied Ino's veil under his arms, and plunged into the sea, intending to swim to shore. King Poseidon watched him. He shook his head and muttered to himself, "Go on, swim your best until you reach those wealthy people. You won't be able to say I let you off too easily." With these words, he whipped his horses and drove off to Aegae, where his palace stood.

But Athena resolved to help Odysseus. She bound the paths of all the winds, except one, making them lie still. Then she stirred up a strong breeze from the North to calm the waters. This continued until Odysseus safely reached the land of the Phaeacians.

He drifted for two nights and two days on the sea. Death stared him in the face amidst the rough waves. But when the third day dawned, the wind died down, and a breathless calm settled. Carried by the swell, Odysseus eagerly looked ahead. He saw land quite close by. Just as children rejoice when their beloved father, long afflicted by illness, begins to recover with the gods' help, Odysseus was overwhelmed with gratitude to see land and trees again. He swam with all his strength, desperate to set foot on dry land once more. As he got close enough to hear the waves, he began to hear the surf crashing against the rocks like thunder. Huge waves still broke against the rocks with a tremendous roar. Everything was covered in spray. There were no harbors for ships, no shelter of any kind, only headlands, low-lying rocks, and mountain peaks.

Odysseus's heart now began to sink. He spoke to himself in despair, "Oh, Zeus! I had given up all hope, yet you let me swim so far and see land! But there's nowhere to land. The coast is rocky and battered by waves, with smooth rocks rising sheer from the sea. The water beneath them is deep, offering no foothold to climb out. I fear a giant wave will sweep me up as I try to leave the water, smashing me against the rocks. That would be a truly terrible landing. If I swim further, searching for a gentle beach or a harbor, a storm might drag me back out to sea against my will. Or the heavens might send some monstrous creature from the deep to attack me. Amphitrite breeds many such beasts, and I know Poseidon is furious with me."

As he hesitated, a giant wave crashed over him, slamming him against the rocks with furious force. If Athena hadn't shown him what to do, he would have been smashed and torn to pieces. He clung to the rock with both hands, groaning in pain, until the wave receded. He was saved in that moment. But soon, the wave surged back and dragged him far out into the sea. Just as the suckers of an octopus are torn when someone pulls it from a rock, bringing stones with it, the rocks ripped the skin from his strong hands. Then the wave pulled him deep underwater.

Poor Odysseus would surely have died here, despite his destiny, if Athena hadn't helped him keep his wits. He swam seaward again, beyond the reach of the crashing surf. At the same time, he kept scanning the shore for a haven or a headland that could break the waves at an angle. As he swam on, he eventually reached the mouth of a river. He thought this would be the best place, as there were no rocks, and it offered shelter from the wind. He felt a current and prayed silently, saying:

"Oh, King, whoever you may be, hear me. Save me from the anger of the sea god Poseidon. I approach you with fervent prayer. Anyone who has lost their way always has a right to appeal even to the gods. Therefore, in my distress, I surrender myself to your stream and cling to the knees of your river-spirit. Have mercy on me, King, for I declare myself your suppliant."

The river god stilled his stream and calmed the waves, making everything peaceful before Odysseus. He guided Odysseus safely into the river mouth. Here, at last, Odysseus's knees and strong hands gave out. The sea had completely broken him. His body was swollen, and seawater streamed from his mouth and nostrils like a river. He couldn't breathe or speak, collapsing in a swoon from sheer exhaustion. Presently, when he caught his breath and came to his senses, he took off the scarf Ino had given him and threw it back into the river's salty current. Ino received it into her hands from the wave that carried it to her. Odysseus then left the river, lay down among the rushes, and kissed the bountiful earth.

"Alas," he said to himself, disheartened, "what will become of me, and how will all this end? If I stay here on the riverbed through the long night, I'm so exhausted that the bitter cold and damp will surely kill me, for a keen wind will blow from the river at sunrise. On the other hand, if I climb the hillside, find shelter in the woods, and sleep in a thicket, I might escape the cold and get a good night's rest. But then some savage beast might attack and devour me."

In the end, he decided it was best to go to the woods. He found a forest on high ground, not far from the water. There, he crawled beneath two olive trees that grew from a single root. One was a wild shoot, and the other had been grafted. No matter how fierce the wind, how strong the sun, or how heavy the rain, nothing could penetrate the shelter they formed, for they grew so densely intertwined. Odysseus crawled beneath them and began to make a bed. There was a thick pile of dead leaves, enough to cover two or three people even in harsh winter weather. He was delighted to see this, so he lay down and heaped the leaves all around him. Just as a solitary country dweller, living far from neighbors, hides a glowing ember in the ashes to avoid having to start a new fire elsewhere, Odysseus covered himself with leaves. Then Athena poured sweet sleep over his eyes, closing his eyelids and making him forget all his sorrows.
