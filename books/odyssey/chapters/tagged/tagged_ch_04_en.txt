Book 4

Visiting King Menelaus: His Story and the Suitors in Ithaca

They reached the low-lying city of Lacedaemon and headed straight for King Menelaus's palace. They found King Menelaus hosting a grand feast with his many relatives. The king was celebrating the weddings of both his son and his daughter. His daughter was marrying the son of the brave warrior Achilles. Menelaus had promised her to Achilles' son during the Trojan War, and now the gods were making the marriage happen. So, he was sending her with chariots and horses to the city of the Myrmidons, where Achilles' son ruled. Meanwhile, his only son was set to marry the daughter of Alector from Sparta. This son, Megapenthes, had been born to him by a slave woman. The gods had not granted Helen any more children after she bore Hermione, who was as beautiful as golden Aphrodite herself.

Menelaus's neighbors and relatives were enjoying the feast, making merry throughout the palace. A bard sang and played his lyre, and two acrobats performed amazing tricks among the guests, moving to the bard's music.

Telemachus and Pisistratus, Nestor's son, stopped their horses at the palace gate. Menelaus's servant, Eteoneus, came out. As soon as he saw them, Eteoneus rushed back inside to tell his master. He approached the king and said, <others>"King Menelaus, two strangers have arrived. They look like sons of Zeus! What should we do? Should we stable their horses, or tell them to find lodging somewhere else?"</others>

King Menelaus grew very angry and said, <others>"Eteoneus, son of Boethous! You're usually not a fool, but now you're talking like a simpleton! Of course, stable their horses and bring the guests inside so they can have supper! You and I have stayed in countless homes before we finally returned here. May the gods grant us peaceful rest from now on!"</others>

Eteoneus hurried back and told the other servants to come with him. The servants unhitched the sweating horses from their yoke, tied them in the stalls, and gave them a mix of oats and barley. They leaned the chariot against the courtyard wall and then led the guests into the palace. Telemachus and Pisistratus were amazed by the palace's splendor. It shone like the sun and moon. After admiring everything to their hearts' content, they went to the bathhouse to wash.

Servants washed them and anointed them with fragrant oil, then brought them warm woolen cloaks and shirts. The two young men took their seats beside King Menelaus. A maidservant brought water in a beautiful golden ewer and poured it into a silver basin for them to wash their hands. She then placed a clean table next to them. A chief servant brought bread and offered them many delicious dishes from the palace. The carver brought plates of various meats and set golden cups beside them.

King Menelaus welcomed them, saying, <others>"Please, eat and make yourselves welcome. After supper, I'll ask who you are. The lineage of such fine men as yourselves cannot have been lost. You must be descendants of scepter-bearing kings. Poor people simply don't have sons like you."</others>

King Menelaus handed them a piece of rich, roasted loin, a prime cut that had been placed near him. They began to eat the delicious food set before them. After they had eaten and drunk their fill, Telemachus leaned close to Pisistratus, Nestor's son, and whispered so no one else could hear, <telemachus>"Pisistratus, my dear friend, look at the dazzling gleam of bronze and gold, amber, ivory, and silver! Everything is so magnificent, it's like seeing the palace of Olympian Zeus himself. I'm completely awestruck!"</telemachus>

King Menelaus overheard their conversation and said, <others>"My sons, no one can compare to Zeus. His palace and everything about him are immortal. Among mortal men, however, there might be someone as wealthy as I am, or there might not. In any case, I've traveled far and endured countless hardships. It took me almost eight years to return home with my fleet. I visited Cyprus, Phoenicia, and the lands of the Egyptians. I also went to the Ethiopians, the Sidonians, the Erembians, and to Libya, where lambs are born with horns, and sheep give birth three times a year. In that country, everyone, master or servant, had plenty of cheese, meat, and good milk, because the ewes produced milk all year round. But while I was traveling and accumulating great riches among these people, my brother was secretly and horribly murdered through the treachery of his wicked wife. So, I find no joy in being lord of all this wealth. Whoever your parents may be, they must have told you about all this, and about my great loss: the ruin of a stately, magnificently furnished mansion. I wish I had only a third of what I have now, if only I had stayed home, and all those who perished on the plains of Troy, far from Argos, were still alive. I often grieve for each and every one of them as I sit here in my palace. Sometimes I cry aloud in sorrow, but then I stop. Crying offers no comfort, and one quickly grows tired of it. Yet, more than all of them, I mourn for one man. Thinking of him makes me so miserable that I loathe both food and sleep. No one among all the Achaeans worked as hard or risked as much as he did. He gained nothing from it, and left me only a legacy of sorrow. He has been gone so long that we don't even know if he's alive or dead. His old father, his long-suffering wife Penelope, and his son Telemachus, whom he left as an infant in arms, are all plunged into deep grief because of him."</others>

As the king finished speaking, Telemachus's heart ached, thinking of his father. Tears streamed from his eyes at the mention of his father's name, and he covered his face with his cloak, using both hands. Seeing this, King Menelaus hesitated. He wondered whether to wait for Telemachus to speak on his own, or to ask him directly and learn everything at once.

While the king was still conflicted, Helen descended from her high, perfumed, arched room. She was as beautiful as the goddess Artemis herself. Adraste brought her a seat, and Alcippe brought a soft woolen rug. Phylo fetched her the silver work-box that Alcandra, wife of Polybus, had given to Helen. Polybus lived in Egyptian Thebes, the richest city in the entire world. He had given King Menelaus two pure silver baths, two tripods, and ten talents of gold. Besides these, his wife had given Helen beautiful gifts: a golden distaff and a silver work-box on wheels. A gold band encircled the top of the box. Phylo now placed this box beside Helen. It was full of finely spun yarn, with a distaff wrapped in violet-colored wool resting on top. Helen sat down, placed her feet on the footstool, and began to question her husband.

Helen spoke. <others>"Menelaus, do you know the names of these strangers who have come to visit us? I don't know if my guess is right or wrong, but I can't help saying what I think. I've never seen anyone, man or woman, so strikingly similar to another person. Honestly, every time I look at him, I'm not sure what to think. This young man looks so much like Telemachus, whom Odysseus left behind as a baby when you Achaeans went to Troy to fight because of my utterly shameless actions."</others>

King Menelaus replied, <others>"My dear wife, I see the likeness just as you do. His hands and feet are exactly like Odysseus's, and so are his hair, the shape of his head, and the look in his eyes. What's more, when I was speaking about Odysseus and how much he suffered because of me, tears fell from his eyes, and he covered his face with his cloak."</others>

Then Pisistratus spoke. <others>"King Menelaus, son of Atreus, you are correct in thinking this young man is Telemachus. However, he is very modest and hesitates to speak first to someone whose conversation is as divinely interesting as yours. My father, Nestor, sent me to escort him here. He wanted to know if you could offer him any counsel or suggestions. A son always faces difficulties at home when his father is gone and leaves him without supporters. This is exactly Telemachus's situation now. His father is absent, and there's no one among his own people to stand by him."</others>

King Menelaus said, <others>"Bless my heart! So, I am receiving a visit from the son of a very dear friend, one who suffered so much hardship for my sake. I had always hoped to honor him with the highest distinction once the gods granted us a safe return from across the seas. I would have founded a city for him in Argos and built him a house. I would have had him leave Ithaca with his possessions, his son, and all his people, and then conquered one of the neighboring cities under my rule to give to them. That way, we would have seen each other constantly, and nothing but death could have interrupted our close and happy friendship. As the king finished speaking, they all began to weep. Helen wept, Telemachus wept, and King Menelaus wept too. Pisistratus also couldn't hold back his tears, remembering his beloved brother Antilochus, whom the son of bright Dawn had killed. Then he spoke to King Menelaus.

<others>"Sir, my father Nestor always spoke of you at home as a man of rare and excellent wisdom. So, if it's possible, please do as I ask. I don't like to cry while I'm eating supper. We can only shave our heads for them and let the tears stream down our cheeks. I also had a brother who died at Troy. He was certainly not the worst man there. You must have known him, sir. King Menelaus answered, <others>"My friend, your good sense goes far beyond your years. It's clear you take after your father. Asphalion, one of the servants, poured water over their hands, and they began to enjoy the delicious food laid out before them.

Then Helen, daughter of Zeus, had another idea. She secretly added a special herb to the wine, an herb that made people forget all their worries, sadness, and bad moods. Anyone who drank this drugged wine couldn't shed a single tear for the rest of the day. This was true even if they saw their parents die right before their eyes, or watched a brother or son torn to pieces. This incredibly powerful medicine had been given to Helen by Polydamna, the wife of Thon, an Egyptian woman. Egypt was a land where all sorts of herbs grew, some used for medicine and others for poison. Plus, everyone in that country was an excellent doctor, for they were descendants of Paeon, the god of healing. After Helen put the herb into the bowl and told the servants to pour the wine, she began to speak.

Helen began, <others>"Lord Menelaus, son of Atreus, and my noble friends, sons of honorable men! Menelaus replied, <others>"My dear wife, everything you've said is true. I've traveled extensively and met countless heroes, but I've never encountered anyone quite like Odysseus. The maidservants went out with torches and made the beds. Soon after, a male servant led the strangers to their room. When the child of morning, rosy-fingered Dawn, appeared, Menelaus rose and dressed. He tied his sandals onto his fine feet, strapped his sword to his shoulder, and left his room looking like an immortal god. Then, he sat down next to Telemachus and spoke.

<others>"Telemachus,"</others> he asked, <others>"what has brought you on this long sea journey all the way to Lacedaemon? Is it for official business, or a personal matter? Tell me everything."</others>

Once they had gathered, Antinous, son of Eupeithes, spoke, his voice filled with rage. His heart burned black with fury, and sparks seemed to fly from his eyes.

<others>"By the gods, Telemachus's voyage is a serious problem!"</others> Antinous exclaimed. <others>"We were so sure it was nothing, but this young fool has slipped away right under our noses, and with a crew of elite sailors! He'll soon become a real headache for us. May mighty Zeus take him before he fully grows into a man! So, find a ship that can carry twenty men. I will hide and wait in the strait between Ithaca and Samos. Then he will bitterly regret the day he decided to go looking for news of his father!"</others>

When he finished speaking, the others applauded his words, and they all went inside the building.

It wasn't long before Penelope found out what the suitors were plotting. A servant named Medon had overheard them making their plans from outside in the courtyard. He immediately went to tell his mistress. As Medon stepped across the threshold of her room, Penelope said,

<others>"Medon, why have the suitors sent you here? Is it to tell the maids to abandon their master's work and prepare dinner for them? I wish they would never woo or dine here again, neither here nor anywhere else! This must be the very last time, seeing how all of you are wasting my son's estate. Didn't your fathers tell you when you were children how good Odysseus was to them? He never acted arrogantly or spoke harshly to anyone. Kings might sometimes say this or that, or favor one person and dislike another, but Odysseus never did an unjust thing to anyone. This just shows what wicked hearts you have, and how little gratitude is left in this world!"</others>

Then Medon said, <others>"Madam, I wish that were all, but they are plotting something far more dreadful now. May the heavens frustrate their design! They plan to murder young Telemachus on his way home from Pylos and Lacedaemon, where he went to seek news of his father."</others>

Penelope's heart sank within her, and for a long time, she couldn't speak. Her eyes filled with tears, and no words would come out. At last, she managed to say, <others>"Why did my son leave me? What business did he have sailing off in ships that make long voyages across the ocean, like sea-horses? Does he want to die without leaving anyone behind to carry on his name?"</others>

<others>"I don't know,"</others> Medon answered, <others>"whether some god urged him on, or maybe he went on his own to find out if his father was dead, or alive and on his way home."</others>

Medon went downstairs again, leaving Penelope in an agony of grief. There were many seats in the house, but she had no heart to sit on any of them. She could only throw herself onto the floor of her room and weep. Then all the maids in the house, both old and young, gathered around her and began to cry with her. Finally, overwhelmed with sorrow, she cried out,

<others>"My dears, heaven seems determined to give me more suffering than any other woman my age in this world! First, I lost my brave, lion-hearted husband. He possessed every good quality under the sun, and his name was known throughout all Hellas and central Argos. Now, my beloved son is at the mercy of the winds and waves, and I didn't hear a single word about him leaving home! You wretches! You all clearly knew he was leaving, yet not one of you thought to call me from my bed and tell me! If I had known he intended to take this voyage, I would have made him give it up, no matter how stubborn he was, or I would have died trying. One or the other! Now, some of you go and call old Dolius. He was given to me by my father when I married and is my gardener. Tell him to go at once and tell everything to Laertes. Perhaps Laertes can find a way to gain public sympathy on our side, against those who are trying to wipe out his own family and Odysseus's family."</others>

Then the dear old nurse Eurycleia said, <others>"Madam, you may kill me, or let me live on in your house, whichever you please, but I will tell you the truth. I knew all about it. I gave him everything he wanted, like bread and wine. However, young master made me swear a solemn oath that I wouldn't tell you anything for about ten or twelve days, unless you asked directly or happened to find out he had gone. He didn't want you to spoil your beauty by crying. So now, Madam, wash your face, change your dress, and go upstairs with your maids to offer prayers to Athena, daughter of shield-bearing Zeus. She can save him, even if he is at death's door. Do not trouble Laertes; he has enough worries already. Besides, I cannot believe the gods hate the family of Arceisius's son so much. Surely, a son will be left to follow him and inherit both the house and the beautiful fields all around it."</others>

With these words, the nurse made her mistress stop crying and wiped the tears from her eyes. Penelope washed her face, changed her dress, and went upstairs with her maids. She then put some crushed barley into a basket and began praying to Athena.

<others>"Hear me!"</others> she cried. <others>"Daughter of shield-bearing Zeus, tireless one! If ever Odysseus, while he was here, burned you the fat thigh bones of sheep or heifer, remember that favor now for my sake. Save my beloved son from the suitors' wickedness!"</others>

She cried aloud as she spoke, and the goddess heard her prayer.

Meanwhile, the suitors were making a loud commotion in the covered cloister. One of them said,

<others>"The queen is getting ready to marry one of us. Little does she dream that her son is now doomed to die!"</others>

They spoke these words, but they had no idea what was truly going to happen. Then Antinous said, <others>"Comrades, let's not talk so loudly. Our voices might carry inside. Let us quietly carry out the plan we all agreed upon."</others>

He then chose twenty men, and they went down to the ship by the seashore. They pulled the vessel into the water and loaded its mast and sails inside. They firmly tied the oars to the oarlocks with leather straps and hoisted the white sails high. Their loyal servants brought them their armor. They secured the ship a little way out from shore, then returned to the beach. They ate their supper and waited for night to fall.

Penelope lay in her upstairs room, unable to eat or drink. She worried constantly about her brave son. Would he escape the wicked suitors, or would they capture him? Like a lioness caught in a trap, surrounded by hunters, she thought and thought until sleep finally claimed her. She lay still on her bed, her mind and body at rest.

Then Athena had another idea. She created a phantom in the form of Iphthime, Penelope's sister. Iphthime was the daughter of Icarius, married to Eumelus, and lived in Pherae. Athena instructed the phantom to go to Odysseus's house and make Penelope stop crying. The phantom entered the room through the hole where the door-thong passed. It hovered above Penelope's head and spoke.

<others>"Penelope, you are asleep,"</others> the phantom said. <others>"The gods, who live in comfort, will not let you grieve and weep like this. Your son has done nothing wrong to them, so he will surely return to you."</others>

Penelope, who had been sweetly sleeping on the threshold of dreams, replied, <others>"Sister, what brings you here? You don't visit often, which is understandable since you live so far away. Are you saying I should stop crying and put an end to all these sad thoughts that torment me? I lost my husband, who was brave and strong as a lion. He possessed every good quality, and his name was renowned throughout Hellas and central Argos. Now, even my beloved son has sailed away. He's an inexperienced, foolish boy, not used to crowds or hardship. I worry more about my son than my husband. The thought of something happening to him, whether from the people he meets or the dangers of the sea, makes my whole body tremble. So many enemies are plotting to kill him before he can return home."</others>

Then the phantom said, <others>"Be brave, and don't despair too much. Someone many would wish to have by their side has gone with him. It is the goddess Athena herself. She pities you and sent me to deliver this message."</others>

Penelope asked again, <others>"If you are a god, or sent by a god, then tell me about that other unfortunate person, my husband. Is he still alive, or is he already dead and in the house of Hades?"</others>

The phantom replied, <others>"I cannot tell you for certain whether he is alive or dead. Let's stop this idle conversation."</others>

Then the phantom vanished through the door-thong hole and dissolved into thin air. But Penelope woke from her sleep feeling refreshed and comforted. Her dream had been so vivid.

Meanwhile, the suitors boarded their ships and sailed out to sea. Their plan was to murder Telemachus. In the middle of the channel between Ithaca and the island of Samos, there is a small rocky islet called Asteris. It has harbors on both sides where a ship can anchor. This is where the Achaeans set their ambush.
