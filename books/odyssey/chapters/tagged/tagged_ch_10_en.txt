AEOLUS, THE LAESTRYGONIANS, CIRCE

From there, we sailed to the island of Aeolus. He was the son of Hippotas, a favorite of the immortal gods. The island was like a floating fortress, surrounded by an iron wall. Aeolus had six strong sons and six daughters. He married them to each other. They all lived together with their parents, enjoying endless feasts and every luxury imaginable. All day long, the scent of roasting meats filled the air, making the whole house hum with activity. At night, they slept in their comfortable beds, each with their spouse. This was the home of the people we had now encountered.

Aeolus treated me with great hospitality for a whole month. He constantly asked about the Trojan War, the Argive fleet, and the Achaeans' journey home. I told him every detail. When it was time to leave, I asked him to help us on our way. He didn't hesitate. He gave me a sturdy ox-hide bag, filled with all the wild, roaring winds. Zeus had made him the master of the winds, so he could unleash or calm them as he pleased. He placed the bag on our ship and tied its opening tightly with a silver thread, so not even a whisper of a breeze could escape. Only the West Wind, which would guide us home, was allowed to blow freely. But all his efforts were in vain. We were lost because of our own foolishness.

We sailed for nine days and nine nights. On the tenth day, our homeland finally appeared on the horizon. We were so close we could even see the stubble fires burning in the fields. I was utterly exhausted and fell into a light sleep. I hadn't let go of the rudder for a moment, eager to reach home faster. While I slept, my crew began to whisper among themselves. They believed the bag Aeolus gave me was full of gold and silver. One sailor turned to his neighbor and said, <others>"Look at this man! He's welcomed everywhere he goes and makes friends so easily. Just look at all the valuable treasures he's bringing home from Troy! We've traveled just as far, but we're returning empty-handed. And now Aeolus has given him even more! Come on, let's open that bag and see what's inside, how much gold and silver it holds!"</others>

Their whispers turned into action, and evil advice won out over reason. They untied the bag, and the winds burst out, howling furiously and unleashing a terrible storm. The storm swept us, weeping, far out to sea, away from our homeland. I woke up, torn between throwing myself into the ocean or enduring this new disaster. But I chose to bear it. I huddled in the ship, while my men cried out in despair as the fierce winds drove our fleet back to Aeolus's island.

When we reached the island, we landed to get water and ate our meal by the ships. Right after dinner, I took a herald and one of my men and went straight to Aeolus's house. I found him feasting with his wife and family. We sat down on the doorstep, like beggars seeking help. They were shocked to see us. <others>"Odysseus,"</others> they exclaimed, <others>"what brings you back here? What god has tormented you? We went to great lengths to help you on your way home to Ithaca, or wherever you wished to go!"</others>

I answered them, my heart heavy with sorrow. <odysseus>"My men ruined me, along with that cruel sleep. Friends, please fix this misfortune. You can do it if you wish."</odysseus>

I pleaded as earnestly as I could, but they remained silent. Finally, their father, Aeolus, spoke. <others>"You most wretched of men, get out of this island at once! I will never help someone hated by the gods. Go! You have come here as one despised by heaven!"</others> With those harsh words, he drove me, heartbroken, from his door.

Heartbroken, we set sail again. With no wind to help us, my men grew exhausted from endless, fruitless rowing. For six days and nights, we struggled onward. On the seventh day, we reached Telepylus, the rocky fortress of Lamus, the city of the Laestrygonians. It was a strange place where the sun's rising and setting were so long or short that a shepherd driving in his flock would greet another driving his out. In that land, if you could stay awake, you could earn double wages, working as both a cattle herder and a shepherd, because they worked almost as much by night as by day.

When we reached the harbor, we found a bay enclosed by steep cliffs, with a narrow entrance between two headlands. My captains steered all their ships inside, mooring them close together, because the bay was always perfectly calm, without a single breath of wind. I, however, kept my own ship outside, tying it to a rock at the very tip of the headland. Then I climbed a tall rock to scout the area. I saw no signs of people or animals, only a faint wisp of smoke rising from the ground. So, I sent two of my men and an attendant to find out what kind of people lived there.

My men went ashore and followed a flat road that led from the mountains to the town, where people carried firewood. Soon, they met a young woman who was fetching water. She was the daughter of Antiphates, a Laestrygonian. She was on her way to the Artacia fountain, where the townspeople drew their water. My men approached her and asked about the king of the land and his people. She led them to her father's house. But when they arrived, they were horrified to see Antiphates' wife, a giantess as enormous as a mountain.

The giantess immediately called her husband, Antiphates, from the assembly place. As soon as he appeared, Antiphates began to kill my men. He snatched one up and devoured him on the spot for his dinner. The other two men ran back to the ships as fast as they could. But Antiphates shouted after them, and thousands of strong Laestrygonians appeared from everywhere. They were not men, but terrifying monsters! They hurled enormous rocks at us from the cliffs, as if they were mere pebbles. I heard the horrifying sound of ships crashing into each other and the death cries of my men. The Laestrygonians speared them like fish, carrying them home to eat. While they were slaughtering my men in the harbor, I drew my sword and cut the cable of my own ship. I shouted to my crew to row with all their might if they didn't want to suffer the same fate. They rowed for their lives. We were incredibly grateful when we reached the open sea, out of reach of the rocks they were throwing. Not a single other ship remained.

Grieving for our lost comrades, yet relieved to have escaped death, we continued our voyage. We arrived at the island of Aeaea, home to the great and cunning goddess Circe. Circe was the sister of the sorcerer Aeetes, both children of the Sun god and Perse, daughter of Oceanus. Some god guided us there, and we silently brought our ship into a safe harbor. Exhausted in body and mind, we stayed there for two days and two nights. On the morning of the third day, I took my spear and sword and left the ship to scout, hoping to find signs of people or hear voices. Climbing to the top of a high lookout point, I saw smoke rising from Circe's house in the middle of a dense forest. Seeing the smoke, I hesitated, wondering if I should go investigate immediately. But I decided it was best to return to the ship, feed my men, and send some of them instead of going myself.

As I was almost back to the ship, a god, pitying my loneliness, sent a magnificent antlered stag right into my path. It was coming down from its forest pasture to drink from the river, driven by the sun's heat. As it passed, I struck it in the middle of its back. The bronze spearhead pierced clean through its body, and it groaned in the dust before dying. I placed my foot on the stag, pulled my spear from the wound, and set it aside. Then I gathered rough grass and reeds, twisting them into a strong rope about a fathom long. With this, I tied the noble creature's four feet together. The stag was too large for me to carry on my shoulder with one hand, so I slung it around my neck and walked back to the ship, leaning on my spear. I dropped the stag in front of the ship and called my men, encouraging each one of them. <odysseus>"Friends,"</odysseus> I said, <odysseus>"Look here! We won't die before our time after all. And as long as we have food and drink on board, we won't starve."</odysseus> At my words, they uncovered their heads on the seashore and gazed at the stag with admiration, for it was truly a splendid animal. After they had admired it enough, they washed their hands and began to cook it for dinner.

We spent the entire day eating and drinking our fill until sunset. When the sun went down and darkness fell, we camped on the seashore. As rosy-fingered Dawn, child of morning, appeared, I called a council and spoke. <odysseus>"Friends,"</odysseus> I said, <odysseus>"We are in serious trouble. Listen to me. We have no idea where the sun rises or sets, so we don't even know east from west. I can't see a way out of this, but we must find one."</odysseus> I continued, <odysseus>"We are definitely on an island. This morning, I climbed as high as I could and saw the sea stretching all around us to the horizon. The island lies low, but towards the center, I saw smoke rising from a thick forest."</odysseus>

My words made their hearts sink. They remembered the terrible things that had happened with Antiphates the Laestrygonian and the cruel monster Polyphemus. They wept bitterly in despair, but crying wouldn't help. So, I divided them into two groups, assigning a captain to each. I put one group under Eurylochus's command and led the other myself. We cast lots in a helmet, and the lot fell to Eurylochus. So, he set off with his twenty-two men. They wept as they left, and we who remained behind also wept.

When they reached Circe's house, they found it built of finely cut stones, standing in the middle of the forest, visible from afar. Wild mountain wolves and lions roamed around it. These were poor, bewitched creatures that Circe had tamed with her magic and drugs, forcing them into submission. The animals didn't attack my men. Instead, they wagged their huge tails, fawned over them, and rubbed their noses affectionately. It was like hunting dogs greeting their master returning from dinner, knowing he'll bring them something. But my men were terrified by these strange beasts. Soon, they reached the gates of the goddess's house. As they stood there, they heard Circe inside, singing beautifully as she worked at her loom. The fabric she wove was so delicate, soft, and dazzling in color that only a goddess could have made it. Then Polites, whom I valued and trusted more than any other of my men, spoke. <others>"Someone inside is working at a loom and singing so beautifully. The whole house echoes with her voice. Let's call her and find out if she's a woman or a goddess."</others>

When they called, Circe came down, opened the door, and invited them in. My men, suspecting no harm, followed her inside. Only Eurylochus, sensing trouble, stayed outside. Circe led them into her house, seated them on benches and chairs, and offered them a meal mixed with cheese, honey, grain, and Pramnian wine. But she had secretly added wicked poisons to make them forget their homes. As soon as they drank it, Circe waved her wand, transforming them into pigs and herding them into her pigsties. They became pigs completely, from head to hair, grunting just like swine. Yet, astonishingly, their minds remained human, and they remembered everything vividly.

So, they were trapped in the pigsties, grunting, while Circe threw them acorns and beech nuts, the food of pigs. Meanwhile, Eurylochus rushed back to tell me about our comrades' tragic fate. He was so overwhelmed with despair that he tried to speak but couldn't find the words. His eyes filled with tears, and he could only sob and sigh. Finally, we pressed him until he managed to tell us what had happened to the others.

He said, <others>"We went through the forest just as you told us. In the middle of it, there was a beautiful stone house, easy to spot from a distance. Inside, we found a woman, perhaps even a goddess, sitting at her loom and singing a lovely song. My men called out to her, and she immediately came down, opened the door, and invited us in. The others followed her inside without a second thought, but I stayed put, sensing a trap. From that moment on, I never saw them again. Not a single one came out, no matter how long I waited."</others>

I slung my bronze sword over my shoulder and grabbed my bow. Then I told Eurylochus to come back with me and show me the way. But he grabbed me with both hands and pleaded, <others>"Captain, please don't force me to go with you. I'll stay here. You won't bring any of them back, and you won't even return alive yourself. It's better if the few of us left try to escape and save our lives!"</others>

I replied, <odysseus>"Then you stay here on the ship, eating and drinking. But I must go. I am bound by duty."</odysseus>

With that, I left the ship and headed inland. As I passed through the enchanted grove and neared the great house of the sorceress Circe, I met Hermes. He carried a golden wand and was disguised as a young man, in the prime of his youth and beauty, with the first down just appearing on his face. He approached me, took my hand, and said, <others>"My poor, unfortunate man, where are you going alone over this mountaintop, without knowing the way? Your men are trapped in Circe's pigsties, like wild boars in their dens. You surely don't think you can set them free, do you? Honestly, you'll never get back; you'll end up staying there with the rest of them. But don't worry. I will protect you and help you out of this trouble. Take this herb. It has great power, so keep it with you when you go to Circe's house. It will be a charm to protect you from all harm."</others>

<others>"I will also tell you about all the wicked magic Circe will try to use on you. She will mix a drink for you, and she will drug the food she uses to make it. Here's what you must do: When Circe strikes you with her wand, draw your sword and rush at her as if you mean to kill her. She will be frightened then and will want you to go to bed with her. As he spoke, he pulled the herb from the ground and showed me what it looked like. Its root was black, and its flower was as white as milk. The gods call this herb 'Moly.' Mortal men cannot uproot it, but the gods can do anything they wish.

Hermes then returned to high Olympus, flying over the wooded island. But I continued on to Circe's house, my heart heavy with worry as I walked. When I reached the gates, I stood there and called out to the goddess. As soon as she heard me, she came down, opened the door, and invited me in. I followed her inside, my mind troubled. She seated me on a richly decorated chair inlaid with silver, with a footstool beneath my feet. She mixed a drink in a golden goblet for me, but she had drugged it, intending to harm me. When she gave it to me, I drank it, but it didn't enchant me. Then she struck me with her wand. <others>"Now, off to the pigsty with you!"</others> she cried. <others>"Make your lair with the rest of them!"</others>

But I rushed at her with my sword drawn as if to kill her. She screamed and fell, clutching my knees, pleading, <others>"Who are you, and where do you come from? From what land and people have you arrived? How is it that my magic has no power over you? No man has ever been able to withstand even a taste of the herb I gave you, yet you are immune to spells! Surely you are none other than the brave hero Odysseus, whom Hermes always said would come here someday with his ship on his way home from Troy. So be it then. Sheathe your sword, and let us go to bed together, so we may become friends and trust each other."</others>

I replied, <odysseus>"Circe, how can you expect me to be friendly with you when you've just turned all my men into pigs? She immediately swore as I had told her. Once she had completed her oath, I went to bed with her.

Meanwhile, Circe's four handmaidens, nymphs born from the groves, fountains, and sacred waters flowing into the sea, began their work. One spread a beautiful purple cloth over a seat and laid a carpet beneath it. Another brought silver tables next to the seats and set them with golden baskets. A third mixed sweet wine with water in a silver bowl and placed golden cups on the tables. The fourth brought water and set a large cauldron to boil over a fire she had lit. When the water in the cauldron was boiling, she poured in cold water until it was just the right temperature for me. Then she seated me in a bath and began washing my head and shoulders from the cauldron, to wash away the weariness and stiffness from my limbs. After she had finished washing and anointing me with oil, she dressed me in a fine cloak and shirt and led me to a richly decorated seat inlaid with silver, with a footstool beneath my feet. A maidservant then brought water in a beautiful golden pitcher and poured it into a silver basin for me to wash my hands. She also drew a clean table beside me. Another servant brought bread and offered me many other foods from the house. Circe urged me to eat, but I would not. I sat there, ignoring the food before me, still gloomy and suspicious.

When Circe saw me sitting there, not eating and deep in sorrow, she came to me and said, <others>"Odysseus, why do you sit there like a mute, gnawing at your own heart, refusing both food and drink? Are you still suspicious of me? You shouldn't be, for I have already sworn a solemn oath that I will not harm you."</others>

I replied, <odysseus>"Circe, no man with any sense of what is right can think of eating or drinking in your house until you have set his friends free and let him see them. If you want me to eat and drink, you must free my men and bring them to me so I can see them with my own eyes."</odysseus>

As soon as I finished speaking, Circe took her wand, went straight through the courtyard, and opened the pigsty doors. My men came out, grunting like fat hogs, and stood looking at her. Circe walked among them, anointing each with a second drug. All the bristles that the first drug had given them fell off, and they turned back into men. They were younger than before, much taller, and better looking. They recognized me at once, each seizing my hand and weeping for joy until the whole house was filled with their joyful shouts. Even Circe felt pity for them. She came to me and said, <others>"Odysseus, noble son of Laertes, go back at once to the sea where you left your ship. First, pull it onto the land. Then, hide all your ship's gear and property in a cave, and come back here with your men."</others>

I agreed to Circe's words, so I returned to the seashore. There, I found the men still at the ship, weeping and wailing pitifully. When they saw me, the silly, blubbering fellows began frisking and running around me. It was like calves, after feeding all day, seeing their mother cows return to be milked, and joyfully leaping and lowing. Their cries echoed through the homestead. They seemed as glad to see me as if they had safely returned to their own rugged Ithaca, where they were born and raised. The affectionate men said, <others>"Captain, we are as glad to see you back as if we had safely returned home to Ithaca! But please, tell us all about the fate of our comrades."</others>

I spoke to them comfortingly and said, <odysseus>"We must pull our ship onto the land and hide its gear and all our property in a cave. Then, all of you come with me as fast as you can to Circe's house, where you will find your comrades enjoying abundant food and drink."</odysseus>

The men would have come with me at once, but Eurylochus tried to hold them back. He said, <others>"Alas, poor wretches that we are! What will become of us? When I heard him, I was torn, wondering whether to draw the sharp blade hanging by my sturdy thigh and cut off his head, even though he was a close relative of mine. But the men spoke up for him. With that, we all headed inland. Eurylochus didn't stay behind after all; he came along too, frightened by the severe scolding I had given him.

Meanwhile, Circe had seen to it that the men who had arrived earlier were washed and anointed with olive oil. She had also given them woolen cloaks and shirts. When we arrived, we found them all comfortably having dinner in her house. As soon as the men saw each other face to face and recognized one another, they wept for joy and cried aloud until the whole palace rang again. Circe finished speaking, and we readily agreed. We spent a whole year with Circe, feasting on endless meat and wine. But as the moons waxed and waned, a year passed. The long days returned.
