Book 21

The Axe Contest: Odysseus Reveals Himself to Eumaeus and Philoetius

Athena, the goddess, now planted a clever idea in Penelope's mind. She would challenge the suitors to a contest of skill with Odysseus's bow and iron axes, a plan designed to lead them to their doom. Penelope went upstairs and retrieved the storeroom key, a beautiful thing made of bronze with an ivory handle. Then, with her maidens, she headed to the storeroom at the far end of the house. This was where her husband's treasures were kept: gold, bronze, and finely crafted iron. His mighty bow was there too, along with a quiver full of deadly arrows.

This bow and these arrows had been a gift from Iphitus, son of Eurytus, a friend Odysseus met in Lacedaemon. They had crossed paths by chance in Messene, at the house of Ortilochus. Odysseus was there to collect a debt from the Messenians, who had stolen three hundred sheep from Ithaca and sailed away with them and their shepherds. Odysseus, still a young man, had traveled far to reclaim these sheep, sent on this mission by his father and the other chieftains. Iphitus was also there, trying to get back twelve lost brood mares and their mule foals. Sadly, these mares ultimately led to his death. He had visited the house of mighty Heracles, son of Zeus, a hero famous for his incredible feats. But Heracles, to his eternal shame, killed Iphitus even though he was a guest. He showed no fear of divine vengeance and no respect for the hospitality he had offered. He simply murdered Iphitus and kept the mares for himself. It was while trying to reclaim these mares that Iphitus met Odysseus. He gave Odysseus the bow that the great Eurytus had once used, a bow Eurytus had left to his son upon his death. In return, Odysseus gave Iphitus a sword and a spear. This exchange marked the beginning of a strong friendship between them, though they never had the chance to visit each other's homes. Heracles, son of Zeus, killed Iphitus before they could. Odysseus had not taken this special bow with him when he sailed for Troy. He used it while he was home, but he left it behind as a cherished keepsake from a dear friend.

Penelope soon reached the storeroom's oak threshold. A carpenter had carefully planed it smooth and drawn a straight line to ensure its perfect alignment before setting the doorposts and hanging the doors. She untied the strap from the door handle, inserted the key, and pushed it firmly to slide back the bolts. The doors swung open with a loud groan, like a bull bellowing in a meadow. Penelope stepped onto the raised platform where chests held fine linen and clothes, stored with fragrant herbs. From there, she took down the bow and its case from the peg where it hung. She sat, placing the bow on her knees, and wept bitterly as she drew it from its case. Once her tears had subsided, she carried the bow and its quiver, filled with many deadly arrows, to the cloister where the suitors waited. Her maidens followed, carrying a chest filled with iron and bronze, treasures her husband had won as prizes. When she reached the suitors, she stood beside one of the pillars supporting the cloister roof. A veil covered her face, and a maiden stood on either side of her. Then, she spoke.

<others>"Listen to me, suitors!"</others> Penelope declared. <others>"You have shamelessly abused the hospitality of this house for too long, simply because its master is absent, and with no other excuse than your desire to marry me. Well, here is the prize you've been fighting for! I will bring out Odysseus's mighty bow. Whichever one of you can most easily string it and shoot an arrow clean through the holes of twelve axes, him I will follow. I will leave this house, the home of my rightful husband, a house so noble and rich. But even then, I know I will miss it, even in my dreams."</others>

After Penelope finished speaking, she instructed Eumaeus to place the bow and the iron axes before the suitors. Eumaeus, tears streaming down his face, carried them out as she commanded. Beside him, Philoetius, the cowherd, also wept at the sight of his master's bow. But Antinous sharply rebuked them. <others>"You country bumpkins, you foolish simpletons!"</others> Antinous sneered. <others>"Why are you crying and adding to your mistress's sorrow? She already grieves enough over her lost husband. Sit down quietly and eat your dinner. If you want to cry, go outside, but leave the bow here. We suitors will have to compete for it with all our strength. Stringing this bow will be no easy task. None of us are as skilled with a bow as Odysseus was. I saw him myself when I was a child, and I remember him."</others>

Antinous said this, but secretly, he fully expected to string the bow and shoot through the iron axes himself. In truth, he would be the very first to taste an arrow from Odysseus's hand. He was dishonoring Odysseus in his own home, and encouraging the other suitors to do the same.

Then Telemachus spoke up. <telemachus>"Great heavens!"</telemachus> he exclaimed. <telemachus>"Zeus must have stolen my wits! My dear, excellent mother says she will leave this house and marry again, yet here I am, laughing and enjoying myself as if nothing is happening. But suitors, since this contest has been agreed upon, let it begin! This challenge is for a woman whose equal cannot be found in Pylos, Argos, Mycenae, or even here in Ithaca or on the mainland. You know this as well as I do; why should I need to praise my mother? Come on, then, no more excuses for delay. Let's see who can string the bow! I will try it myself. If I can string it and shoot through the iron axes, I will not allow my mother to leave this house with a stranger, not if I can claim the prizes my father won before me!"</telemachus>

As he finished speaking, Telemachus sprang from his seat, tossing aside his crimson cloak and drawing his sword from his shoulder. First, he lined up the axes in a neat row, placing them perfectly straight within a long groove he had dug. Then, he firmly packed the earth around them. Everyone was amazed to see him arrange the axes so orderly, especially since he had never seen such a thing done before. After this, he stepped onto the paved floor to try the bow. Three times he tugged with all his might, trying to draw the string, and three times he had to give up, even though he longed to string the bow and shoot through the iron axes. He was attempting it for the fourth time, and he would have strung it if Odysseus had not subtly signaled him to stop, despite Telemachus's eagerness. So, Telemachus spoke.

<telemachus>"Alas!"</telemachus> he sighed. <telemachus>"It seems I am either destined to be forever weak and without skill, or I am still too young, not yet strong enough to defend myself if attacked. So, you others, who are stronger than I, try this bow and settle this contest."</telemachus>

He then set the bow down, leaning it against the door that led into the house, with an arrow resting against its tip. He returned to his seat and sat down. Antinous then spoke.

<others>"Now, each of you take your turn,"</others> Antinous instructed. <others>"Go around to the right, in the same order the cupbearer serves the wine, and try to string the bow."</others>

The other suitors agreed. Leiodes, son of Oenops, was the first to rise. He was the suitors' sacrificial priest and sat in the corner near the mixing-bowl. He was also the only one among them who truly hated their wicked deeds and felt angry about their behavior. He was the first to take the bow and arrow, stepping onto the paved floor to try his luck. But he couldn't string the bow. His hands were weak and unaccustomed to hard labor, so they quickly grew tired.

Leiodes spoke to the suitors. <others>"My friends,"</others> he said, <others>"I cannot string this bow. Let someone else try. This bow will surely take the life and soul from many of our leaders. It's better to die than to live after failing to win the prize we've strived for so long, the prize that brought us all here. Some of us are still hoping and praying to marry Penelope. But once you've seen this bow and tried it, you'll be better off wooing another woman and offering her bridal gifts. Penelope will marry whoever makes her the best offer and is fated to win her."</others>

He set the bow down, leaning it against the door with an arrow resting against its tip. Then he returned to his seat. Antinous immediately rebuked him.

<others>"Leiodes, what nonsense are you spouting?"</others> Antinous demanded. <others>"Your words are outrageous and unbearable! It makes me furious just to listen to you. Are you saying this bow will take the lives of many of our leaders, simply because *you* can't bend it? True, you weren't born to be an archer, but there are others here who will string it soon enough!"</others>

Then Antinous turned to Melanthius, the goatherd. <others>"Hurry up,"</others> he ordered, <others>"light a fire in the courtyard and place a seat covered with a sheepskin nearby. Also, bring us a large lump of lard from the house. Let's warm the bow and grease it. Then we can try it again and finally finish this contest."</others>

Melanthius lit the fire and placed a sheepskin-covered seat beside it. He also brought a large lump of lard from the house. The suitors warmed the bow and tried again, but none of them were strong enough to string it. Still, Antinous and Eurymachus, the ringleaders and most prominent of the suitors, had yet to try.

Just then, the swineherd and the cowherd left the cloister together, and Odysseus followed them. Once they were outside the gates and in the outer yard, Odysseus spoke to them quietly.

<odysseus>"Cowherd,"</odysseus> Odysseus began, <odysseus>"and you, swineherd, I have something on my mind that I'm not sure whether to say or not. But I think I will. What kind of men would you be if some god suddenly brought Odysseus back here? Tell me, whose side would you take? The suitors', or Odysseus's?"</odysseus>

<others>"Father Zeus,"</others> the cowherd replied, <others>"I wish you could make that happen! If only some god would bring Odysseus back, you would see how fiercely I would fight for him with all my might!"</others>


While Eumaeus prayed to the gods for Odysseus's return, Odysseus saw their true hearts. He then said, <odysseus>"I am Odysseus. I have faced countless trials, but after twenty long years, I have finally returned to my homeland. Of all my servants, only you two are happy about my return. I haven't heard anyone else praying for me. So, I will tell only you two the truth. If the gods deliver the suitors into my hands, I will find wives for both of you. I will give you homes and land close to my own. You will be as dear to me as brothers and friends to Telemachus. Now, I will show you undeniable proof so you can recognize me and be sure. Look, this is the scar from the boar's tusk that tore me when I was hunting on Mount Parnassus with Autolycus's sons."</odysseus>

As he spoke, Odysseus pulled aside his ragged clothes, revealing the large scar. When they examined it closely, both men grabbed Odysseus and burst into tears. They hugged his head and shoulders, kissing him. Odysseus returned their affection, kissing their hands and faces. Their sorrow would have lasted until sunset if Odysseus hadn't stopped them. He said,

<odysseus>"Stop your weeping,"</odysseus> Odysseus commanded. <odysseus>"Someone might come outside, see us, and tell those inside. When you go in, go separately, not together. I will go first, and you two follow later. And here is our plan: The suitors will all try to stop me from getting hold of the bow and quiver. So, Eumaeus, when you are carrying the bow, place it directly into my hands. Tell the women to close the doors of their rooms. If they hear any sounds of fighting or shouting from inside the house, they must not come out. They must stay quiet and continue their work. And Philoetius, I charge you to firmly lock the gates of the outer courtyard and secure them at once."</odysseus>

After giving his orders, Odysseus returned to the house and sat back in his place. A short while later, his two servants followed him inside.

At that moment, Eurymachus held the bow. He tried to warm it by the fire to make it more flexible, but he still couldn't string it. He was deeply disappointed. With a heavy sigh, he said, <others>"I grieve for myself and for all of us. It's sad to give up on the marriage, but that's not the biggest problem; there are plenty of other women in Ithaca and elsewhere. What truly bothers me is that we are so much weaker than Odysseus that we can't even string his bow. This will bring shame upon us for generations to come."</others>

Antinous said, <others>"That's not true, Eurymachus, and you know it yourself. Today is a feast day throughout the land, honoring the god Apollo. Who could possibly string a bow on a day like this? Let's put the bow aside. The axes can stay where they are; no one is likely to come and take them. Let the cupbearer serve drinks so we can make our offerings to the gods, and let's forget about the bow for a while. Tomorrow, we'll tell Melanthius to bring us some of his best goats. Then we can offer the thigh bones to Apollo, the mighty archer, and try the bow again to finish this contest."</others>

The rest of the suitors agreed with his words. Then, servants poured water over the guests' hands. Pages filled the mixing bowls with wine and water, then served each man his drink after they had made their offerings to the gods. After they had made their offerings and drunk as much as they desired, Odysseus cleverly said,

<odysseus>"Suitors of the illustrious queen, please listen to what I have to say. I especially appeal to Eurymachus, and to Antinous, who just spoke so reasonably. Stop trying the bow for now, and leave this matter to the gods. Tomorrow morning, the gods will grant victory to whomever they wish. But for this moment, please give me the bow. I want to test the strength of my hands before all of you. I want to see if I still have as much power as I used to, or if my long travels and hardships have drained all my strength."</odysseus>

This made all the suitors very angry, for they feared Odysseus might actually string the bow. Antinous fiercely rebuked him, saying, <others>"You wretched creature, you don't have a shred of sense in your entire body! You should consider yourself lucky to have dined unharmed among your betters, receiving the same amount of food as us, and even hearing our conversations. No other beggar or stranger has been allowed to hear what we discuss. Clearly, the wine is ruining you, as it does all who drink too much. The Centaur Eurytion, when he was staying with King Peirithous of the Lapithae, also lost his mind because of wine. When the wine went to his head, he went mad and committed all sorts of evil deeds in King Peirithous's house. The heroes gathered there were furious; they rushed at him, cut off his ears and nostrils, and dragged him out the door. He left crazed, having lost his understanding, and paid the price for his crime. From that day on, war began between humans and centaurs, all because of his drunkenness. Likewise, I can tell you that it will go very badly for you if you string this bow. No one here will show you any mercy. We will immediately ship you off to King Echetus, who kills everyone who comes near him. You will never return alive. So, drink your wine and be quiet, without getting into a quarrel with men younger than yourself."</others>

Then Penelope spoke to him. <others>"Antinous,"</others> she said, <others>"it is not right for you to treat any guest of Telemachus who comes to this house so poorly. Even if this stranger proves strong enough to string Odysseus's mighty bow, do you truly believe he would take me home with him and make me his wife? The man himself wouldn't even dream of such a thing. So, none of you need to let that disturb your feasting. That's completely unreasonable."</others>

<others>"Queen Penelope,"</others> Eurymachus answered, <others>"we don't believe this man will take you away with him; that's impossible. But we are afraid that some of the common folk, men or women among the Achaeans, might go gossiping and say, 'Those suitors are truly pathetic! They courted the wife of a brave man, but not one of them could string his bow. Yet, a shabby wanderer who came to the house strung it at once and shot an arrow through the iron rings!' Such talk would bring great shame upon us."</others>

<others>"Eurymachus,"</others> Penelope answered, <others>"people who persist in eating up a great chieftain's estate and dishonoring his house should not expect others to think well of them. So why should you mind if people talk as you think they will? This stranger is strong and well-built, and he says he is of noble birth. Give him the bow, and let's see whether he can string it or not. I promise you, and it shall surely be, that if the god Apollo grants him the glory of stringing it, I will give him a sturdy cloak and tunic. I will also give him a spear to ward off dogs and robbers, and a sharp sword. I will give him sandals, and I will ensure he is safely sent wherever he wishes to go."</others>

Then Telemachus said, <telemachus>"Mother, I am the only one, either in Ithaca or in the islands opposite Elis, who has the right to decide who gets this bow or who doesn't. No one can force me one way or the other, not even if I choose to give the stranger the bow as a complete gift. So, Mother, go inside the house and busy yourself with your daily duties: your loom, your distaff, and the care of your servants. This bow is a man's matter, and above all, it is my matter, for I am the master of this house."</telemachus>

Penelope, amazed, returned into the house, taking her son's words to heart. Going upstairs with her handmaids into her room, she mourned her dear husband until the goddess Athena sent sweet sleep over her eyelids.

Now the swineherd Eumaeus picked up the bow, intending to take it to Odysseus. But the suitors clamored at him from all parts of the hall. One of them shouted, <others>"You idiot, where are you taking that bow? Are you out of your mind! If the god Apollo and the other gods grant our prayer, your own hunting dogs will drag you to some quiet place and tear you to death!"</others>

Eumaeus was frightened by their collective shouts and put the bow down right there. But Telemachus shouted at him from the other side of the hall, threatening him. <telemachus>"Uncle Eumaeus, ignore them and bring the bow! Even though I'm young, I'll pelt you with stones and drive you back to the countryside, for I am the better man of the two. I wish I were as much stronger than all the other suitors in this house as I am than you. Then I would quickly send some of them away, sick and sorry, for they are always plotting evil."</telemachus>

When Telemachus spoke like this, they all laughed heartily, which put them in a much better mood toward him. So Eumaeus brought the bow and placed it in Odysseus's hands. After this, he called Eurycleia aside and said to her, <others>"Eurycleia, young master Telemachus says you are to close the doors of the women's quarters. If you hear any groaning or commotion, as if men are fighting inside the house, you must not come out. You must stay quiet and continue your work where you are."</others>

Eurycleia did as she was told and closed the doors of the women's quarters.

Meanwhile, Philoetius quietly stepped outside and securely locked the outer courtyard gate. A papyrus rope lay by the entrance, and he used it to tie the gate shut. Then he came back inside and returned to his seat. He watched Odysseus closely. Odysseus held the bow in his hands, turning it over and over, carefully checking if worms had gnawed at its horns while he was away. Then one of the suitors turned to the person next to him and said, <others>"That old man must be an expert archer. Maybe he has a bow like that at home, or he wants to make one himself. The way that old wanderer handles the bow, he looks like a master craftsman."</others>

Another suitor said, <others>"I hope he has as little success in other things as he does in stringing this bow."</others>

Odysseus picked up the bow and examined it. Then, as easily as a skilled bard strings a new lyre and fastens the ends, he strung the bow. He drew the string with his right hand to test it. A beautiful sound, like the twittering of a swallow, rang out. Hearing it, all the suitors turned pale with shock. Just then, Zeus sent a loud clap of thunder as a sign. Odysseus rejoiced greatly in his heart at this good omen from Zeus.

He picked up an arrow that lay on the table. He placed it on the center of the bow. Still seated, he drew the arrow's nock and the string firmly towards himself. Once he had aimed, Odysseus shot the arrow. It pierced every one of the handle-holes of the axes, from the first to the last, and finally flew out into the outer courtyard. Odysseus said to Telemachus,

<odysseus>"Telemachus, your guest has not disgraced you. I didn't miss my aim, and I didn't take long to string the bow. I am still strong, not weak as the suitors mock me for being. Now, before the sun sets, it's time for the Achaeans to prepare supper. After that, we'll enjoy ourselves with song and dance, the crowning ornaments of any feast."</odysseus>

As he spoke, Odysseus made a sign with his eyebrows. Telemachus immediately strapped on his sword, grasped his spear, and stood armed beside his father's seat.
