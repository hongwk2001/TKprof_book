Book 18

The Fight with Irus — Odysseus Warns Amphinomus — Penelope Gets Presents from the Suitors — The Brazier — Odysseus Rebukes Eurymachus

There was a common beggar who wandered all over the city of Ithaca. He was infamous as an unstoppable glutton and drunkard. This man had no real strength or endurance, but he looked like a big, hulking fellow. His mother had named him Arnaeus, but the young men of the town called him Irus because he would run errands for anyone who asked. As soon as Irus saw Odysseus, he started insulting him and tried to drive him out of his own house.

Irus shouted, <others>"Hey, old man, get out of the doorway right now! Or I'll grab you by the neck and drag you out. Can't you see everyone winking at me, urging me to kick you out by force? I'm holding back because I don't want to. So get up and leave on your own, or we'll come to blows!"</others>

Odysseus glared at him and said, <odysseus>"My friend, I haven't done you any harm. People give you a lot, but I'm not envious. There's enough room in this doorway for both of us. You don't need to be stingy with things that aren't yours to give. You seem to be just another wanderer like me. Perhaps the gods will bring us better luck someday. But don't talk too much about fighting. If you make me angry, old as I am, I'll stain your mouth and chest with blood. Tomorrow will be more peaceful if I do, because you won't bother Odysseus's house again."</odysseus>

Irus, furious, shot back, <others>"You filthy glutton! You babble like an old fishwife. I have a good mind to grab you with both hands and knock out all your teeth like boar's tusks. So get ready. Let everyone here watch. You'll never be able to fight someone much younger than you!"</others>

They hurled insults at each other on the smooth floor in front of the doorway. Antinous saw this and burst out laughing. He turned to the other suitors. <others>"This is the best show we've ever seen! The gods have never sent such entertainment to this house. That stranger and Irus have quarreled and are going to fight. Let's make them fight right now!"</others>

The suitors all came up laughing and gathered around the two ragged beggars. Antinous said, <others>"Listen up! By the fire, we have some goat stomachs, stuffed with blood and fat, set aside for supper. The winner, the one who proves himself the better man, can choose one. He'll be welcome at our table, and we won't let any other beggar near this house."</others>

The others all agreed. But Odysseus, to avoid suspicion, said, <odysseus>"Gentlemen, an old man like me, worn out by hardship, cannot stand against a young man. But my unstoppable hunger drives me, even though I know I'll probably get beaten. You must swear, however, that none of you will give me a cowardly blow to help Irus win, or assist him in any way."</odysseus>

They swore as Odysseus asked. When they had completed their oath, Telemachus spoke up. <telemachus>"Honored stranger, if you want to fight this man, you don't need to fear anyone here. Anyone who strikes you will have to fight more than just you. I am the master of this house, and the wise lords Antinous and Eurymachus agree with me."</telemachus>

Everyone agreed. Odysseus tied his old rags tightly around his waist, revealing his strong thighs, broad chest and shoulders, and powerful arms. Just then, Athena came and made his body even more solid. The suitors were completely astonished. They turned to each other and whispered, <others>"That stranger just showed off thighs like that from under his rags! Irus is finished."</others>

Irus grew nervous when he heard them. But the servants dragged him by force to the center of the courtyard. He was so terrified his limbs shook. Antinous yelled at him, <others>"You boastful thug, you shouldn't have been born if you're scared of an old, worn-out beggar like him! So listen up: if he beats you and proves he's the better man, I'll put you on a ship to the mainland and send you to King Echetus. He kills everyone who comes near him. He'll cut off your nose and ears, rip out your guts, and feed them to the dogs!"</others>

This frightened Irus even more. But they brought him into the middle of the courtyard, and the two men prepared to fight. Odysseus wondered: should he hit Irus hard enough to kill him on the spot, or just lightly enough to knock him down? He decided a lighter blow was better, so the Achaeans wouldn't suspect his true identity. Then they began to fight. Irus struck Odysseus's right shoulder. But Odysseus landed a powerful blow on Irus's neck, just below the ear. The bones of his skull shattered, and blood gushed from his mouth. He fell to the dusty ground, groaning, gnashing his teeth, and kicking. The suitors clapped and roared with laughter. Odysseus grabbed Irus by the foot and dragged him to the gatehouse in the outer courtyard. He propped Irus against the wall and put a staff in his hand. Odysseus said, <odysseus>"Sit here and keep the dogs and pigs away. You're a pathetic wretch. Don't ever try to be the king of beggars again, or you'll face something much worse."</odysseus>

Then he threw his dirty, tattered old bag over his shoulder and went back to sit on the threshold. The suitors went back inside the hall, laughing and greeting him. They said, <others>"May Zeus and all the other gods grant you everything you wish, for getting rid of that annoying, greedy beggar! We'll soon take him to the mainland, to King Echetus, who kills everyone who comes near him."</others>

Odysseus considered this a good sign. Antinous placed a large goat's stomach, filled with blood and fat, in front of him. Amphinomus took two loaves of bread from the basket and brought them to Odysseus. He poured wine into a golden cup and offered it, raising a toast. Amphinomus said, <others>"Good luck to you, honored stranger. Your situation is difficult now, but I hope better days are coming soon."</others>

Odysseus replied, <odysseus>"Amphinomus, you seem like a wise man, and it's no surprise, given who your father is. I've heard good things about your father; he is Nisus of Dulichium, a brave and wealthy man. They tell me you are his son, and you appear to be an important person. Listen carefully to my words and remember them. Of all living things on Earth, humans are the most arrogant. As long as the gods grant him health and strength, a person believes no harm will ever come to him. Even when the blessed gods send sorrow, he endures it as he must, and makes the best of it, because the almighty god gives humans their daily thoughts, day by day. I know all about it, for I was a rich man once, and made many mistakes due to my stubborn pride and my confidence that my father and brothers would support me. Therefore, a person should always revere the gods in all things, and accept the good things heaven sends without arrogance. Look at the shameful acts these suitors are committing. They are wasting the wealth and dishonoring the wife of a man who is sure to return someday, and soon. Indeed, he will be here soon. May the gods send you home safely before that day, so you don't meet him when he returns. Because once he is here, he and the suitors will not part without bloodshed."</odysseus>

With these words, he poured a libation. After drinking, he placed the golden cup back in Amphinomus's hand. Amphinomus walked away, head bowed and looking serious, for he felt a sense of dread. But he couldn't escape his doom, for Athena had fated him to die by Telemachus's hand. So he returned to his seat.

Then Athena inspired Penelope to show herself to the suitors. This was to make the suitors even more captivated by her, and to earn greater respect from her son and husband. Penelope gave a wry smile and said, <others>"Eurynome, I've changed my mind. I want to show myself to the suitors, even though I despise them. I also want to hint to my son that he should avoid them. They speak sweetly, but their intentions are wicked."</others>

<others>"My dear lady,"</others> answered Eurynome, <others>"everything you've said is true. Go tell your son. But first, wash yourself and anoint your face with oil. Don't go around with tear-stained cheeks. It's not right to grieve so constantly. After all, Telemachus, whom you always prayed to see with a beard, is now a grown man."</others>

Penelope replied, <others>"I know you mean well, Eurynome, but don't try to persuade me to wash and anoint myself. The gods took away all my beauty the day my husband left. Still, tell Autonoe and Hippodamia that I need them. They must be with me when I'm in the hall. I can't go among the men alone; it wouldn't be proper."</others>

The old woman left the room to tell the maids to go to Queen Penelope. Meanwhile, the goddess Athena had another idea. She cast a sweet sleep over Penelope. Penelope lay on her couch, her body heavy with deep slumber. Then, Athena filled her with such grace and beauty that all the Achaeans would be amazed. She washed Penelope's face with a mysterious, radiant beauty, like Aphrodite when she dances with the Graces. Athena made her taller, gave her a more regal figure, and made her skin shine whiter than polished ivory. Once Athena had finished, she departed. Soon after, the maids entered from their quarters and woke Penelope with their chattering voices.

Penelope passed her hands over her face and said, <others>"What an incredibly sweet and wonderful sleep I've had, despite all my misery. I wish the goddess Artemis would let me die so peacefully right now. I don't want to wither away in despair any longer, grieving for my dear husband. He possessed every good quality and was the most outstanding man among the Achaeans."</others>

With these words, she came down from her upstairs room, not alone, but attended by two of her maidservants. When she reached the suitors, she stood by one of the pillars supporting the cloister roof, holding a veil before her face, with a respectable maidservant on either side of her. As the suitors beheld her, they were completely captivated and fell madly in love with her. Each desperately wished she would become his wife.

Penelope spoke to her son, Telemachus. <others>"Telemachus, I'm worried you're no longer as thoughtful and well-behaved as you used to be. When you were younger, you had better judgment. Now that you're grown up, though a stranger would take you for the son of a wealthy family, judging by your size and good looks, your conduct is far from what it should be. What was all that disturbance, and how could you allow a guest to be so shamefully abused? What would have happened if he had suffered serious injury while under our protection in this house? Surely, that would have brought great dishonor upon you."</others>

Telemachus replied, <telemachus>"Mother, I understand your unhappiness. I understand the whole situation and know when things are wrong, which I couldn't do when I was younger. However, I can't always act perfectly. These evil men here keep driving me out of my mind, one after another, and I have no one to stand by me. Still, the fight between Irus and the stranger didn't turn out the way the suitors wanted, because the stranger won. I wish Father Zeus, Athena, and Apollo would strike down every one of these suitors, some inside the house and some outside! I wish they would all be as helpless as Irus is over there by the outer gate. Look, he keeps nodding his head like a drunk. He was beaten so badly that he can't stand on his feet or even make it back to his home, wherever that may be, for he has no strength left."</telemachus>

They spoke for a moment. Then Eurymachus came up and said, <others>"Queen Penelope, daughter of Icarius, if all the Achaeans in Argos could see you at this moment, you would have even more suitors in your house by tomorrow morning. You are the most remarkable woman in the whole world, both for your personal beauty and your sharp mind."</others>

To this, Penelope replied, <others>"Eurymachus, the gods took away all my beauty, both of face and figure, when the Argives sailed for Troy and my dear husband went with them. If he were to return and look after my affairs, I would be more respected and present a better appearance to the world. As it is, I am weighed down with worry and the sorrows the gods have piled upon me. My husband predicted all of this. When he was leaving home, he took my right wrist in his hand and said, 'Wife, not all of us will return safely from Troy. The Trojans fight well with both bow and spear, and they are skilled at fighting from chariots. Nothing ends a battle faster than that. So, I don't know if the gods will send me back to you, or if I might fall there in Troy. In the meantime, you must look after things here. Take care of my father and mother as you do now, and even more so while I am gone. But when you see our son growing a beard, then marry whomever you wish, and leave this home you live in now.' That's what he said, and now it's all coming true. A night will come when I shall have to yield myself to a marriage I detest, for Zeus has stolen all hope of happiness from me. This further grief, moreover, tears my heart apart. You suitors are not courting me according to our country's traditions. When men court a noblewoman they believe will be a good wife, and each tries to win her for himself, they usually bring cattle and sheep to feast the lady's friends, and offer her splendid gifts. They don't just devour other people's wealth without paying for it."</others>

Odysseus was glad when he heard her trying to get presents from the suitors, and praising them with sweet words she didn't truly mean.

Then Antinous said, <others>"Queen Penelope, daughter of Icarius, take as many presents as you please from anyone who will give them to you. It's not right to refuse a gift. But we will not stop what we're doing or move from this spot until you have married the best man among us, whoever he may be."</others>

The other suitors cheered at what Antinous had said, and each sent his servant to bring his present. Antinous's servant returned with a large and lovely dress, beautifully embroidered. It came with twelve solid gold brooch pins to fasten it. Eurymachus immediately brought her a stunning necklace of gold and amber beads that shimmered like sunlight. Eurydamas's two servants returned with earrings shaped like three sparkling pendants that glittered brilliantly. Meanwhile, King Pisander, son of Polyctor, offered her a necklace of the finest craftsmanship, and all the other suitors brought her beautiful gifts of some kind.

Then the queen went back to her room upstairs, and her maids brought the presents after her. Meanwhile, the suitors took to singing and dancing, and stayed until evening came. They danced and sang until it grew dark. Then they brought in three braziers to give light, piled them high with old, dry chopped wood, and lit torches from them, which the maids took turns holding. Then Odysseus said:

<odysseus>"You maids of Odysseus, who has been gone so long, go inside to the queen. Sit with her and keep her company, or spin yarn, or sort wool. I will hold the light for everyone here. They may stay until morning, but they won't beat me, for I can endure a lot of pain."</odysseus>

The maids looked at one another and laughed. Then beautiful Melantho began to mock him with scorn. She was Dolius's daughter, but Penelope had raised her, giving her toys and caring for her as a child. Yet, despite all this, she had no sympathy for her mistress's sorrow and behaved improperly with Eurymachus, her lover.

Melantho said, <others>"You pathetic wanderer, have you lost your mind? Go and sleep in some blacksmith's shop or some public gathering place, instead of babbling here. Aren't you ashamed of speaking out in front of your betters—so many of them too? Are you drunk, or do you always talk so much? You seem to have gone crazy just because you beat that tramp Irus. Be careful that a better man than he doesn't come and club you over the head until you're bleeding and thrown out of this house!"</others>

<odysseus>"You hateful girl!"</odysseus> Odysseus replied, glaring at her. <odysseus>"I will go and tell Telemachus what you have been saying, and he will have you torn to pieces!"</odysseus>

With these words, he terrified the maids, and they hurried away into the inner parts of the house. They trembled from head to toe, for they thought he would do as he said. But Odysseus took his stand near the burning braziers, holding up torches and looking at the people—deep in thought about the events that were sure to unfold.

But Athena would not let the suitors for one moment stop their arrogance, for she wanted Odysseus to feel even greater rage toward them. She therefore prompted Eurymachus, son of Polybus, to mock Odysseus, which made the others laugh. Eurymachus said, <others>"Listen to me, you suitors of Queen Penelope, so I may speak my mind. It's no accident that this man has come to Odysseus's house. I believe the light isn't coming from the torches, but from his own head! He's completely bald, you see!"</others>

Then, turning to Odysseus, he said, <others>"Stranger, will you work as a servant if I send you to the fields and pay you well? Can you build a stone fence or plant trees? I will feed you all year round and provide you with shoes and clothes. Will you go, then? Probably not. You've fallen into bad habits. You don't want to work; you'd rather wander the country begging to fill your stomach."</others>

<odysseus>"Eurymachus,"</odysseus> answered Odysseus, <odysseus>"what if you and I were to compete in early summer, when the days are longest? Give me a good scythe, and take one yourself, and let's see which of us can last longer or mow more powerfully, from dawn until dusk, as the grass is ready for cutting. Or if you want to plow against me, let's each take a yoke of strong, well-matched tawny oxen, full of power and stamina. Turn me into a four-acre field, and let's see whether you or I can plow a straighter furrow. If, again, war were to break out today, give me a shield, a couple of spears, and a helmet that fits snugly on my head. Then you would find me leading the charge, and you would stop mocking me about my appetite. You are arrogant and cruel. You think you're a great man, but you live in a small, miserable world. If Odysseus comes to his own again, the doors of his house are wide, but they'll feel very narrow when you try to escape through them."</odysseus>

Eurymachus was enraged by these words. He glared at him and shouted, <others>"You miserable fool! I will soon make you pay for daring to say such things to me, especially in front of everyone! Are you drunk, or do you always babble like this? You seem to have lost your mind because you beat that tramp Irus!"</others> With this, he grabbed a footstool. But Odysseus feared for his life and clung to the knees of Amphinomus of Dulichium, begging for protection. The stool struck the cupbearer's right hand, knocking him down. The man screamed as he crashed onto his back, his wine jug clattering to the floor. The suitors in the covered hall erupted in chaos. One turned to his neighbor and muttered, <others>"I wish the stranger had gone somewhere else, curse him for all the trouble he's causing us! We can't have this kind of uproar over a beggar. If this bad advice continues, we won't enjoy our feast anymore."</others>

Telemachus stepped forward and said, <telemachus>"Sirs, what in the world are you doing? Have you lost your minds? Can't you enjoy your food and wine with some decency? Has some evil spirit possessed you? I don't want to kick anyone out, but you've all had your dinner. It's time for each of you to go home and get some sleep."</telemachus>

The suitors bit their lips, amazed by Telemachus's bold words. But Amphinomus, son of Nisus and grandson of Aretias, spoke up. <others>"Let's not take offense. Telemachus has a point, so there's no need to argue with him. And let's not harm the stranger or any of Odysseus's servants. Let the cupbearer pass around the wine for our offerings to the gods. Then we can all go home and rest. The stranger is Telemachus's guest, so let's leave him to Telemachus."</others>

Everyone liked what Amphinomus said. So Mulius of Dulichium, Amphinomus's servant, mixed wine and water in a large bowl and passed it around to each man. Then they poured out offerings to the blessed gods. After making their offerings and drinking as much as they wished, they all went home.
