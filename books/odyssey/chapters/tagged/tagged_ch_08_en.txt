Book 8

King Alcinous's Feast and the Games

As rosy-fingered Dawn appeared, King Alcinous and Odysseus rose from their seats. King Alcinous led the way to the Phaeacian assembly hall, which was located near the ships. Once there, they sat side by side on smooth stone benches. Meanwhile, Athena, disguised as one of King Alcinous's servants, went through the town. She wanted to help Odysseus return home. She approached the citizens, one by one, and spoke. <others>"Phaeacian elders and citizens, gather at the assembly! Come and hear the story of the stranger who has arrived at King Alcinous's palace after a long journey. He looks like an immortal god!"</others>

Athena's words made everyone eager to attend. They rushed to the assembly, filling every seat and even the standing room. Everyone was amazed by Odysseus's appearance. Athena had made his head and shoulders even more handsome. He looked taller and stronger than he actually was. She did this so he would make a great impression on the Phaeacians as a truly remarkable man. It would also help him succeed in the many skill challenges they were sure to offer. Once everyone had gathered, King Alcinous began to speak.

King Alcinous said, <others>"Phaeacian elders and citizens, listen to what I have to say. This stranger, no matter where he came from, east or west, has arrived at my palace. He needs an escort and wants this matter settled quickly. So, let's prepare an escort for him, just as we have for other guests before. In fact, no one who has ever stayed at my palace has complained that I didn't help them on their journey fast enough. Let's launch a ship into the sea, one that has never sailed before. We'll crew it with fifty-two of our cleverest young sailors. Once each of you has secured your oar to your seat, leave the ship and come to my palace to prepare a feast. I will provide everything you need. These instructions are for the young men who will be the crew. As for you, elders and citizens, please join me in the cloisters to entertain our guest. I won't accept any excuses. And we will summon Demodocus to sing for us. No bard is as skilled as he, no matter what story he chooses to tell."</others>

King Alcinous led the way, and the others followed. A servant went to fetch Demodocus. The fifty-two chosen young oarsmen headed to the seashore as instructed. Once there, they launched the ship into the water and set up its mast and sails. Next, they firmly tied the oars to the oarlocks with leather straps and hoisted the white sails high. They moored the ship a short distance from shore. Then they returned to land and went to King Alcinous's palace. The courtyards, outbuildings, and all the surrounding areas were packed with countless people, young and old. King Alcinous had twelve sheep, eight full-grown pigs, and two oxen slaughtered. The people prepared these animals, getting them ready for a magnificent feast.

Soon after, a servant brought in the famous bard, Demodocus. The Muse loved him deeply, but she had given him both blessings and burdens. She had blessed him with the divine gift of song, but she had also taken away his sight. Pontonous made a place for him among the guests, leaning a seat against a sturdy pillar. He hung Demodocus's lyre on a peg above his head and showed him where to reach for it. Next to him, a table was set with a basket of food and a cup of wine. He could drink whenever he wished.

The guests enjoyed the delicious food laid before them. After they had eaten and drunk their fill, the Muse inspired Demodocus to sing of heroic deeds. He sang a story that everyone was talking about at the time. It was about the fierce quarrel between Odysseus and Achilles, and the harsh words they exchanged while sitting together at a banquet. But Agamemnon was pleased to hear his chieftains arguing. Apollo had foretold this to him at Pytho when Agamemnon crossed the stone floor to consult the oracle. This was the beginning of the misfortune that, by Zeus's will, would befall both the Danaans and the Trojans.

As the bard sang, Odysseus pulled his purple cloak over his head, covering his face. He didn't want the Phaeacians to see him cry. When the bard finished singing, Odysseus wiped his tears and uncovered his face. He raised his cup and poured a drink offering to the gods. But when the Phaeacians, delighted by Demodocus's songs, urged him to sing more, Odysseus again pulled his cloak over his head and wept bitterly. No one noticed his deep sorrow. Only King Alcinous, sitting beside him, heard his heavy sighs. So, King Alcinous immediately said, <others>"Phaeacian elders and citizens, we have enjoyed the feast and the bard's songs enough. Now, let's move on to the athletic games! Our guest can then return home and tell his friends how much we surpass all other nations in boxing, wrestling, jumping, and running."</others>

With these words, King Alcinous led the way, and the others followed. A servant hung Demodocus's lyre on its peg and led him out of the cloister. The servant then guided him along the path where all the leading Phaeacian men were headed to watch the games. Thousands of people followed them, and many excellent athletes were ready to compete for the prizes. Among them were Acroneos, Ocyalus, Elatreus, Nauteus, Prymneus, Anchialus, Eretmeus, Ponteus, Proreus, Thoon, Anabesineus, and Amphialus, son of Polyneus, son of Tecton. There was also Euryalus, son of Naubolus, who looked like the war god Ares himself. He was the most handsome man among the Phaeacians, second only to Laodamas. King Alcinous's three sons, Laodamas, Halios, and Clytoneus, also joined the competition.

The foot races began first. The course was marked from the starting line. As everyone burst forward at once, a cloud of dust rose from the plain. Clytoneus won first place by a huge margin. He left the other competitors far behind, by a distance equal to the furrow two mules could plow in a fallow field. Next came the challenging wrestling matches, where Euryalus proved to be the strongest. Amphialus outshone everyone in jumping, and no one could match Elatreus in discus throwing. King Alcinous's son, Laodamas, was the best boxer. As everyone enjoyed the games, he spoke up. <others>"Let's ask the stranger if he's good at any of these sports. He looks incredibly strong. His thighs, calves, hands, and neck seem immensely powerful. He's not very old, but he's clearly been through a lot lately. Even the strongest person can be worn down by life at sea."</others>

Euryalus replied, <others>"Laodamas, you're absolutely right. Go and speak to our guest yourself."</others>

Hearing this, Laodamas walked into the middle of the crowd and spoke to Odysseus. <others>"Sir, if you are skilled in any sport, we hope you'll join our competitions. You must have competed in many before. There's nothing that brings a person more honor throughout their life than proving their strength and skill. So, please try something and cast away your sadness. Your journey home won't be delayed much longer. The ship is already in the water, and the crew is ready."</others>

Odysseus replied, <odysseus>"Laodamas, why do you mock me like this? My mind is filled with worries, not thoughts of contests. I've endured countless hardships, and now I've come to you as a humble guest. I pray that your king and people will help me return home."</odysseus>

Then Euryalus openly insulted him. <others>"So, I guess you're not skilled in any of the many sports people usually enjoy. Perhaps you're just one of those greedy traders who sail on ships as a captain or merchant. You probably only care about the cargo you carry out and the goods you bring back. You certainly don't look like an athlete."</others>

Odysseus replied fiercely, <odysseus>"Listen, rude man! You are truly arrogant. It's true that the gods don't give everyone the same gifts of speech, looks, and wisdom. One person might not be impressive to look at, but the heavens bless them with such wonderful speech that they charm everyone who hears them. Their gentle, wise words captivate listeners, making them a leader among their peers and respected wherever they go. Another might be as handsome as a god, but their good looks aren't matched with good sense. That's exactly your situation. No god could create a more handsome man than you, yet you are a fool. Your thoughtless words have made me incredibly angry. And you are completely mistaken. I am skilled in many athletic contests. In fact, when I was young and strong, I was one of the best athletes of my time. Now, however, I am worn out by hardship and sorrow. I've suffered greatly on battlefields and across the weary seas. Even so, your insults have stung me to the core, so I will gladly compete!"</odysseus>

Odysseus rushed forward without even taking off his cloak. He grabbed a discus, one much larger, heavier, and more massive than any the Phaeacians usually used. He swung it back with all his might and hurled it from his powerful hand. The discus whizzed through the air. As the discus soared gracefully from his hand, the Phaeacians flinched at its incredible speed. It flew much farther than any mark set before. The goddess Athena, disguised as a man, appeared and marked where the discus landed. Athena said, <others>"Sir, even a blind man could easily find your mark by feeling for it. It's so far beyond any other throw. You can rest easy about this contest, for no Phaeacian will come close to your discus throw."</others>

Odysseus was pleased to find he had a friend among the spectators. So he began to speak in a gentler tone. He said, <odysseus>"Young men, try to match that throw if you can! I can throw another discus just as heavy, or even heavier. If anyone wants to challenge me, step forward! I am still very angry. I'll box, wrestle, or run, I don't care what it is, against any of you—except Laodamas. I won't compete with Laodamas because I am his guest, and a person cannot compete against their own friend. At least, I don't think it's wise or sensible for a guest to challenge their host's family in any game, especially in a foreign land. Doing so would only harm themselves. But I make no exceptions for anyone else. I want to settle this and find out who is truly the best. I am skilled in every kind of athletic sport known to humankind. I am an excellent archer. In battle, no matter how many men stand beside me aiming their bows, I am always the first to strike down an enemy with my arrow. When we Achaeans trained before Troy, Philoctetes was the only one who could shoot better than me. I far surpass everyone else in the world who still walks this earth. However, I wouldn't want to compete with the mighty dead, like Heracles or Eurytus of Oechalia. These were men who could even challenge the gods themselves. In fact, Eurytus met an early end because he challenged Apollo in archery, and the god, angered, killed him. I can throw a spear farther than anyone else can shoot an arrow. Running is the only event where I fear some Phaeacians might beat me. This is because I've suffered so much at sea, and my provisions ran short, leaving me still weak."</odysseus>

Everyone fell silent except King Alcinous. The king began to speak. <others>"Sir, we have greatly enjoyed hearing everything you've told us. I understand you wish to show your skill because you were displeased by some rude remarks from one of our athletes. Those were words no polite person would ever say. I hope you understand what I mean. When you return home and dine with your family and chief men, please explain to them that we Phaeacians have a natural talent for all kinds of accomplishments. We aren't especially known for boxing or wrestling, but we are incredibly fast runners and excellent sailors. We absolutely love delicious dinners, music, and dancing. We also enjoy changing our clothes often, taking warm baths, and sleeping in comfortable beds. So now, please, let some of our best dancers begin to perform. Our guest can then return home and tell his friends how much we surpass all other nations as sailors, runners, dancers, and bards. Demodocus left his lyre at my palace, so one of you, go and fetch it for him."</others>

At this, a servant hurried to the king's palace to retrieve the lyre. Then, nine men chosen as stewards stepped forward. Their job was to manage everything related to the games. So they smoothed the ground and marked out a wide space for the dancers. Soon, the servant returned with Demodocus's lyre, and the bard took his place among them. Then, the town's best young dancers began to move their feet with such incredible speed and grace. Odysseus was utterly delighted by their joyful, twinkling steps.

Meanwhile, the bard began to sing the story of Ares and Aphrodite's love. He sang of how their secret affair started in Hephaestus's home. Ares gave Aphrodite countless gifts, dishonoring Hephaestus's marriage bed. The sun god, who saw everything, told Hephaestus what was happening. Hephaestus was furious when he heard the terrible news. He vowed revenge and headed to his forge. He set his huge anvil in place and began to craft chains that no one could ever undo or break. He wanted to trap them there, completely helpless. Once his trap was ready, Hephaestus went to his bedroom. He wrapped the bedposts all over with chains as fine as spiderwebs. He also hung many chains from the great beam in the ceiling. These chains were so thin and cleverly made that even the gods wouldn't notice them. After setting the chains all over the bed, Hephaestus pretended to leave for Lemnos, the city he loved most in the world. But Ares was watching closely. The moment he saw Hephaestus leave, Ares, burning with love for Aphrodite, rushed to his house.

Aphrodite had just returned from visiting her father, Zeus, and was about to sit down. Just then, Ares entered the house. He took her hand and said, <others>"Let's go to Hephaestus's bed. He's not home. He's gone off to Lemnos, among the Sintians, whose language is strange."</others>

Aphrodite didn't hesitate at all. They went to the bed to rest, but they were caught in the trap cunning Hephaestus had set for them. They couldn't get up or move a muscle. They realized too late that they were caught. Just then, Hephaestus appeared before them. He had turned back before even reaching Lemnos, because the sun god, his spy, had told him everything that was happening. Hephaestus was in a raging fury. He stood in the doorway, shouting terrible words to all the gods.

<others>"Father Zeus, and all you other blessed gods who live forever!" he cried. "Come here and see the ridiculous and shameful sight I will show you! Zeus's daughter, Aphrodite, always dishonors me because I am lame. She loves Ares, who is handsome and strong, while I am a cripple. But that's not my fault; it's my parents' fault. They should never have had me! Look at these two together on my bed. It makes me furious to see them. They are very fond of each other, but I don't think they'll lie there much longer, nor will they get much sleep. They'll stay trapped there until her father repays me the money I gave him for his daughter, who is beautiful but dishonest baggage!"</others>

At his words, the gods gathered at Hephaestus's house. Poseidon, who encircles the earth, Hermes, the bringer of luck, and King Apollo all came. But the goddesses all stayed home out of shame. Then, the gods who give all good things stood in the doorway. They saw cunning Hephaestus's handiwork and burst into uncontrollable laughter. They turned to each other and said,

<others>"Bad deeds never turn out well," they said. "And the weak can outsmart the strong. Look how limping Hephaestus, lame as he is, caught Ares, the fastest god in heaven! Now Ares will have to pay a huge penalty."</others>

While the gods were talking like this, King Apollo said to Hermes, <others>"Messenger Hermes, giver of good things, you wouldn't care how strong the chains were, would you, if you could sleep with Aphrodite?"</others>

<others>"King Apollo," Hermes answered, "I only wish I could get that chance, even if there were three times as many chains! And even if all of you, gods and goddesses, were watching, I would still sleep with her if I could."</others>

The immortal gods burst out laughing when they heard him. But Poseidon took it all seriously. He kept imploring Hephaestus to set Ares free. "Let him go!" he cried. "I promise, as you require, that he will pay you all the damages considered reasonable among the immortal gods."

<others>"Don't," Hephaestus replied. "Don't ask me to do this. A bad man's promise is bad security. What could I do to hold you accountable if Ares were to escape and leave his debts behind, along with his chains?"</others>

Poseidon said to Hephaestus, <others>"If Ares goes away without paying his damages, I will pay you myself."</others>

Hephaestus answered, <others>"In that case, I cannot and must not refuse you."</others>

Hephaestus then loosened the bonds that held them. As soon as they were free, they quickly ran off. Ares headed to Thrace, and laughter-loving Aphrodite went to Cyprus and Paphos. There, her sacred grove and altar, fragrant with offerings, were located. The Charites bathed Aphrodite there and anointed her with ambrosia oil, the kind the immortal gods use. Then they dressed her in the most enchanting and beautiful clothes.

The bard sang this, and both Odysseus and the seafaring Phaeacians were captivated as they listened.

Then King Alcinous told Laodamas and Halius to dance alone, because no one could compete with them. They took a red ball that Polybus had made for them. One of them bent backward and threw it high into the sky, while the other jumped from the ground and easily caught it before it fell. After throwing the ball straight up, they began to dance. At the same time, they kept throwing it back and forth to each other. All the young men in the circle clapped and stomped their feet, cheering loudly.

Then Odysseus said, <odysseus>"King Alcinous, you said your people were the nimblest dancers in the world, and indeed, they have proven it! I was astonished as I watched them."</odysseus>

The king was delighted by this and exclaimed to the Phaeacians, <others>"Elders and citizens, our guest seems to be a person of remarkable judgment! Let's give him the kind of hospitality he can reasonably expect. There are twelve chief men among you, and counting myself, that makes thirteen. Each of you, contribute a clean cloak, a shirt, and a talent of fine gold. Let's give him all of this at once, so he can enjoy his supper with a light heart. As for Euryalus, he was rude, so he will have to make a formal apology and give a gift as well."</others>

As he spoke, everyone applauded his words and sent their servants to fetch the presents.

Then Euryalus said, <others>"King Alcinous, I will give the stranger all the satisfaction you require. He shall have my sword. Its blade is bronze, and only the hilt is silver. I will also give him its scabbard, made of newly carved ivory, which fits it perfectly. It will be a very valuable gift for him."</others>

As he spoke, he placed a sword in Odysseus's hand. <others>"Stranger," he said, "I wish you good fortune. If I've said anything wrong, may the winds carry my words far away. May the heavens grant you a safe return home. I've heard you've been away for a long time and suffered many hardships."</others>

Odysseus replied, <odysseus>"Friend, I wish you good fortune as well. May the gods grant you every happiness. And may you never regret giving me this sword along with your apology."</odysseus>

As Odysseus spoke, he slung the sword over his shoulder. As the sun began to set, gifts started to appear one by one, as servants of those who had given them kept bringing them to King Alcinous's house. The king's sons received them and entrusted them to their mother. Then King Alcinous led the way into the house and invited his guests to sit.

King Alcinous turned to Queen Arete and said, <others>"My dear, please bring our best chest and put a clean cloak and shirt inside. Also, put a cauldron on the fire to heat water so our guest can have a warm bath. And carefully pack all the gifts the noble Phaeacians have given him. That way, he'll enjoy his supper and the singing that follows even more. I will personally give him this golden goblet. It's exquisitely crafted, so he'll remember me for the rest of his life whenever he pours a drink offering to Zeus or any of the other gods."</others>

Queen Arete told her maids to quickly place a large tripod cauldron on the fire. The maids then placed the cauldron, full of bath water, onto the blazing fire. They threw in more wood to make the fire burn brighter, and as the flames licked the cauldron's base, the water quickly grew hot. Meanwhile, Queen Arete brought a magnificent chest from her own room and carefully packed all the beautiful gifts of gold and clothing the Phaeacians had brought. Finally, she added a cloak and a fine shirt from King Alcinous, and then she spoke to Odysseus.

<others>"Close the lid yourself and tie it securely with a cord," she said. "Someone might try to steal your belongings while you're asleep on the ship."</others>

Hearing this, Odysseus closed the chest lid and securely tied it with a knot Circe had taught him. As soon as he finished, a chief servant told him it was time for his bath. He was overjoyed to wash himself in warm water, for no one had attended to him since he left Calypso's island. Calypso had cared for him like a god while he was with her. After the servants washed and anointed him with oil, and gave him a clean cloak and shirt, he left the bath and joined the guests who were enjoying their wine. Beautiful Nausicaa stood by one of the pillars supporting the cloister roof, watching him pass with admiration. Nausicaa said, <others>"Farewell, stranger. When you are safely home, please don't forget me. Remember that I was the first to help save your life."</others>

Odysseus replied, <odysseus>"Nausicaa, daughter of great King Alcinous, may Zeus, the mighty husband of Hera, grant that I may reach my home safely. If that happens, I will bless you as my guardian angel all my days, for it was you who saved me."</odysseus>

After saying this, Odysseus sat down beside King Alcinous. Supper was then served, and wine was prepared for drinking. A servant led in Demodocus, the king's favorite bard, and seated him in the middle of the company, near a pillar supporting the cloister, so he could lean comfortably against it. Then Odysseus cut off a piece of roast pork, rich with fat, from the joint. He said to a servant, <odysseus>"Take this piece of pork to Demodocus and tell him to eat it. Even though his songs may cause me pain, I will still honor him. Bards are honored and respected everywhere in the world, for the Muses teach them their songs and love them."</odysseus>

The servant carried the pork with his fingers to Demodocus, who took it and was very pleased. They then began to enjoy the delicious food laid before them. As soon as they had finished eating and drinking, Odysseus spoke to Demodocus. <odysseus>"Demodocus, there is no one in the world I admire more than you. You must have learned from the Muses, daughters of Zeus, and from Apollo himself. How can you sing so accurately of the Achaeans' return, with all their sufferings and adventures? If you weren't there yourself, you must have heard it all from someone who was. Now, please change your song and tell us about the wooden horse that Epeus built with Athena's help. Tell us how Odysseus got that horse, filled with soldiers, inside the fortress of Troy, and how those soldiers later sacked the city. If you sing this tale correctly, I will tell everyone how magnificently the gods have gifted you."</odysseus>

The god-inspired bard began his tale at the moment some of the Argives set fire to their tents and sailed away. Meanwhile, others, hidden inside the wooden horse, waited with Odysseus in the Trojan assembly place. The Trojans themselves dragged the horse into their fortress, where it stood. The Trojans gathered around it in council, but they were divided into three opinions about what to do. Some wanted to break it apart right then and there. Others suggested dragging it to the top of the rock where the fortress stood and throwing it down the cliff. Still others argued for leaving it as an offering to the gods. In the end, they followed the last suggestion. But when they brought that horse into the city, Troy was already doomed. Inside were all the bravest Argive warriors, waiting to bring death and destruction upon the Trojans. He then sang how the Achaean heroes poured out of the horse, ambushed the city, and sacked it. He sang of them sweeping through the city, destroying everything, and how Odysseus, raging like Ares, rushed with Menelaus to the house of Deiphobus. The fiercest battle raged there, but with Athena's help, Odysseus was victorious.

As the bard told all these tales, Odysseus was overcome with emotion, and his cheeks were wet with tears. He wept like a woman who throws herself on the body of her husband, fallen bravely defending his city and people. She screams and wraps her arms around her dying husband, gasping for breath, but her enemies beat her back and shoulders from behind, dragging her away into slavery. Her life becomes one of labor and sorrow, and the beauty fades from her face. Odysseus wept just as piteously. But no one present noticed his tears. Only King Alcinous, sitting beside him, could hear his sobs and sighs. So the king immediately rose and said,

<others>"Elders and councilors of Phaeacia, let Demodocus stop his song. There is someone here who doesn't seem to enjoy it. Ever since we finished supper and Demodocus began to sing, our guest has been groaning and lamenting. He is clearly in great distress, so the bard must stop. That way, we can all enjoy ourselves, both hosts and guest. This is how it should be. All this hospitality and these gifts, which we offer with such good will, are entirely for his honor. Anyone with a decent heart knows that a guest and a suppliant should be treated like a brother."</others>

<others>"So, guest, please don't hide or hesitate any longer about what I'm about to ask you. It would be more polite for you to give me a straightforward answer. Tell me the name your father and mother called you back home, and the name you were known by among your neighbors and fellow citizens. No one, rich or poor, is entirely without a name, for parents always give their children names as soon as they are born.

"Also, tell me your homeland, your country, and your city. Then our ships can set their course accordingly and take you there. We Phaeacians have no pilots, and our vessels have no rudders like those of other nations. Instead, our ships understand our thoughts and desires on their own. They know all the cities and countries in the world and can navigate skillfully even when the sea is covered with mist and clouds. There's no danger of them being wrecked or coming to any harm. However, I remember my father saying that Poseidon was angry with us for being too eager to escort people. He said that one day Poseidon would wreck one of our ships as it returned from an escort mission and bury our city under a high mountain. That's what my father always used to say, but whether the god will carry out his threat is something he will decide for himself.

"Now, tell me everything truthfully. Where have you wandered, and what countries have you traveled through? Tell us about the people and their cities. Were they hostile, savage, and uncivilized, or were they kind and humane? Also, tell us why you are so unhappy hearing about the return of the Argive Danaans from Troy. The gods planned all this, sending them misfortunes so that future generations would have stories to sing about. Did you lose a brave kinsman of your wife's during the Trojan War? Perhaps a son-in-law or father-in-law, who are the closest relations a man has outside his own blood. Or was it a brave and kindly comrade? A good friend is as dear to a man as his own brother."</others>
