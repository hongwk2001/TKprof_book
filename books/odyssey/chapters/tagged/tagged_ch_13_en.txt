Book 13

Odysseus Leaves Scheria and Returns to Ithaca

Odysseus finished his incredible tale, and the entire hall, filled with people, fell silent. They were utterly spellbound, hanging on his every word. After a moment, King Alcinous broke the quiet.

King Alcinous said, "<others>Odysseus, now that you've reached my home, I'm sure you'll return to your own without any more trouble, no matter how much you've suffered. But to the rest of you, who come here every night to drink my finest wine and listen to my bard, I have something to say. Our guest has already gathered all the clothes, crafted gold, and other precious items you brought for him. So now, let each of us give him an additional gift: a large tripod and a cauldron. We'll cover the cost together, sharing the burden. It wouldn't be fair to expect individuals to bear the weight of such generous gifts alone, would it?</others>"

Everyone agreed to the plan, and they all went home to bed. When rosy-fingered Dawn, child of morning, appeared, they rushed to the ship with their cauldrons. King Alcinous boarded the ship and made sure all the items were securely fastened beneath the benches, so nothing would come loose and hurt the rowers. Afterward, they went to King Alcinous's house for dinner. The king sacrificed a bull to honor Zeus, the lord of all. They grilled the meat and enjoyed a wonderful dinner, while Demodocus, the beloved and inspired bard, sang for them. But Odysseus kept glancing at the sun, as if trying to hurry its setting. He desperately longed for the time to leave. Just as a farmer, who has spent all day plowing a fallow field with two oxen, thinks of supper and rejoices when night falls, knowing he can finally go home because his legs can barely carry him, so too did Odysseus rejoice the moment the sun went down. He immediately spoke to the Phaeacians, addressing King Alcinous in particular.

<odysseus>"My King, and all of you here, farewell. Please pour your drink-offerings and send me off with joy. You have fulfilled my heart's desire by giving me an escort and many gifts. May the heavens grant that I put all these to good use. I hope to find my wonderful wife living peacefully among friends. And may you, whom I leave behind, bring joy to your wives and children. May the heavens grant you every good blessing, and may no evil ever befall your people."</odysseus>

When Odysseus finished speaking, everyone who heard him agreed that he had spoken wisely and consented to his escort. So King Alcinous said to his servant, "<others>Pontonous, mix some wine and pass it around to everyone. We will offer a prayer to Father Zeus and bless our guest on his journey.</others>"

Pontonous mixed the wine and passed it to everyone in turn. From their seats, the others poured drink-offerings to the blessed gods who live in the heavens. But Odysseus stood up and placed a two-handled cup into Queen Arete's hands.

Odysseus said, "<odysseus>Farewell, Queen. May you live in peace from this day forward, forever, until old age and death—the common fate of all humankind—claim you. I am leaving now. May you be happy in this house with your children, your people, and King Alcinous.</odysseus>"

As soon as he finished speaking, he stepped across the threshold. King Alcinous sent a man to lead him to his ship and the seashore. Queen Arete also sent some maidservants with him. One maidservant carried a clean shirt and cloak, another his sturdy strongbox, and a third brought grain and wine. When they reached the water's edge, the crew loaded these items, along with all the meat and drink, onto the ship. For Odysseus, they spread a rug and a linen sheet on the deck in the stern of the ship, so he could sleep soundly. Odysseus also boarded and lay down without a word. The crew took their places and untied the hawser from the pierced stone that had held the ship. As they began to row out to sea, Odysseus fell into a deep, sweet slumber, almost like death itself.

The ship shot forward like a four-horse chariot driven by a whip. Its prow arched like the neck of a powerful stallion, and a great wave of dark blue water foamed violently in its wake. The ship held its course steadily; even a falcon, the swiftest of all birds, couldn't have kept up with it. So the ship cut through the water. Inside, lay Odysseus, a man as cunning as the gods themselves. He slept peacefully, completely forgetting all the pain he had endured on the battlefield and all the trials he had faced on the weary sea.

As the bright star heralding dawn appeared, the ship drew near to land. On Ithaca, there was a harbor belonging to the old sea god Phorcys. It lay nestled between two headlands that jutted out into the sea, enclosing the harbor. These headlands protected the harbor from the fierce winds and waves outside, so once inside, a ship could anchor safely without even needing to be tied up. At the head of this harbor stood a large olive tree, and nearby was a beautiful, arched cavern. This cave was sacred to the Naiad nymphs. Inside the cave were stone mixing bowls and wine jars, and bees made their hives there. There were also huge stone looms where the nymphs wove their sea-purple robes—a truly wondrous sight! And clear water always flowed within the cave. It had two entrances. Mortals could enter the cave through the north entrance, but the south entrance was far more mysterious. Humans couldn't possibly get in that way; it was a path only for the gods.

Since they knew this place well, they steered the ship into the harbor. The ship came in so fast that half its length ran aground on the shore. As soon as they landed, their first task was to lift Odysseus, still wrapped in his rug and linen sheet, out of the ship. They gently laid him on the sand, still deeply asleep. Then they unloaded the gifts that Athena had persuaded the Phaeacians to give Odysseus. They gathered all the gifts together by the root of the olive tree, away from the road, fearing that some passerby might steal them before Odysseus awoke. Once everything was done, the Phaeacians quickly headed back home.

But Poseidon had not forgotten the threats he had made against Odysseus. He sought counsel from Zeus.

<others>"Father Zeus,"</others> Poseidon said, "<others>If mortals like the Phaeacians, who are my own people, show such little regard for me, then I will no longer be respected among you gods. I said I would let Odysseus return home after he had suffered enough. I never said he would never get home at all, for I knew you had already nodded your head and promised he would. But now the Phaeacians have brought him in a ship, fast asleep, and landed him on Ithaca. What's more, they've loaded him with more magnificent gifts of bronze, gold, and fine clothing than he would ever have brought back from Troy, even if he had received his full share of the spoils and returned without trouble.</others>"

Zeus answered, "<others>Lord of the Earthquake, what are you talking about? It's unthinkable that the gods would disrespect you. It would be monstrous for them to insult a god as ancient and honored as you are. However, when it comes to mortals, if any of them act arrogantly or treat you disrespectfully, it's always up to you to deal with them as you see fit. So do as you please.</others>"

<others>"I would have done so at once,"</others> Poseidon replied, "<others>if I weren't worried about displeasing you, Father. Now, I want to wreck the Phaeacian ship as it returns from its escort. This will stop them from escorting anyone in the future. And I also want to bury their city under a huge mountain.</others>"

<others>"My good friend,"</others> Zeus answered, "<others>I recommend that at the very moment the city people are watching the ship return, you turn it into a rock near the land, one that still looks like a ship. This will astonish everyone, and then you can bury their city under the mountain.</others>"

Earth-encircling Poseidon heard this and went to Scheria, where the Phaeacians lived. He waited there until the swiftly sailing ship came close. Then he approached the ship, turned it into stone, and with the flat of his hand, drove it down, rooting it into the seabed. After doing all this, he vanished.

The Phaeacians began whispering among themselves. One person turned to their neighbor and said, "<others>Good heavens! Who could have rooted that ship in the sea just as it was entering the harbor? We saw it whole and complete only a moment ago!</others>"

They talked like this, but they had no idea what had happened. Then Alcinous spoke. "<others>Now I remember my father's old prophecy. He said that Poseidon would be furious with us for safely escorting everyone across the sea, and one day he would wreck a Phaeacian ship returning from an escort and bury our city under a high mountain. My old father always used to say this, and now it's all coming true. So now, let's all do as I say. First, we must stop escorting people who come here in the future. Second, let's sacrifice twelve chosen bulls to Poseidon, so he may have mercy on us and not bury our city under the high mountain.</others>" When the people heard this, they were terrified and prepared the bulls.

The leaders and kings of the Phaeacians were praying to Poseidon, standing around his altar. Just then, Odysseus finally woke up on his own homeland. He had been away for so long that he didn't recognize it as his home. What's more, Athena, daughter of Zeus, had created a thick fog. This was so no one would notice his return. Athena planned to tell Odysseus everything he needed to know. She wanted to make sure his wife, fellow citizens, and friends wouldn't recognize him until he had taken revenge on the wicked suitors. So, everything looked unfamiliar to him. The long roads, the harbors, the steep cliffs, even the beautiful trees—as he looked around his homeland, everything seemed to have changed. In despair, Odysseus slapped his thighs with both hands and cried out loudly.

He cried out, "<odysseus>Oh no! What kind of people have I landed among? Are they wild and uncivilized, or are they kind and welcoming to strangers? Where can I put all this treasure? And where am I supposed to go? I wish I had stayed with the Phaeacians! Or found another great leader who would have treated me well and given me an escort. Now, I don't know where to put my treasures, and I can't just leave them here for someone else to take. Truly, the Phaeacian leaders and kings didn't treat me fairly. They dropped me off in the wrong country. They promised to take me to Ithaca, but they didn't keep their word. May Zeus, the protector of those who plead for help, punish them! Zeus watches everyone and punishes those who do wrong. Still, I should count my belongings and check if the crew took anything.</odysseus>"

He counted his beautiful copper pots and cauldrons, his gold, and all his clothes. Luckily, nothing was missing. But he was still sad, believing he hadn't returned home. He walked up and down the shore of the echoing sea, lamenting his cruel fate. Just then, the goddess Athena appeared. She was disguised as a young shepherd, delicate and noble in appearance. A fine cloak was folded twice over her shoulders. She wore sandals on her graceful feet and carried a spear in her hand. Odysseus was delighted to see her and went straight up to her.

Odysseus said, "<odysseus>My friend, you are the first person I've met in this land. I greet you, and I beg you to be kind to me. Please protect my belongings and me. I embrace your knees and plead with you as if you were a god. So, tell me the truth: What land and country is this? Who are its people? Am I on an island, or is this the coast of a continent?</odysseus>"

The goddess Athena replied, "<others>Stranger, you must be very naive, or you've come from a very distant land, not to know this country. This is a famous place, known to everyone from east to west. It's rugged, not ideal for chariots, but it's certainly not a bad island. It gets water from rain and dew, so it produces plenty of grain and wine. It also raises many cattle and goats. All kinds of trees grow here, and there are many springs that never run dry. So, the name Ithaca is known even as far as Troy. And as I understand it, Troy is a long way from this Achaean land.</others>"

Odysseus was thrilled to hear Athena tell him he was in his own country. He began to answer, but with his natural cunning, he didn't speak the truth. Instead, he made up a false story.

Odysseus said, "<odysseus>I heard of Ithaca when I was in Crete, across the sea. Now, it seems I've arrived here with all these treasures. I've left just as much treasure behind for my children, but I'm on the run. That's because I killed Orsilochus, son of Idomeneus, the fastest runner in Crete. He tried to rob me of the spoils I'd gained from Troy, after so much hardship and danger on the battlefield and the weary sea. He claimed I hadn't served his father loyally as a vassal in Troy, but had acted as an independent ruler. So, I hid by the roadside with one of my men. When he came into town from the countryside, I speared him to death. It was a very dark night, and no one saw us. So, no one knew I had killed him. As soon as I had done it, I went to a ship and begged the Phoenician owners to take me on board and drop me off in Pylos or Elis, where the Epeans rule. I gave them enough spoils to satisfy them. They meant no harm, but the wind blew them off course. We sailed until we reached this place by night. We barely made it into the harbor. We were all starving, but no one said a word about supper. We all went ashore and lay down just as we were. I was so tired that I fell asleep immediately. So, they took my belongings out of the ship and placed them next to me, where I lay on the sand. Then they sailed away to Sidonia, leaving me here alone and in great distress.</odysseus>"

That was Odysseus's story. But the goddess Athena smiled and gently stroked him with her hand. Then she transformed into a beautiful, stately, and wise woman and said, "<others>Truly, no one could surpass you in cunning and storytelling, not even if you were up against a god! You daring rogue! Full of tricks and tireless in deceit, can't you stop your schemes and instinctive lies, even now that you're back in your own country? But let's not talk about that anymore. Both of us can use deception when needed. You are the most skilled counselor and speaker among all mortals, and among the gods, I have no equal in diplomacy and cleverness. Didn't you recognize me, Athena, daughter of Zeus? I have always been with you, watching over you through all your troubles. I'm the one who made the Phaeacians like you so much. And now I've come here again to talk with you and help you hide the treasures the Phaeacians gave you. I also want to tell you about the troubles waiting for you at home. You must face these problems, but don't tell anyone, man or woman, that you've returned home. Endure everything, and silently put up with everyone's arrogance.</others>"

Odysseus replied, "<odysseus>Goddess, a person can know many things, but you change your appearance so often that it's hard to recognize you when we meet. However, I know this much for sure: You were very kind to me while we Achaeans fought before Troy. But from the day we plundered Priam's city and boarded our ships, and the heavens scattered us—from that day, Athena, I saw you no more. I don't remember you ever coming to my ship to help me when I was in trouble. I had to wander, sick and sorrowful, until the gods delivered me from evil. Then I reached the city of the Phaeacians, where you encouraged me and led me into town. So now, I beg you in your father's name, tell me the truth. I don't believe I'm truly back in Ithaca. I think I'm in some other country, and you're mocking and deceiving me with everything you've said. So, tell me truly: Have I really returned to my homeland?</odysseus>"

The goddess Athena replied, "<others>You always think that way! That's why I can't abandon you in your troubles. You are so convincing, clever, and cunning. Anyone else, returning from such a long voyage, would have rushed home immediately to see his wife and children. But you don't seem interested in asking about them or hearing any news until you've dealt with your wife, who grieves for you in vain at home, shedding tears day and night without peace. As for my not coming near you, I was never worried about you. I was certain you would return safely, even if you lost all your men. And I didn't want to quarrel with my uncle Poseidon, who has never forgiven you for blinding his son. However, I will now show you the lay of the land, and then perhaps you will believe me. This is the harbor of the old sea god Phorcys, and an olive tree grows at its entrance. Nearby is a cave sacred to the Naiad nymphs. Here, too, is the arched cavern where you offered many excellent hecatombs to the nymphs. And this is the wooded Mount Neritum.</others>"

As Athena finished speaking, the goddess cleared away the mist, and the land appeared. Odysseus rejoiced to be on his homeland again and kissed the fertile earth. He raised his hands and prayed to the nymphs, saying, "<odysseus>Naiad nymphs, daughters of great Zeus! I thought I would never see you again. Now I greet you with all my love. If Zeus's mighty daughter grants me life and helps my son grow into a man, I will offer you sacrifices as I did in the old days.</odysseus>"

Athena replied, "<others>Take heart, and don't worry about that. First, let's quickly store your belongings safely in the cave. Then, we'll figure out the best way to handle everything.</others>"

Athena went into the cave to find the safest hiding spots. Meanwhile, Odysseus brought all the treasures the Phaeacians had given him: gold, bronze, and fine clothing. They carefully hid everything, and Athena sealed the cave entrance with a stone. Then the two sat by the root of a large olive tree and began to discuss how to destroy the wicked suitors.

Athena said to Odysseus, "<others>Noble son of Laertes, think about how you'll deal with these shameless men. For three years, they've been acting like masters in your house, wooing your wife and showering her with wedding gifts. Your wife laments your absence, yet she sends hopeful and encouraging messages to each of them. But in truth, her words mean the exact opposite of what she says.</others>"

Odysseus replied, "<odysseus>Goddess, to be honest, if you hadn't given me this timely information, I would have met the same tragic end in my own house as Agamemnon. Advise me on the best way to get my revenge. Stand by my side and fill my heart with courage, just like the day we brought down Troy. If you help me now as you did then, goddess, I can fight three hundred enemies if you are with me.</odysseus>"

Athena said, "<others>Leave that to me. Once we begin, I won't let you out of my sight for a moment. And I imagine some of those who are devouring your wealth will soon stain the pavement with their blood and brains. First, I will disguise you so no one will recognize you. I will cover your body with wrinkles and make all your golden hair disappear. I will dress you in clothes that will disgust everyone who sees you. I will dim your beautiful eyes and make you look ugly to the suitors, your wife, and the son you left behind. After that, go at once to your swineherd, who looks after your pigs. He has always been loyal to you and is devoted to Penelope and your son. You'll find him feeding his pigs near the Arethusa spring, by a place called 'Raven Rock,' where they fatten on beechmast and spring water. Stay with him and find out how things are going. Meanwhile, I will go to Sparta to meet your son. He is with King Menelaus in Lacedaemon, trying to find out if you are still alive.</others>"

Odysseus asked, "<odysseus>But why, since you knew everything, didn't you tell him? Did you want him to sail around facing all sorts of hardships while others were eating up his estate?</odysseus>"

Athena replied, "<others>Don't worry about him. I sent him so that people would speak well of his journey. He's not in any trouble at all. He's staying very comfortably with King Menelaus, surrounded by every kind of luxury. The suitors have gone out to sea and are waiting for him, planning to kill him before he can get home. But I don't think they'll succeed. Instead, some of those who are now eating up your estate will find their own graves first.</others>"

As she spoke, Athena touched Odysseus with her wand, covering his body with wrinkles. She made all his golden hair disappear and withered the flesh all over his body. His naturally beautiful eyes became dim. She changed his clothes, giving him an old, tattered cloak and a tunic that was ripped, filthy, and stained with soot. For an outer garment, she gave him an undressed deerskin. She also gave him a staff and a worn-out bag with a twisted strap to sling over his shoulder.

After making their plans, the two parted ways. The goddess went straight to Lacedaemon to bring Telemachus home.
