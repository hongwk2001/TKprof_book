Book 1

The Gods Meet — Athena Visits Ithaca — Telemachus Confronts the Suitors

Tell me, O Muse, about the clever hero who traveled far and wide after he destroyed the famous city of Troy. He visited countless cities and learned about the customs and cultures of many different people. He also faced terrible hardships at sea, trying to save his own life and bring his men safely home. But no matter how hard he tried, he couldn't save his crew. They died because of their own foolishness, eating the cattle of the Sun-god Helios. This angered the god, and none of them ever made it home. Daughter of Zeus, please tell me everything you know about these events.

Everyone else who survived the war or shipwrecks had made it home safely. Everyone, that is, except Odysseus. He desperately longed to return to his wife and homeland, but the goddess Calypso held him captive. She kept him in her vast cave, determined to marry him. Yet, as years passed, the gods finally decided it was time for Odysseus to return to Ithaca. Even then, his troubles wouldn't end once he was back among his own people. All the gods felt sorry for him, except for Poseidon. Poseidon relentlessly tormented Odysseus, blocking his path home.

Meanwhile, Poseidon had traveled to the land of the Ethiopians, who live at the very edge of the world, split into two halves, east and west. He was there enjoying a grand feast, accepting a sacrifice of a hundred sheep and a hundred oxen. While he was away, the other gods gathered in Zeus's palace on Mount Olympus. Zeus, father of gods and mortals, was the first to speak. He was thinking of Aegisthus, who had been killed by Agamemnon's son, Orestes. Zeus addressed the other gods. <others>"Look," he said, "how mortals blame us gods for things that are simply their own foolish mistakes! Take Aegisthus, for example. He wrongly fell in love with Agamemnon's wife and then murdered Agamemnon, even though he knew it would lead to his own death. Didn't I send Hermes to warn him? I told him that Orestes would surely seek revenge once he grew up and returned home. Hermes gave him sincere advice, but Aegisthus refused to listen. Now he has paid the ultimate price."</others>

Then Athena spoke. <others>"Father, son of Cronus, King of kings!" she exclaimed. "Aegisthus got what he deserved, and so would anyone else who acts like him. But Aegisthus isn't the issue right now. My heart aches for Odysseus. It breaks my heart to think of poor Odysseus suffering alone on that remote, forest-covered island in the middle of the sea, so far from all his friends. A goddess lives there, the daughter of the powerful magician Atlas, who guards the ocean depths and holds up the mighty pillars that separate heaven and earth. This daughter of Atlas has captured the unhappy Odysseus, and she constantly tries to charm him with sweet words, hoping he'll forget his home. Odysseus is weary of life, dreaming only of the day he can see the smoke rising from his own chimneys again. Yet, Father, you ignore all of this! Didn't Odysseus honor you with countless burnt sacrifices when he fought in the Trojan War? Why are you still so angry with him?"</others>

Zeus replied, <others>"My dear daughter, what are you saying? How could I ever forget Odysseus? There's no man on Earth more capable than he, nor one more generous with his offerings to us, the immortal gods of the sky! But remember this: Poseidon is still absolutely furious with Odysseus for blinding Polyphemus, the king of the Cyclopes. Polyphemus is Poseidon's son, born to the nymph Thoosa, daughter of the sea-king Phorcys. So, while Poseidon won't kill Odysseus outright, he torments him by stopping him from reaching home. Still, let's put our heads together and figure out a way for him to return. If we all agree, Poseidon won't be able to stand against us, and he'll eventually calm down."</others>

Then Athena said, <others>"Father, son of Cronus, King of kings! If the gods have truly decided that Odysseus should return home, then we must first send Hermes to Ogygia Island. He will tell Calypso our decision and order her to release Odysseus. In the meantime, I will go to Ithaca. I'll inspire Odysseus's son, Telemachus, and give him courage. I'll encourage him to call an assembly of the Achaeans and boldly confront the suitors who are devouring his sheep and oxen while trying to marry his mother, Penelope. I will also guide him to Sparta and Pylos, so he can search for any news about his beloved father's return. This will surely earn him respect and praise from the people."</others>

With these words, Athena fastened on her gleaming golden sandals. These sandals were indestructible, carrying her like the wind over land and sea. She gripped her formidable bronze spear, a sturdy and powerful weapon she used to subdue heroes who defied her will. Athena shot down from the highest peaks of Mount Olympus and instantly arrived in Ithaca. She stood at the gateway of Odysseus's house, disguised as a visitor named Mentes, chief of the Taphians, with the bronze spear in her hand. There, she saw the arrogant suitors. They were lounging on the hides of oxen they had slaughtered and eaten, playing a game similar to checkers right in front of the house. Servants and pages bustled around, waiting on them. Some mixed wine and water in large bowls, others wiped down tables with wet sponges and reset them, while still others carved huge portions of meat.

Telemachus saw her before anyone else. He was sitting sadly among the suitors, dreaming of his brave father. He imagined his father returning, honored as before, and kicking these men out of the house. Lost in thought, he spotted Athena. Annoyed that a stranger was waiting, he hurried to the gate. He took her right hand and asked for her spear. Telemachus said, <telemachus>"Welcome to our home. Once you've eaten, please tell us why you've come."</telemachus>

As he spoke, he led the way, and Athena followed. Inside, he took her spear and placed it in a spear-stand next to a sturdy pillar, among his unlucky father's many other spears. He then guided her to a richly decorated chair, spreading a damask cloth beneath it. There was a footstool for her feet. Telemachus placed his own chair far from the suitors. He wanted to shield Athena from their loud, arrogant behavior during her meal. He also hoped to ask her about his father more freely.

A maid brought a beautiful golden pitcher of water and poured it into a silver basin for them to wash their hands. Then she set a clean table beside them. A senior servant brought bread and many delicious dishes from the house. The carver brought plates of all kinds of meat and placed golden cups beside them. Another servant brought wine and poured it.

Then the suitors entered and took their places on the benches and chairs. Immediately, servants poured water over their hands, and maids circulated with baskets of bread. Pages filled the mixing bowls with wine and water, and the suitors began to eat the delicious food before them. Once they had eaten and drunk enough, they wanted music and dancing, the best parts of any feast. So a servant brought a lyre to Phemius, whom they forced to sing for them. As Phemius played his lyre and began to sing, Telemachus leaned close to Athena, speaking softly so no one else could hear.

Telemachus said, <telemachus>"Sir, I hope you won't be offended by what I'm about to say. This singing is a cheap thrill for those who don't pay for it. All this feasting comes at the expense of one man, whose bones are likely rotting in some wilderness or being ground to dust by the waves. If these men saw my father return to Ithaca, they'd wish for faster legs to run away, not fatter purses. Money wouldn't save them. But alas, he's met a terrible fate. Even when people sometimes say he's coming back, we don't believe it anymore. We'll never see him again. Now, sir, please tell me honestly: who are you and where do you come from? Tell me about your town and your parents. What kind of ship brought you here? How did your crew get you to Ithaca, and what nation did they say they were from? You couldn't have come by land. Also, tell me truthfully, for I want to know: are you new to this house, or have you visited before, during my father's time? In the old days, we had many visitors because my father traveled a lot himself."</telemachus>

Athena replied, <others>"I will tell you everything, truthfully and in detail. I am Mentes, son of Anchialus, and I am King of the Taphians. I've come here with my ship and crew on a trading voyage. We're heading to Temesa, to people who speak a different language, with a cargo of iron. I'll be bringing back copper. My ship is anchored over there, beyond the countryside, away from town, in Rheithron harbor, beneath Mount Neritum. Our fathers were friends long ago, as old Laertes will tell you if you go and ask him. People say, though, that he never comes to town anymore. He lives alone in the countryside, living a hard life, with an old woman to look after him and prepare his dinner when he returns tired from his vineyard. I came because I heard your father was home again, but it seems the gods are still holding him back. He's not dead, nor is he on the mainland. It's more likely he's on some remote island in the middle of the ocean, or held captive by savages against his will. I'm no prophet, and I know little about omens, but I speak as a message from the gods comes to me: he won't be away much longer. He is so clever and resourceful that even if he were bound in iron chains, he would find a way to get home again. But tell me, and tell me true, can Odysseus really have such a fine-looking son? You are wonderfully like him around the head and eyes. We were close friends before he sailed for Troy, where the best of all the Achaeans also went. Since then, neither of us has seen the other."</others>

Telemachus answered, <telemachus>"My mother tells me I am Odysseus's son, but they say it's a wise child who truly knows their own father. I wish I were the son of a man who had grown old on his own land. Honestly, since you asked, there's no man under heaven more unlucky than the one they say is my father."</telemachus>

Athena said, <others>"There's no fear of your family line dying out, not while Penelope has such a fine son like you. But tell me, and tell me true, what is the meaning of all this feasting? Who are these people? What's going on? Is it a feast, or a wedding in the family? No one seems to be bringing their own food. And these guests—their behavior is awful! They're making such a racket throughout the house, it would disgust any decent person who came near."</others>

Telemachus said, <telemachus>"Sir, regarding your question: as long as my father was here, our household and we ourselves were at peace. But the gods, in their anger, decided otherwise. They've hidden him away more completely than any mortal man has ever been hidden. I could have handled it better if he had died, if he had fallen with his men before Troy, or passed away surrounded by friends after his fighting days were over. Then the Achaeans would have built a mound over his ashes, and I would have inherited his fame. But now, storm-winds have swept him away to who knows where. He's vanished without a trace, and all I've inherited is despair. And it's not just the grief for my father's loss. The heavens have given me another kind of sorrow. The chiefs from all our islands—Dulichium, Same, and the wooded island of Zacynthus—along with the most important men of Ithaca itself, are devouring my household under the guise of courting my mother. She won't flat-out refuse to marry, but she also won't bring this situation to an end. So they are ruining my estate, and soon they will ruin me too."</telemachus>

<others>"Is that so?" Athena exclaimed. "Then you truly want Odysseus to come home! Give him his helmet, shield, and two spears. If he's the man I first met in our house, drinking and making merry, he would quickly deal with these wretched suitors the moment he stood on his own doorstep again. Back then, he had come from Ephyra to ask Ilus, son of Mermerus, for poison for his arrows. Ilus feared the immortal gods and wouldn't give him any, but my father gave him some because he was very fond of Odysseus. If Odysseus is still that man, these suitors will face a swift judgment and a miserable wedding."</others>

<others>Whether Odysseus returns to take his revenge in his own home, that's up to the gods. But I strongly urge you to find a way to get rid of these suitors right away. Listen to my advice. Tomorrow morning, gather the Achaean heroes for an assembly. Explain your situation to them and ask the gods to be your witnesses. Order the suitors to go back to their own homes. If your mother decides she wants to marry again, send her back to her father. He will find her a husband and provide all the wedding gifts a beloved daughter deserves.

For yourself, I will convince you to get the best ship you can find, with a crew of twenty men, and set out to search for your father, who has been missing for so long. Someone might tell you something, or perhaps a divine message will guide you, as often happens. First, go to Pylos and ask Nestor. Then, travel to Sparta and visit Menelaus, for he was the last of the Achaeans to return home. If you hear that your father is alive and on his way back, you can tolerate the suitors wasting your wealth for another year. But if you hear of his death, return home immediately. Hold a grand funeral, build a tomb in his memory, and let your mother marry again.

After you've done all this, carefully consider how you might kill these suitors in your own house, by fair means or foul. You are too old to act like a child any longer. Haven't you heard how people praise Orestes for killing Aegisthus, his father's murderer? You are a fine, clever young man. So, show your courage and make a name for yourself in history. Now, I must return to my ship and my crew. They will grow impatient if I keep them waiting longer. Think this matter over for yourself, and remember what I have told you.</others>

Telemachus answered, <telemachus>"Sir, thank you for speaking to me so kindly, as if I were your own son. I will follow everything you've told me. I know you're eager to continue your journey, but please stay a little longer. Take a bath and rest a bit. Then I will give you a present, and you can depart happily. I will give you a precious and beautiful gift, the kind only close friends exchange."</telemachus>

Athena replied, <others>"Don't try to hold me back. I must leave right away. As for the gift you wish to give me, keep it until I return. Then I will take it home with me. If you give me a fine gift, I will give you something equally valuable in return."</others>

With these words, Athena soared into the sky like a bird and vanished. But she had filled Telemachus with courage and made him think about his father more deeply than ever before. He felt the change within himself and was amazed. He realized the stranger had been a god. So, he went straight to where the suitors were sitting.

The bard Phemius was still singing. His listeners sat in silent awe, captivated by his sad tale of the return from the Trojan War and the hardships Athena had inflicted upon the Achaeans. Penelope, daughter of Icarius, heard his song from her upstairs room. She came down the grand staircase, not alone, but attended by two of her handmaids. When she reached the suitors, she stood by one of the pillars supporting the cloister roof. A calm handmaid stood on either side of her. She held a veil before her face and wept bitterly.

Penelope cried out, <others>"Phemius, you know so many other glorious deeds of gods and heroes, the kind poets love to sing about! Sing one of those stories for the suitors, and let them drink their wine in peace. But please stop this sad tale. It tears at my sorrowful heart and reminds me of my lost husband, whom I mourn endlessly. His name was renowned throughout all of Hellas and central Argos."</others>

Telemachus replied, <telemachus>"Mother, let the bard sing whatever he wishes. Bards don't create the misfortunes they sing about. It is Zeus, not them, who sends happiness or sorrow upon humankind according to his will. This man means no harm by singing of the Danaans' ill-fated return, for people always applaud the newest songs most enthusiastically. Be strong and accept this reality. Odysseus isn't the only man who never returned from Troy; many others lost their lives alongside him. So, go inside and focus on your daily duties: your loom, your distaff, and managing your servants. Speaking is a man's concern, and especially mine, for I am the master here."</telemachus>

She returned to the house, astonished, and took her son's words deeply to heart. Then, going upstairs to her room with her handmaids, she mourned her beloved husband until Athena shed sweet sleep over her eyes.

But the suitors were loud and boisterous throughout the covered hall, each one dreaming of becoming Penelope's husband.

Telemachus cried out, <telemachus>"You shameless, arrogant suitors! Feast all you want for now, but keep the noise down. It's not every day we hear a singer with a voice as divine as Phemius's. But tomorrow morning, I want all of you to gather and meet me. I will officially order you to leave. From now on, you'll take turns hosting feasts at your own homes, paying for them yourselves. If you insist on freeloading off one person, then even if the heavens don't help me, mighty Zeus himself will hold you fully responsible. And when you fall in my father's house, no one will come to avenge you!"</telemachus>

The suitors bit their lips, astonished by his bold speech. Then Antinous, son of Eupheithes, spoke up. <others>"It seems the gods have taught you how to bluster and exaggerate, young man. I pray that Zeus never allows you to become the ruler of Ithaca, as your father was!"</others>

Telemachus replied, <telemachus>"Antinous, don't scold me. If it's the will of the gods, then I will indeed become a ruler. Is that truly the worst fate you can imagine for me? Being a ruler isn't a bad thing at all; it brings both wealth and honor. But now that Odysseus is gone, Ithaca has many great men, both old and young, and any one of them might take the lead. Still, I will be the master of my own house, and I will rule over everything Odysseus won for me."</telemachus>

Then Eurymachus, son of Polybus, replied, <others>"It's up to the heavens to decide who among us will be ruler. But you, young man, will certainly be the master of your own house and your possessions. As long as there's a single man in Ithaca, no one will lay a hand on you or steal what is yours. Now, tell me, I'm curious about this stranger. What country does he hail from? What family does he belong to, and where is his estate? Did he bring news of your father's return, or was he here on his own business? He seemed like a man of means, but he vanished so suddenly before we could truly get to know him."</others>

<telemachus>"My father is dead and gone," Telemachus answered. "I no longer put any faith in rumors, no matter what I hear. My mother does sometimes call for a soothsayer and question him, but I pay no mind to his predictions. As for the stranger, he was Mentes, son of Anchialus, chief of the Taphians, an old friend of my father's."</telemachus> But deep in his heart, Telemachus knew it had been the goddess Athena.

The suitors went back to their singing and dancing, carrying on until evening. But when night finally fell, and their merrymaking came to an end, they each returned to their own homes to sleep.

Telemachus's room was high up in a tower, overlooking the outer courtyard. He made his way there, lost in deep thought. The kind old woman, Eurycleia, walked ahead of him, carrying two blazing torches. She was the daughter of Ops, son of Pisenor. Laertes had bought her with his own money when she was just a young girl, paying the worth of twenty oxen for her. He treated her with as much respect in his household as he did his own wife, though he never took her to his bed, fearing his wife's resentment. Now, she guided Telemachus to his room. She loved him more than any other woman in the house, for she had nursed him since he was a baby.

He opened his bedroom door and sat down on the bed. As he took off his shirt, he handed it to the kind old woman. She neatly folded it and hung it on a peg beside his bed. Then she went out, closing the door with a silver latch and securing the bolt with a leather strap. But Telemachus, covered by his woolen blanket, lay awake all night, his mind buzzing with thoughts of his planned voyage and the wise counsel Athena had given him.
