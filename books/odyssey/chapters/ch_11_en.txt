Book 11

A Visit to the Land of the Dead

We reached the shore, launched our ship, and set up the mast and sails. We loaded the sheep onto the ship and took our places. Our hearts were heavy, and we couldn't stop weeping. Circe, the powerful and clever goddess, sent us a favorable wind. The wind blew steadily from behind, filling our sails. We adjusted the ship's gear and let the wind and helmsman guide us. All day long, our sails stayed full, and the ship sailed smoothly across the sea. When the sun set and darkness covered the world, we entered the deep currents of the River Oceanus. This was the land and city of the Cimmerians, a people shrouded in mist and darkness. The sun's rays never reached them, not when it rose nor when it set. These poor Cimmerians lived in an endless, gloomy night. Once there, we beached our ship and unloaded the sheep. Then we walked along the banks of Oceanus until we reached the place Circe had told us about.

There, Perimedes and Eurylochus held the sheep we would sacrifice. I drew my sword and dug a trench, a cubit long on each side. I poured offerings for all the dead souls. First, I poured honey and milk, then wine, and finally water. I sprinkled white barley meal over it all. I prayed earnestly to the helpless spirits, promising them this: When I returned to Ithaca, I would sacrifice my best barren heifer for them and load the pyre with many good things. I specifically swore that Teiresias would receive a black sheep, the finest in my flock. After I had prayed enough to the dead, I slit the throats of the two sheep, letting their blood flow into the trench. Immediately, ghosts swarmed up from Erebus. There were brides, young bachelors, old men worn out by hard work, maidens whose love had been denied, and brave warriors killed in battle, their armor still stained with blood. They rushed from all directions, circling the trench with strange, eerie screams. The sound made my face turn pale with fear. Seeing the spirits gather, I quickly ordered my men to skin the dead sheep and burn them as offerings. At the same time, I told them to pray to Hades and Persephone. But I remained seated, my sword drawn. I wouldn't let those poor, helpless spirits come near the blood until Teiresias had answered my questions.

The first ghost to appear was my comrade, Elpenor. He hadn't been buried yet. We had left his body unmourned and unburied at Circe's house. We had simply been too busy. I felt such pity for him and wept when I saw him. "Elpenor," I said, "how did you come down to this dark, gloomy place? You've arrived here on foot faster than I have with my ship!"

He groaned and replied, "Captain, it was all due to bad luck and my own unbelievable drunkenness. I was sleeping on the roof of Circe's house. I never even thought of using the grand staircase to come down. Instead, I fell right off the roof and broke my neck. That's how my soul came to the house of Hades. Now, I beg you, by all those you've left behind, even if they aren't here with you—by your wife, by the father who raised you as a child, and by Telemachus, the sole hope of your house—please grant my request. I know that once you leave this underworld, you'll sail your ship back to Aeaea. Don't leave without mourning and burying me. If you do, the wrath of the gods might fall upon you. Burn me with whatever armor I have, and build a burial mound for me on the seashore. This way, people in the future will know what an unlucky fellow I was. And plant the oar I used to row with my shipmates when I was alive on top of my grave." "My poor friend," I said, "I will do everything you've asked."

So we sat there, sharing our sad conversation. I sat on one side of the trench, my sword drawn over the blood, while my comrade's ghost spoke to me from the other. Next, the ghost of my dead mother, Anticlea, daughter of Autolycus, appeared. She had been alive when I left for Troy, and seeing her brought a flood of tears to my eyes. But despite my sorrow, I wouldn't let her approach the blood until I had asked my questions of Teiresias.

Then came the ghost of Teiresias of Thebes, holding his golden staff. He recognized me and spoke. "Odysseus, noble son of Laertes, why, you poor man, have you left the light of day to visit the dead in this sorrowful place? Step back from the trench and put away your sword. Let me drink the blood, and I will answer your questions truthfully."

I stepped back and sheathed my sword. As soon as he drank the blood, he began his prophecy.

He said, "You want to know about your journey home. But the gods will make that path very difficult for you. It will be hard to escape the wrath of Poseidon, who still holds a deep grudge against you for blinding his son. Still, after much suffering, you might reach home if you can control yourself and your companions when your ship arrives at Thrinacia. On that island, you will find the sheep and cattle belonging to Helios, the Sun god, who sees and hears everything. If you leave these flocks unharmed and focus only on getting home, you may yet reach Ithaca after many hardships. But if you harm them, I warn you, both your ship and your men will be destroyed. Even if you yourself escape, you will return in a terrible state, having lost all your men, and on another man's ship. And you will find great trouble in your own house. Arrogant, disrespectful men will be squandering your wealth. They will be devouring your property under the pretense of courting your wife and offering her gifts."

"When you return home, you will take revenge on these suitors. After you have dealt with them in your house, whether by force or trickery, you must take a well-made oar and keep walking with it on your shoulder. Walk until you reach a land where the people have never heard of the sea, where they don't even mix salt with their food, and know nothing of ships or oars, which are like the wings of a ship. At that point, you will receive a clear sign that you cannot miss. A traveler will meet you and ask if what you carry on your shoulder is a winnowing shovel. There, you must plant the oar in the ground and sacrifice a ram, a bull, and a boar to Poseidon. Then, return home and offer grand sacrifices to all the gods in heaven, one after another. Your own death will come from the sea, and your life will gently fade away when you are full of years and at peace. Your people will bless you. All that I have foretold will come true."

"That," I replied, "must be as the heavens wish. But please, tell me the truth. My poor mother's ghost is here beside us. She sits by the blood, silent, and though I am her own son, she doesn't recognize me or speak to me. Sir, how can I make my mother know me?"

"That," he said, "I can tell you quickly. Any spirit you allow to taste the blood will speak with you like a living, rational being. But if you don't give them blood, they will simply vanish again."

After finishing his prophecies, Teiresias's ghost returned to the house of Hades. I remained seated until my mother came forward and tasted the blood. Immediately, she recognized me and spoke to me fondly. "My son, how did you come down to this dark dwelling while still alive? It's a difficult thing for the living to see these places. Between us and this place are vast, terrifying waters, and the River Oceanus, which no one can cross on foot. You must have a good ship to make the journey. Have you been trying all this time to find your way home from Troy? Have you still not returned to Ithaca or seen your wife in your own house?"

"Mother," I said, "I was forced to come here to seek advice from the ghost of Teiresias, the prophet of Thebes. I haven't even been near Achaea or set foot on my homeland. From the very first day I set out with Agamemnon for Ilion, the land of noble horses, to fight the Trojans, it has been an endless string of misfortunes. But please, tell me the truth. How did you die? Did you suffer from a long illness, or did the gods grant you a gentle, peaceful journey to eternity? Tell me also about my father and the son I left behind. Is my property still in their hands, or has someone else taken possession of it, believing I won't return to claim it? And tell me again what my wife intends to do, and what is in her heart. Does she live with my son, guarding my estate securely, or has she found the best match she could and remarried?"

My mother replied, "Your wife still remains in your house, but her heart is heavy with sorrow, and she weeps day and night. No one has yet taken possession of your fine property, and Telemachus still holds your lands undisturbed. He must entertain guests lavishly, as is expected of his position as a magistrate, and because everyone invites him. Your father stays at his old country home and never goes near the town. He has no comfortable bed or bedding. In winter, he sleeps on the floor by the fire with the servants, dressed in rags. But in summer, when the warm weather returns, he sleeps in the vineyard on a bed of vine leaves scattered on the ground. He grieves constantly over your never returning home, and he suffers more and more as he grows older. As for my own death, it happened like this: The gods did not take me swiftly and painlessly in my own house, nor was I struck down by any common illness that wears people out and kills them. It was simply my intense longing to know what you were doing, and the powerful force of my love for you, that led me to my death."

I tried to find a way to embrace my poor mother's ghost. Three times I rushed toward her, trying to wrap my arms around her, but each time she slipped through my embrace like a dream or a phantom. Heartbroken, I spoke to her. "Mother, why don't you stay still when I try to embrace you? If we could hold each other, we might find some sad comfort in sharing our sorrows, even here in the house of Hades. Is Persephone mocking me with a mere phantom, trying to burden me with even greater grief?"

My mother answered, "My son, most ill-fated of all mortals, it is not Persephone who is deceiving you. All people are like this when they are dead. The sinews no longer hold the flesh and bones together. As soon as life leaves the body, all these things vanish in a fierce, consuming fire, and the soul flits away like a dream. Now, return to the light of day as quickly as you can. Remember all these things well, so you can tell them to your wife later."

As we spoke, Persephone soon sent up the ghosts of the wives and daughters of all the most famous men. They gathered in crowds around the blood, and I wondered how I could question them one by one. In the end, I decided it would be best to draw the sharp sword hanging by my sturdy thigh and prevent them from all drinking the blood at once. So they came forward one after another, and each one, as I questioned her, told me her lineage and family.

The first ghost I saw was Tyro. She was the daughter of Salmoneus and the wife of Cretheus, son of Aeolus. She had fallen in love with the River Enipeus, the most beautiful river in the world. One day, as she was walking by the river as usual, Poseidon, disguised as her lover, lay with her at the river's mouth. A huge blue wave rose like a mountain, covering both the woman and the god. Poseidon then loosened her virgin girdle and put her into a deep sleep. After the god had completed his act of love, he took her hand and said, "Tyro, rejoice in all good fortune. The embraces of the gods are never in vain. About twelve months from now, you will give birth to splendid twins. Take great care of them. I am Poseidon. Now go home, but keep silent and tell no one."

Poseidon dove beneath the waves, and soon after, she gave birth to Pelias and Neleus. Both sons served Zeus with all their hearts. Pelias became a great sheep breeder in Iolcus, while Neleus lived in Pylos. Her other children, born to Cretheus, were Aeson, Pheres, and Amythaon, a formidable warrior and charioteer.

Next, I saw Antiope, daughter of Asopus. She proudly spoke of being embraced by Zeus himself, and with him, she bore two sons, Amphion and Zethus. They founded Thebes, the city of seven gates, and built a mighty wall around it. Even though they were powerful heroes, they couldn't truly protect Thebes until it was fortified.

After that, I saw Alcmena, Amphitryon's wife. She, too, bore a son to Zeus, the invincible hero Heracles. I also saw Megara, daughter of the great King Creon, who married Amphitryon's brave son, Heracles.

I also saw the beautiful Epicaste, King Oedipus's mother. She was cursed with the terrible fate of marrying her own son, unknowingly. Oedipus had killed his father, then married his mother. But the gods revealed the entire truth to the world. Oedipus remained king of Thebes, but lived in deep sorrow, tormented by the gods' curse. Epicaste, unable to bear her grief, hanged herself and went to the house of Hades, the mighty ruler of the underworld. Avenging spirits constantly tormented Oedipus for the insult to his mother, and he lived a life of bitter regret.

Next, I saw Chloris. Neleus married her for her beauty, offering priceless gifts to win her hand. She was the youngest daughter of Amphion, son of Iasus and king of Minyan Orchomenus, and she became Queen of Pylos. She bore Nestor, Chromius, and Periclymenus. She also gave birth to the astonishingly beautiful Pero, sought by suitors from all over the land. However, Neleus declared he would only give Pero to the man who could steal Iphicles' cattle from the pastures of Phylace. This was an incredibly difficult task. Only one person dared to attempt it: a brilliant prophet. But fate was against him. The cattle herders caught him and threw him in prison. A year passed, and when the same season returned, Iphicles released him, but only after the prophet had revealed all the gods' prophecies. And so, the will of Zeus was fulfilled.

I also saw Leda, wife of Tyndareus. She bore him two famous sons: Castor, the horse-tamer, and Pollux, the mighty boxer. These two heroes are buried beneath the earth, yet they are still alive. By a special grace from Zeus, they eternally alternate between death and life, one day dying and the next reborn. They hold the status of gods.

After her, I saw Iphimedeia, wife of Aloeus. She boasted of being embraced by Poseidon. She bore two sons, Otus and Ephialtes, but both were short-lived. They were the most magnificent and handsome children ever born, second only to Orion. At just nine years old, they were nine fathoms tall, and their chests measured nine cubits around. They declared war on the Olympian gods. They tried to stack Mount Ossa on top of Mount Olympus, and Mount Pelion on top of Ossa, hoping to climb all the way to heaven. If they had lived to adulthood, they surely would have succeeded. But Apollo, son of Leto, killed them both before a single hair had grown on their cheeks or chins.

Next, I saw Phaedra, Procris, and Ariadne, the beautiful daughter of the sorcerer Minos. Theseus was taking her from Crete to Athens. But Theseus never truly had her. Before he could, the goddess Artemis killed her on the island of Dia, because Dionysus had spoken ill of her.

I also saw Maera and Clymene, and the hateful Eriphyle, who sold her own husband for gold. But it would take me all night to name every single wife and daughter of heroes I saw. It's time for me to go to bed, either on my ship with my crew or here. As for my escort home, the gods and you yourselves will see to it.

Odysseus finished his story. The guests, sitting in the covered hall, were completely captivated and speechless. Then Queen Arete spoke to them.

"People of Phaeacia, what do you think of this man? Isn't he tall and handsome, and so clever? It's true he is my guest, but this honor extends to all of you. Do not be in a hurry to send him away, and don't be stingy with gifts for someone in such great need. After all, the heavens have blessed all of you with great abundance."

The oldest hero among them, Echeneus, spoke up. "My friends," he said, "Our noble queen has just spoken words that are both reasonable and fitting. So, let us be persuaded by them. However, the final decision, in both word and deed, rests with King Alcinous."

King Alcinous exclaimed, "It shall be done, as surely as I live and rule over Phaeacia! Our guest is eager to return home, but we must convince him to stay with us until tomorrow. By then, I will have gathered all the gifts I intend to give him. His escort will be a responsibility for all of you, and especially for me, as your leader."

Odysseus answered, "King Alcinous, if you commanded me to stay here for a full twelve months, and then sent me off, laden with your generous gifts, I would gladly obey. It would be a great advantage to me. When I return to Ithaca, I could bring more to my people, and everyone who sees me would respect and love me even more."

Alcinous replied, "Odysseus, none of us who have seen you believe you are a charlatan or a trickster. I know there are many who tell such convincing stories that it's hard to tell truth from lies. But your way of speaking assures me of your good character. What's more, you've told the story of your own misfortunes and those of the Argives like a seasoned bard. But tell me honestly, did you see any of the great heroes who went to Troy with you and died there? The night is still long, and it's not yet time for bed. So please, continue your incredible story. If you keep telling us your adventures, I could sit here and listen until tomorrow morning."

Odysseus answered, "King Alcinous, there is a time for speaking, and a time for sleep. However, since you wish it so strongly, I cannot refuse to tell you the even sadder tale of my comrades. They did not die fighting the Trojans, but perished on their journey home, betrayed by a wicked woman.

After Persephone sent the female spirits away in all directions, the ghost of Agamemnon, son of Atreus, approached me, a sorrowful figure surrounded by those who had died with him in Aegisthus's house. As soon as he tasted the blood, he recognized me. He wept bitterly and stretched out his arms to embrace me, but he had no strength or form left. I, too, wept and felt pity as I watched him. I asked, "King Agamemnon, how did you meet your death? Did Poseidon stir up winds and waves to overwhelm you at sea? Or did your enemies kill you on land while you were stealing cattle or sheep, or fighting to defend your wives and city?"

He answered, "Odysseus, noble son of Laertes, I was not lost at sea in a storm raised by Poseidon, nor did my enemies kill me on land. Aegisthus and my wicked wife conspired to kill me. He invited me to his house for a feast, then butchered me miserably, like a fat animal in a slaughterhouse. My comrades around me were slaughtered like sheep or pigs for a nobleman's wedding feast, picnic, or grand banquet. You must have seen countless men die in great battles or single combat, but you never witnessed anything as truly pitiful as how we fell in that hall. Mixing bowls and laden tables lay scattered everywhere, and the ground was soaked with our blood. I heard Priam's daughter Cassandra scream as Clytemnestra killed her right beside me. I lay dying on the ground, a sword in my body, and reached out to kill that murderous harlot, but she slipped away. She wouldn't even close my lips or eyes as I died. There is nothing in this world as cruel and shameless as a woman who has fallen into such guilt. Imagine, murdering her own husband! I thought I would be welcomed home by my children and servants, but her abominable crime has brought disgrace not only upon herself but upon all women who come after, even the good ones."

I said, "Truly, Zeus seems to have hated the house of Atreus from beginning to end because of the schemes of its women. Look how many of us fell for Helen's sake! And now it seems Clytemnestra plotted mischief against you too, during your absence."

Agamemnon continued, "So, be careful. Don't be too open even with your own wife. Don't tell her everything you know. Tell her only some things, and keep the rest to yourself. Not that your wife, Odysseus, is likely to murder you, for Penelope is a truly admirable woman with a very good nature. When we left for Troy, she was a young bride with a baby at her breast. That child must surely be a grown man now, and he and his father will have a joyful meeting and embrace, as they should. But my wicked wife didn't even allow me the joy of seeing my son; she killed me before I could. And I'll tell you one more thing, take it to heart: when you bring your ship to Ithaca, don't announce your arrival. Go secretly, like a surprise attack. After all I've been through, I know women cannot be trusted. But now, tell me truly, do you have any news of my son Orestes? Is he in Orchomenus, or Pylos, or with Menelaus in Sparta? I assume he is still alive."

I replied, "Agamemnon, why do you ask me that? I don't know if your son is alive or dead. It's not right to speak carelessly about things I don't know."

As we sat there, weeping and talking sadly, the ghost of Achilles approached us, accompanied by Patroclus, Antilochus, and Ajax. Ajax was the finest and most handsome of all the Danaans, second only to Achilles, son of Peleus. Achilles recognized me and spoke with a mournful voice. "Odysseus, noble son of Laertes," he said, "what daring feat brings you down to the house of Hades, among us powerless dead, who are merely the shadows of those who can no longer toil?"

I replied, "Achilles, son of Peleus, greatest hero of the Achaeans, I came to consult Teiresias, hoping he could advise me on my journey back to Ithaca. I haven't even come close to Achaean land, nor have I set foot in my homeland. I've been in constant trouble. But Achilles, no one has ever been as fortunate as you, nor will anyone ever be. When you were alive, all of us Argives adored you. And even here, among the dead, you are treated like a great prince. So, don't be too sad just because you are dead."

Achilles replied, "Don't speak favorably of death. I would rather be a hired servant in a poor man's house, alive on earth, than be king of kings among the dead. But tell me, give me news about my son. Has he gone to war? Is he a great soldier, or not? Also, tell me if you've heard anything about my father, Peleus. Does he still rule the Myrmidons? Or, now that he's old and his body is weak, do people throughout Hellas and Phthia no longer respect him? If only I could stand by his side, even for a single day, with the same strength I had when I killed the bravest of our enemies on the plains of Troy! If I could just go to his house, anyone who tried to harm him or take his place would quickly regret it!"

I replied, "I've heard nothing about Peleus. But I can tell you everything about your son, Neoptolemus, because I brought him from Scyros on my own ship with the Achaeans. In our war councils before Troy, he was always the first to speak, and his judgment was flawless. Only Nestor and I could surpass him. When battles raged on the plains of Troy, he never stayed behind his men. Instead, he charged far ahead, the bravest of them all. He killed countless enemies. I can't list every single person he slew while fighting for the Argives, but I can tell you how he killed the valiant hero Eurypylus, son of Telephus. Eurypylus was the most handsome man I ever saw, except for Memnon. Many other Ceteians also fell around him because of a woman's bribes. Furthermore, when all the bravest Argives entered the horse that Epeus had built, it was my job to decide when we should open or close the door of our ambush. At that time, all the other Danaan leaders and chief men were wiping away tears and trembling all over. But I never once saw him turn pale or wipe a tear from his cheek. He constantly gripped the hilt of his sword and his bronze-tipped spear, breathing fury against the enemy, urging me to burst out from the horse. Yet, when we plundered Priam's city, he boarded his ship with a generous share of the spoils, without a single wound, neither from a thrown spear nor in close combat. For the rage of Ares, the god of war, is unpredictable."

When I finished speaking, the ghost of Achilles strode across a meadow filled with asphodel flowers. He was clearly delighted by my tale of his son's bravery.

The ghosts of other dead men gathered around me, each eager to tell their own sad story. But the ghost of Ajax, son of Telamon, kept his distance. He was still angry with me for winning the dispute over Achilles' armor. Thetis had offered the armor as a prize, and Trojan prisoners along with the goddess Athena had been the judges. Oh, how I wish I had never won that contest! It cost Ajax his life. He was the most outstanding hero among the Danaans, second only to Achilles, son of Peleus, in both stature and bravery.

When I saw him, I tried to calm him, saying, "Ajax, can you not forget and forgive, even in death? Does the judgment over that hateful armor still torment you? Losing such a strong pillar as you were was a terrible loss for us Argives. We mourned you as deeply as we mourned Achilles, son of Peleus himself. The blame for all this lies with the malice Zeus held against the Danaans. It was his spite that led to your destruction. So, come closer, calm your proud spirit, and listen to what I have to tell you."

He gave no answer, but turned away into Erebus, the darkness of the underworld, joining the other ghosts. Even though he was so angry, I should have made him talk to me, or at least kept speaking to him. However, I couldn't, because there were still other dead souls I wished to see.

Then I saw Minos, son of Zeus, holding a golden scepter and judging the dead. In the vast house of Hades, ghosts gathered around him, sitting and standing, waiting to hear his verdicts.

Next, I saw the mighty Orion. In a meadow full of asphodel flowers, he was herding the ghosts of the wild beasts he had hunted in the mountains. In his hand, he carried a massive bronze club, unbreakable forever.

Then I saw Tityus, son of Gaia. He lay stretched out on the plain, his enormous body covering about nine acres of ground. On either side of him, two vultures dug their beaks into his liver. Tityus tried to swat them away with his hands, but it was useless. This was his punishment for violating Leto, Zeus's lover, as she passed through Panopeus on her way to Pytho.

I also witnessed the terrible fate of Tantalus. He stood in a lake that reached his chin. He was dying of thirst, but could never reach the water. Whenever the poor man bent down to drink, the water would dry up and vanish, leaving only parched ground, cursed by the heavens. Above his head, tall trees dangled their fruit. There were pears, pomegranates, apples, sweet figs, and juicy olives. But whenever the poor man reached out to grab some, the wind would whip the branches back up into the clouds.

And then I saw Sisyphus. He was in endless torment, straining with both hands to push a massive stone. He used his hands and feet, struggling to roll the boulder to the top of the hill. But always, just before he could push it over to the other side, its immense weight would overwhelm him. Then the relentless stone would thunder back down to the plain. He would start pushing the stone uphill again, sweat pouring off him, and steam rising behind him.

Next, I saw the mighty Heracles, or rather, his phantom. The true Heracles feasts forever with the immortal gods, having taken the beautiful Hebe, daughter of Zeus and Hera, as his wife. Ghosts shrieked around him, scattering like frightened birds. He held his bow in his hand, an arrow notched to the string, and glared around him, dark as night, ready to shoot at any moment. Around his chest was a wondrous golden belt, amazingly adorned with bears, wild boars, and lions with gleaming eyes. Scenes of war, battle, and death were also etched into the belt. The craftsman who made that belt, no matter how hard he tried, could never create another like it. Heracles recognized me at once and spoke with pity. "My poor Odysseus, noble son of Laertes," he said, "are you also living the same miserable life I endured when I was on earth? I was the son of Zeus, yet I suffered endlessly, becoming a slave to someone far beneath me, a lowly fellow who set me all sorts of grueling tasks. That base man gave me all kinds of missions. He even sent me here once to fetch the hell-hound. He thought there could be no harder task, but with the help of Hermes and Athena, I dragged the hound out of Hades' realm and brought it to him."

After saying this, Heracles descended back into the house of Hades. I remained where I was, hoping that other mighty dead heroes might approach me. I longed to see others who had passed on, glorious children of the gods like Theseus and Pirithous. However, thousands upon thousands of ghosts swarmed around me, uttering appalling cries. I was seized with panic, fearing that Persephone might send up the head of the dreadful monster Gorgon from the house of Hades. At once, I rushed back to my ship and ordered my men to board immediately and cast off the ropes. They quickly boarded and took their places, and the ship sailed down the stream of the river Oceanus. We had to row at first, but soon a fair wind sprang up.
