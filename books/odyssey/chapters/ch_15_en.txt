Book 15

Athena Calls Telemachus Home from Lacedaemon — He Meets Theoclymenus at Pylos and Brings Him to Ithaca — Upon Landing, He Heads to Eumaeus's Hut

Athena journeyed to the beautiful city of Lacedaemon. She went to tell Odysseus's son, Telemachus, that he needed to return home immediately. She found him and Pisistratus asleep in the front courtyard of Menelaus's palace. Pisistratus was sound asleep, but Telemachus had been restless all night, his mind filled with thoughts of his troubled father. Athena approached him and spoke.

"Telemachus, you cannot stay away from home any longer. Don't leave your property in the hands of those dangerous men in your house. They will devour everything you own, and your journey will have been for nothing. Ask Menelaus to send you home immediately. That way, you might still find your excellent mother there when you return. Her father and brothers are already pressuring her to marry Eurymachus. He's showering her with more gifts than any of the other suitors. I hope nothing valuable has been stolen from your house while you've been gone. But you know how women are. They always want to do their best for the man who marries them. They often forget the children of their first husband, or even the husband himself once he's dead and gone. So, go home. Put everything in the care of your most trustworthy female servant until the gods bless you with a wife of your own. Now, listen carefully, for there's another important matter you must attend to. The leading suitors are waiting for you in the strait between Ithaca and Samos. They plan to kill you before you can reach home. But I don't think they'll succeed. In fact, it's more likely that some of those who are now feasting on your property will find their own graves instead. Sail day and night, keeping your ship far from the islands. The god who watches over and protects you will send a favorable wind. As soon as you reach Ithaca, send your ship and crew to the town. But you, go straight to the swineherd who looks after your pigs. He is loyal to you. Stay with him for the night, then send him to Penelope to tell her you've returned safely from Pylos."

Athena then returned to Mount Olympus. Telemachus nudged Pisistratus with his heel to wake him. "Pisistratus, wake up!" he said. "We need to yoke the horses to the chariot. It's time for us to head home."

Pisistratus replied, "No matter how much of a hurry we're in, we can't drive in the dark. Morning will be here soon. Let's wait until Menelaus brings his gifts and loads them into the chariot for us. And let him give us a proper farewell. A guest should never forget a host who has shown them kindness, not as long as they live."

As Pisistratus spoke, day began to break. Menelaus, who had already risen and left Helen in bed, approached them. The moment Telemachus saw him, he quickly pulled on his shirt, draped a large cloak over his shoulders, and went to meet him. "Your Majesty, Menelaus," Telemachus said, "please allow me to return to my homeland now. I long to go home."

Menelaus replied, "Telemachus, if you are determined to go, I won't hold you back. I believe a host should be neither too clingy with his guest nor too rude. Balance is always best in all things. Holding someone back when they want to leave is just as wrong as telling someone to go if they wish to stay. A guest should be treated well while in the house and sent off smoothly when they wish to depart. So, wait a little while. Let me load your beautiful gifts into your chariot, and you can inspect them yourself. I'll tell the women to prepare a good meal for you from what we have in the house. It will be more fitting and practical for you to eat before setting out on such a long journey. And if you wish to tour Hellas or the Peloponnese, I will personally yoke my horses and guide you through all our main cities. No one will send us away empty-handed. Everyone will give us something, like a bronze tripod, a pair of mules, or a golden cup."

Telemachus answered, "Your Majesty, Menelaus, I want to go home immediately. When I left, I left my property unguarded. I fear that while searching for my father, I might face ruin myself, or find that valuable items have been stolen during my absence."

When Menelaus heard this, he immediately told his wife and servants to prepare a good meal from what they had in the house. Just then, Eteoneus joined him, having just woken up and living nearby. Menelaus instructed him to light the fire and cook some meat, which Eteoneus promptly did. Then, Menelaus went down to his fragrant storeroom, not alone, but with Helen and Megapenthes. When he reached the place where his household treasures were kept, he chose a double cup. He told his son, Megapenthes, to also bring a silver mixing bowl. Meanwhile, Helen went to the chest where she kept the beautiful dresses she had made herself. She took out the largest and most beautifully embroidered dress. It glittered like a star and lay at the very bottom of the chest.

Then they all returned through the house to Telemachus. Menelaus said, "Telemachus, may Zeus, the mighty husband of Hera, bring you safely home as you wish. Now, I will present you with the finest and most precious piece of silverware in all my house. It is a mixing bowl of pure silver, with only the rim inlaid with gold. It is the work of Hephaestus himself. King Phaedimus of Sidon gave it to me as a gift when I visited him on my way home. I would be honored to give it to you."

As soon as he finished speaking, Menelaus placed the double cup in Telemachus's hands. Megapenthes brought the beautiful mixing bowl and set it before him. Nearby stood the beautiful Helen, holding a ceremonial robe.

Helen said, "My son, I, Helen, also have a keepsake for you. This is a robe for your bride to wear on her wedding day. Until then, ask your dear mother to keep it safe for you. May you return joyfully to your own country and your home."

With these words, Helen handed him the robe, and Telemachus gladly accepted it. Pisistratus then loaded the gifts into the chariot, admiring each one as he did so. Soon after, Menelaus led Telemachus and Pisistratus inside the house, and they both sat down at the table. A maidservant brought water in a beautiful golden pitcher and poured it into a silver bowl for them to wash their hands. She then pulled a clean table next to them. A senior servant brought bread and offered them all sorts of delicious foods from the house. Eteoneus carved the meat and served each person their portion, while Megapenthes poured the wine. They enjoyed the delicious food before them. As soon as they had eaten and drunk their fill, Telemachus and Pisistratus yoked the horses and took their places in the chariot.

They drove out through the inner gateway and passed under the echoing gatehouse of the outer courtyard. Menelaus followed them, holding a golden goblet of wine in his right hand so they could make a drink-offering before they departed. He stood in front of the horses and offered a toast. "Farewell to both of you," he said. "Please tell Nestor how well I treated you. He was as kind as a father to me when we Achaeans fought before Troy."

Telemachus answered, "Yes, Your Majesty. We will be sure to tell him everything the moment we see him. How I wish I were as certain of finding my father Odysseus returned when I get back to Ithaca! Then I could tell him of the immense kindness you have shown me and of the many beautiful gifts I am taking with me."

Just as Telemachus finished speaking, a bird flew to their right. It was an eagle, clutching a large white goose in its talons, which it had snatched from a farmyard. All the men and women were running after it, shouting. The eagle flew very close to them, then veered off to their right, in front of the horses. When they saw it, they rejoiced and felt a sense of comfort. Pisistratus then asked, "Your Majesty, Menelaus, has heaven sent this omen for us, or for you?"

Menelaus was pondering what the most fitting answer would be, but Helen spoke before him. "I will interpret this as the gods have placed it in my heart," she said, "and I have no doubt it will come to pass. The eagle came from the mountain where it was born and built its nest. In the same way, Odysseus, after traveling far and enduring much suffering, will return to take his revenge. Perhaps he is already back and plotting trouble for the suitors."

Telemachus replied, "May Zeus grant that it be so! If it truly comes to pass, I will vow to honor you as if you were a god, even when I am back home."

As he spoke, Telemachus cracked his whip over the horses, and they galloped at full speed through the town towards the open country. The horses shook the yoke on their necks and traveled all day until the sun set and darkness covered the land. They reached Pherae, the home of Diocles, son of Ortilochus, son of Alpheus. There, they spent the night and were treated with generous hospitality. When rosy-fingered Dawn, child of morning, appeared, they again yoked their horses and took their places in the chariot. They drove out through the inner gateway and under the echoing gatehouse of the outer courtyard. Pisistratus then lashed his horses, and they flew forward eagerly. Before long, they arrived at Pylos, and Telemachus said:

"Pisistratus, will you promise to grant me a favor? You know our fathers were old friends long before us. And we're the same age, too; this journey has brought us even closer. So, please don't drive past my ship. Drop me off there. If I go to your father's house, he'll surely try to keep me with his warm hospitality. I really need to get home right away."

Pisistratus thought about how to grant Telemachus's request. He decided it was best to turn the horses toward the ship and load the beautiful gifts of gold and clothing from Menelaus onto the stern. Then Pisistratus said, "You should get on board your ship right away. Tell your men to do the same before I go home and tell my father. I know how stubborn my father can be. He definitely won't let you leave easily. He'll come all the way here to get you, and he won't go back without you. Then he'll be very angry."

Nestor drove his fine horses back to the city of Pylos and soon arrived home. Meanwhile, Telemachus called his sailors together and gave them orders. "Alright, lads," he said, "make sure everything is ready on board the ship, and let's head home!"

Just as Telemachus had ordered, the sailors boarded the ship. Telemachus was busy at the stern, praying and offering sacrifices to the goddess Athena. Just then, a man from a distant land approached him. He was a seer, fleeing from Argos because he had killed someone.

This man was a descendant of Melampus, who once lived in Pylos, the land of sheep. Melampus was wealthy and owned a grand estate, but the powerful King Neleus drove him into exile. Neleus seized Melampus's property and imprisoned him for a whole year in King Phylacus's house. Melampus suffered greatly, tormented by the issue of Neleus's daughter and a deep sorrow inflicted by the fearsome Erinyes, the goddesses of vengeance. Eventually, he escaped with his life. He drove his cattle from Phylace to Pylos, avenged the injustice done to him, and gave Neleus's daughter to his brother. After that, he left the land and went to Argos, where he was destined to rule over many people.

In Argos, Melampus married and settled down, having two famous sons, Antiphates and Mantius. Antiphates became the father of Oicleus, and Oicleus became the father of Amphiaraus. Amphiaraus was deeply loved by both Zeus and Apollo, but he didn't live to old age. He was killed in Thebes because of a woman's gifts. His sons were Alcmaeon and Amphilochus. Melampus's other son, Mantius, was the father of Polypheides and Cleitus. The goddess Eos, seated on her golden throne, was so enchanted by Cleitus's beauty that she carried him off to live among the immortal gods. But after Amphiaraus's death, Apollo made Polypheides the greatest seer in the entire world. Polypheides quarreled with his father and went to live in Hyperesia, where he remained, prophesying for everyone.

It was this very Polypheides' son, Theoclymenus, who now approached Telemachus as he was pouring out wine offerings and praying on his ship.

"Friend," Theoclymenus said, "I see you making sacrifices here. I beg you, by these very offerings and by the god who receives them, and I also implore you, by your own life and the lives of your crew, to tell me nothing but the truth. Who are you, and where do you come from? Please tell me about your home and your parents."

Telemachus answered, "I will tell you the absolute truth. I am from Ithaca, and my father is Odysseus. I am as certain of that as I am that he once lived. But it seems he met a tragic end. That's why I've taken this ship and gathered my crew, hoping to hear some news of him, for he has been away from home for a very long time."

"I, too," Theoclymenus replied, "am an exile. I killed a man from my own people. He has many brothers and relatives in Argos, and they hold great power among the Argives. I am fleeing to escape death at their hands, and so I am destined to be a wanderer across the earth. I am your humble suppliant. Please, take me aboard your ship so they cannot kill me. I know they are pursuing me."

Telemachus answered, "If you wish to join us, I will not refuse you. Come aboard, then. In Ithaca, we will treat you with generous hospitality, according to what we have."

Telemachus took Theoclymenus's spear and laid it on the ship's deck. He boarded and sat in the stern, inviting Theoclymenus to sit beside him. Then the sailors untied the ropes. Telemachus told them to grab the sail ropes, and they quickly obeyed. They set the mast into its socket in the cross plank, raised it, and secured it firmly with the forestays. Then, using twisted ox-hide ropes, they hoisted the white sails. The goddess Athena sent a fresh, strong tailwind, ensuring the ship sailed as fast as possible. With this, they passed by Crouni and Chalcis.

Soon, the sun set, and darkness covered the entire land. The ship made a swift passage to Pheae and then on to Elis, where the Epeans ruled. Telemachus then steered the ship toward the 'flying islands,' wondering to himself if he would escape death or be captured.

Meanwhile, Odysseus and the swineherd were eating supper in the hut, joined by the other men. As soon as they finished eating and drinking, Odysseus decided to test the swineherd. He wanted to see if Eumaeus would continue to treat him kindly, let him stay there, or send him off to the city. So, Odysseus said:

"Eumaeus, and all of you," Odysseus began, "tomorrow I want to leave and start begging around the town. I don't want to be a burden to you or your men any longer. So, please give me your advice and assign a good guide to go with me and show me the way. I'll have to go around the city begging, just to see if anyone will give me a drink and a piece of bread. I'd also like to go to Odysseus's house and bring news of her husband to Queen Penelope. Then I could move among the suitors and see if, from all their abundance, they might offer me dinner. I could quickly become an excellent servant for them in many ways. Believe me when I tell you, by the blessing of the god Hermes, who grants grace and a good name to the work of all people, there's no one alive who would make a handier servant than I would. I can put fresh wood on the fire, chop fuel, carve meat, cook, pour wine, and perform all the services that poor people must do for their betters."

The swineherd was deeply disturbed when he heard this. "Good heavens!" he cried. "What on earth put such an idea into your head? If you even go near the suitors, you'll surely be ruined! Their pride and arrogance reach the very heavens. They would never consider taking a man like you as a servant. Their servants are all young men, well-dressed, wearing fine cloaks and shirts, with handsome faces and always neatly groomed hair. Their tables are always kept perfectly clean and loaded with bread, meat, and wine. So please, just stay here. You're not bothering anyone. Neither I nor anyone else minds you being here. When young master Telemachus returns, he will give you a shirt and a cloak and send you wherever you wish to go."

Odysseus replied, "I hope the gods hold you as dear as I do, for saving me from wandering into trouble. There's truly nothing worse than constantly being on the move. But once a person is truly desperate, they'll endure any hardship to fill their poor belly. Still, since you insist I stay here and await Telemachus's return, please tell me about Odysseus's mother and his father, who was already on the threshold of old age when Odysseus left for Troy. Are they still alive, or have they already passed into the house of Hades?"

"I will tell you everything about them," Eumaeus replied. "Laertes is still alive, but he's terribly distressed by his son's absence and the death of his wife. He prays to the gods to let him pass away peacefully in his own home. His wife, I'm sad to say, met a tragic end due to her overwhelming grief for her son. I hope no friend or neighbor who has been kind to me ever suffers such a fate. While she was alive, she was always sorrowful, but I enjoyed seeing her and asking how she was. She raised me alongside her youngest daughter, Ctimene. We grew up together, and she treated us both without favoritism. But once we both grew up, Ctimene was married off to Same with a splendid dowry. As for me, my mistress gave me a good shirt, a cloak, and a pair of sandals, and sent me to work in the countryside. Yet, she remained as fond of me as ever. Now, all of that is over. Still, I'm fortunate that the gods have blessed my work in my current position. I have enough to eat and drink, and I can offer hospitality to any respectable traveler who comes here. However, one cannot expect a kind word or deed from my mistress anymore, because the house has fallen into the hands of wicked people. Servants sometimes want to see their mistress and have a chat, to get food and drink from the house, and perhaps something to take back with them to the country. These things are what keep servants in good spirits."

Odysseus replied, "Then, Eumaeus, you must have been a very young boy when you were taken so far away from your home and parents. Tell me honestly: was the city where your father and mother lived plundered and destroyed? Or were you captured by enemies while you were alone, tending sheep or cattle, then shipped here and sold to your master?"

"Stranger," Eumaeus replied, "I will tell you about what you asked. Please, sit comfortably, drink your wine, and listen to my story. These are the longest nights of the year, so there's plenty of time for both sleeping and sitting together, talking. You shouldn't go to bed until it's truly bedtime, as too much sleep is just as bad as too little. If anyone else wants to go to bed, they are free to leave us and do so. They can then take my master's pigs out after they've had breakfast in the morning. You and I will sit here in the hut, eating and drinking, and sharing stories of our misfortunes. For a person who has suffered much and been tossed about by the world often finds a strange pleasure in recalling sad memories from long ago. So, regarding your question, my tale is as follows:"

You might have heard of an island called Syra. It's located above Ortygia, where the land turns in a different direction. It's not heavily populated, but the soil is rich, with plenty of pastures perfect for raising cattle and sheep. Grapes and wheat grow abundantly there too. They never suffer from famine, and the people are free from disease. When they grow old, the gods Apollo and Artemis visit them, bringing a painless death with their arrows. The island has two communities, and the entire land is divided between them. My father's son, Ctesius, was a great man, like the gods themselves, and he ruled both communities.

One day, cunning Phoenician merchants arrived on a ship packed with all sorts of glittering trinkets and jewels. The Phoenicians were incredibly skilled sailors, you see. It so happened that my father's house had a maid from Phoenicia. She was tall, beautiful, and an excellent servant. These scoundrels caught her one day while she was washing clothes near their ship. They seduced her, tempting her in a way no woman could resist. The man who charmed her asked who she was and where she came from. She told him her father's name. "I am from Sidon," she said, "the daughter of Aribas, a man of immense wealth. One day, as I was coming into town from the countryside, Taphians pirates captured me. They brought me across the sea to this place and sold me to the master of this house. He paid them my ransom."

The man who had charmed her said, "Wouldn't you like to come with us and see your parents and their home? I hear they are both alive and doing well."

"I would gladly do that," she replied. "But first, you must swear a solemn oath not to harm me on our journey."

They all swore as she asked. Once the oath was made, the woman said, "Shhh! If any of you see me on the street or at the well, don't speak to me. If someone tells my master, he'll grow suspicious. Then I'll be thrown in prison, and you'll all be killed! So keep your mouths shut. Buy your goods as quickly as you can. And when you're finished loading, let me know. I'll bring as much gold as I can get my hands on. I can also do something else to help pay my share. I am the nurse of this house's kind master's son. He's a cute little boy, just starting to run around. I'll take him onto your ship. If you take him to another country and sell him, you'll earn a great deal of money."

The woman returned to the house. The Phoenicians stayed for a full year, loading their ship with precious goods. Once the ship was full, they sent a messenger to the woman. Their messenger was a very cunning fellow. He came to my father's house, carrying a gold necklace strung with amber beads. While my mother and the servants held the necklace, admiring it and bargaining over its price, he quietly signaled the woman. Then he returned to the ship. She took my hand and led me out of the house. In the front part of the house, I saw tables set with cups from guests who had been feasting with my father. These guests were all his attendants, but they had gone to a public assembly. The woman quickly snatched up three cups and hid them in her dress. I followed her, not knowing any better. The sun had already set, and darkness covered the land. We hurried as fast as we could until we reached the harbor where the Phoenician ship was anchored. Once we were on board, they sailed across the sea, taking us with them. Zeus sent a fair wind. We sailed for six days and nights. But on the seventh day, the goddess Artemis struck the woman. She crashed down into the ship's hold as if she were a seagull landing on the water. The crew threw her overboard to become food for the seals and fish, leaving me alone and filled with sorrow. Soon, the winds and waves carried the ship to Ithaca. There, Laertes gave some of his possessions for me. That is how I first came to see this land."

Odysseus replied, "Eumaeus, I listened to your sad story with great interest and pity. But Zeus has given you good things along with your misfortunes. Despite all your hardships, you have a good master. Thanks to him, you always have plenty to eat and drink, and you live a good life. Meanwhile, I am still forced to wander from city to city, begging for my meals."

The two men talked like this. They had little time left for sleep, as dawn was quickly approaching. Meanwhile, Telemachus and his crew were nearing land. They lowered the sails, took down the mast, and rowed their ship into the harbor. They dropped their anchors and tied the ropes securely. Then they came ashore, mixed their wine, and prepared dinner. Once they had eaten and drunk their fill, Telemachus said, "Take the ship on to the town. But I will stay here, for I want to check on the herdsmen at one of my farms. In the evening, after I've finished my business, I will go down to the city. And tomorrow morning, as a reward for your hard work, I will treat all of you to a hearty dinner with plenty of meat and wine."

Then Theoclymenus asked, "My young friend, what should I do? To whose house among your important people should I go? Or should I go straight to your own house and see your mother?"

Telemachus replied, "Any other time, I would have told you to go straight to my house. You would find no lack of hospitality there. But right now, it wouldn't be comfortable for you. I'll be away, and my mother won't see guests. She rarely even shows herself to the suitors, preferring to weave in an upstairs room to avoid them. However, I can tell you of a man whose house you can go to. That's Eurymachus, son of Polybus, whom everyone in Ithaca respects above all others. He is by far the best and most persistent suitor among those trying to marry my mother and take Odysseus's place. But only Zeus in heaven knows whether they will meet a miserable end before any marriage takes place."

As he spoke, a hawk, the messenger of the god Apollo, flew to his right. It clutched a dove in its talons, and as it tore at the bird, feathers drifted down to the ground between Telemachus and the ship. Theoclymenus then pulled Telemachus aside and took his hand. "Telemachus," Theoclymenus said, "that bird would not have flown to your right unless a god had sent it. As soon as I saw it, I knew it was a good omen. It means you will remain powerful, and there will be no house in Ithaca more royal than your own."

Telemachus replied, "I truly hope that proves to be true. If it does, I will show you immense favor and give you many gifts. Everyone who meets you will congratulate you."

Then he spoke to his friend Piraeus. "Piraeus, son of Clytius, of all those who accompanied me to Pylos, you have shown yourself the most willing to serve me. I would be grateful if you would take this stranger to your own house and treat him generously until I can come for him."

Piraeus replied, "Telemachus, you may stay away as long as you wish. I will look after him for you, and he will find no lack of hospitality."

As he spoke, Piraeus boarded the ship and told the others to do the same and untie the ropes. So they all took their places in the ship. But Telemachus put on his sandals and took a long, sturdy spear with a sharp bronze head from the ship's deck. Then they untied the ropes, pushed the ship away from the land, and headed towards the city as they had been instructed. Meanwhile, Telemachus strode on as fast as he could, until he reached the farmhouse where countless herds of pigs were grazing. This was the home of the excellent swineherd, who was so loyal to his master.
