001: CHAPTER II
002: 
003: JONATHAN HARKER’S JOURNAL--_continued_
004: 
005: 
006: _5 May._--I must have been asleep, for certainly if I had been fully
007: awake I must have noticed the approach of such a remarkable place. In
008: the gloom the courtyard looked of considerable size, and as several dark
009: ways led from it under great round arches, it perhaps seemed bigger than
010: it really is. I have not yet been able to see it by daylight.
011: 
012: When the calèche stopped, the driver jumped down and held out his hand
013: to assist me to alight. Again I could not but notice his prodigious
014: strength. His hand actually seemed like a steel vice that could have
015: crushed mine if he had chosen. Then he took out my traps, and placed
016: them on the ground beside me as I stood close to a great door, old and
017: studded with large iron nails, and set in a projecting doorway of
018: massive stone. I could see even in the dim light that the stone was
019: massively carved, but that the carving had been much worn by time and
020: weather. As I stood, the driver jumped again into his seat and shook the
021: reins; the horses started forward, and trap and all disappeared down one
022: of the dark openings.
023: 
024: I stood in silence where I was, for I did not know what to do. Of bell
025: or knocker there was no sign; through these frowning walls and dark
026: window openings it was not likely that my voice could penetrate. The
027: time I waited seemed endless, and I felt doubts and fears crowding upon
028: me. What sort of place had I come to, and among what kind of people?
029: What sort of grim adventure was it on which I had embarked? Was this a
030: customary incident in the life of a solicitor’s clerk sent out to
031: explain the purchase of a London estate to a foreigner? Solicitor’s
032: clerk! Mina would not like that. Solicitor--for just before leaving
033: London I got word that my examination was successful; and I am now a
034: full-blown solicitor! I began to rub my eyes and pinch myself to see if
035: I were awake. It all seemed like a horrible nightmare to me, and I
036: expected that I should suddenly awake, and find myself at home, with
037: the dawn struggling in through the windows, as I had now and again felt
038: in the morning after a day of overwork. But my flesh answered the
039: pinching test, and my eyes were not to be deceived. I was indeed awake
040: and among the Carpathians. All I could do now was to be patient, and to
041: wait the coming of the morning.
042: 
043: Just as I had come to this conclusion I heard a heavy step approaching
044: behind the great door, and saw through the chinks the gleam of a coming
045: light. Then there was the sound of rattling chains and the clanking of
046: massive bolts drawn back. A key was turned with the loud grating noise
047: of long disuse, and the great door swung back.
048: 
049: Within, stood a tall old man, clean shaven save for a long white
050: moustache, and clad in black from head to foot, without a single speck
051: of colour about him anywhere. He held in his hand an antique silver
052: lamp, in which the flame burned without chimney or globe of any kind,
053: throwing long quivering shadows as it flickered in the draught of the
054: open door. The old man motioned me in with his right hand with a courtly
055: gesture, saying in excellent English, but with a strange intonation:--
056: 
057: “Welcome to my house! Enter freely and of your own will!” He made no
058: motion of stepping to meet me, but stood like a statue, as though his
059: gesture of welcome had fixed him into stone. The instant, however, that
060: I had stepped over the threshold, he moved impulsively forward, and
061: holding out his hand grasped mine with a strength which made me wince,
062: an effect which was not lessened by the fact that it seemed as cold as
063: ice--more like the hand of a dead than a living man. Again he said:--
064: 
065: “Welcome to my house. Come freely. Go safely; and leave something of the
066: happiness you bring!” The strength of the handshake was so much akin to
067: that which I had noticed in the driver, whose face I had not seen, that
068: for a moment I doubted if it were not the same person to whom I was
069: speaking; so to make sure, I said interrogatively:--
070: 
071: “Count Dracula?” He bowed in a courtly way as he replied:--
072: 
073: “I am Dracula; and I bid you welcome, Mr. Harker, to my house. Come in;
074: the night air is chill, and you must need to eat and rest.” As he was
075: speaking, he put the lamp on a bracket on the wall, and stepping out,
076: took my luggage; he had carried it in before I could forestall him. I
077: protested but he insisted:--
078: 
079: “Nay, sir, you are my guest. It is late, and my people are not
080: available. Let me see to your comfort myself.” He insisted on carrying
081: my traps along the passage, and then up a great winding stair, and
082: along another great passage, on whose stone floor our steps rang
083: heavily. At the end of this he threw open a heavy door, and I rejoiced
084: to see within a well-lit room in which a table was spread for supper,
085: and on whose mighty hearth a great fire of logs, freshly replenished,
086: flamed and flared.
087: 
088: The Count halted, putting down my bags, closed the door, and crossing
089: the room, opened another door, which led into a small octagonal room lit
090: by a single lamp, and seemingly without a window of any sort. Passing
091: through this, he opened another door, and motioned me to enter. It was a
092: welcome sight; for here was a great bedroom well lighted and warmed with
093: another log fire,--also added to but lately, for the top logs were
094: fresh--which sent a hollow roar up the wide chimney. The Count himself
095: left my luggage inside and withdrew, saying, before he closed the
096: door:--
097: 
098: “You will need, after your journey, to refresh yourself by making your
099: toilet. I trust you will find all you wish. When you are ready, come
100: into the other room, where you will find your supper prepared.”
101: 
102: The light and warmth and the Count’s courteous welcome seemed to have
103: dissipated all my doubts and fears. Having then reached my normal state,
104: I discovered that I was half famished with hunger; so making a hasty
105: toilet, I went into the other room.
106: 
107: I found supper already laid out. My host, who stood on one side of the
108: great fireplace, leaning against the stonework, made a graceful wave of
109: his hand to the table, and said:--
110: 
111: “I pray you, be seated and sup how you please. You will, I trust, excuse
112: me that I do not join you; but I have dined already, and I do not sup.”
113: 
114: I handed to him the sealed letter which Mr. Hawkins had entrusted to me.
115: He opened it and read it gravely; then, with a charming smile, he handed
116: it to me to read. One passage of it, at least, gave me a thrill of
117: pleasure.
118: 
119: “I must regret that an attack of gout, from which malady I am a constant
120: sufferer, forbids absolutely any travelling on my part for some time to
121: come; but I am happy to say I can send a sufficient substitute, one in
122: whom I have every possible confidence. He is a young man, full of energy
123: and talent in his own way, and of a very faithful disposition. He is
124: discreet and silent, and has grown into manhood in my service. He shall
125: be ready to attend on you when you will during his stay, and shall take
126: your instructions in all matters.”
127: 
128: The Count himself came forward and took off the cover of a dish, and I
129: fell to at once on an excellent roast chicken. This, with some cheese
130: and a salad and a bottle of old Tokay, of which I had two glasses, was
131: my supper. During the time I was eating it the Count asked me many
132: questions as to my journey, and I told him by degrees all I had
133: experienced.
134: 
135: By this time I had finished my supper, and by my host’s desire had drawn
136: up a chair by the fire and begun to smoke a cigar which he offered me,
137: at the same time excusing himself that he did not smoke. I had now an
138: opportunity of observing him, and found him of a very marked
139: physiognomy.
140: 
141: His face was a strong--a very strong--aquiline, with high bridge of the
142: thin nose and peculiarly arched nostrils; with lofty domed forehead, and
143: hair growing scantily round the temples but profusely elsewhere. His
144: eyebrows were very massive, almost meeting over the nose, and with bushy
145: hair that seemed to curl in its own profusion. The mouth, so far as I
146: could see it under the heavy moustache, was fixed and rather
147: cruel-looking, with peculiarly sharp white teeth; these protruded over
148: the lips, whose remarkable ruddiness showed astonishing vitality in a
149: man of his years. For the rest, his ears were pale, and at the tops
150: extremely pointed; the chin was broad and strong, and the cheeks firm
151: though thin. The general effect was one of extraordinary pallor.
152: 
153: Hitherto I had noticed the backs of his hands as they lay on his knees
154: in the firelight, and they had seemed rather white and fine; but seeing
155: them now close to me, I could not but notice that they were rather
156: coarse--broad, with squat fingers. Strange to say, there were hairs in
157: the centre of the palm. The nails were long and fine, and cut to a sharp
158: point. As the Count leaned over me and his hands touched me, I could not
159: repress a shudder. It may have been that his breath was rank, but a
160: horrible feeling of nausea came over me, which, do what I would, I could
161: not conceal. The Count, evidently noticing it, drew back; and with a
162: grim sort of smile, which showed more than he had yet done his
163: protuberant teeth, sat himself down again on his own side of the
164: fireplace. We were both silent for a while; and as I looked towards the
165: window I saw the first dim streak of the coming dawn. There seemed a
166: strange stillness over everything; but as I listened I heard as if from
167: down below in the valley the howling of many wolves. The Count’s eyes
168: gleamed, and he said:--
169: 
170: “Listen to them--the children of the night. What music they make!”
171: Seeing, I suppose, some expression in my face strange to him, he
172: added:--
173: 
174: “Ah, sir, you dwellers in the city cannot enter into the feelings of the
175: hunter.” Then he rose and said:--
176: 
177: “But you must be tired. Your bedroom is all ready, and to-morrow you
178: shall sleep as late as you will. I have to be away till the afternoon;
179: so sleep well and dream well!” With a courteous bow, he opened for me
180: himself the door to the octagonal room, and I entered my bedroom....
181: 
182: I am all in a sea of wonders. I doubt; I fear; I think strange things,
183: which I dare not confess to my own soul. God keep me, if only for the
184: sake of those dear to me!
185: 
186:        *       *       *       *       *
187: 
188: _7 May._--It is again early morning, but I have rested and enjoyed the
189: last twenty-four hours. I slept till late in the day, and awoke of my
190: own accord. When I had dressed myself I went into the room where we had
191: supped, and found a cold breakfast laid out, with coffee kept hot by the
192: pot being placed on the hearth. There was a card on the table, on which
193: was written:--
194: 
195: “I have to be absent for a while. Do not wait for me.--D.” I set to and
196: enjoyed a hearty meal. When I had done, I looked for a bell, so that I
197: might let the servants know I had finished; but I could not find one.
198: There are certainly odd deficiencies in the house, considering the
199: extraordinary evidences of wealth which are round me. The table service
200: is of gold, and so beautifully wrought that it must be of immense value.
201: The curtains and upholstery of the chairs and sofas and the hangings of
202: my bed are of the costliest and most beautiful fabrics, and must have
203: been of fabulous value when they were made, for they are centuries old,
204: though in excellent order. I saw something like them in Hampton Court,
205: but there they were worn and frayed and moth-eaten. But still in none of
206: the rooms is there a mirror. There is not even a toilet glass on my
207: table, and I had to get the little shaving glass from my bag before I
208: could either shave or brush my hair. I have not yet seen a servant
209: anywhere, or heard a sound near the castle except the howling of wolves.
210: Some time after I had finished my meal--I do not know whether to call it
211: breakfast or dinner, for it was between five and six o’clock when I had
212: it--I looked about for something to read, for I did not like to go about
213: the castle until I had asked the Count’s permission. There was
214: absolutely nothing in the room, book, newspaper, or even writing
215: materials; so I opened another door in the room and found a sort of
216: library. The door opposite mine I tried, but found it locked.
217: 
218: In the library I found, to my great delight, a vast number of English
219: books, whole shelves full of them, and bound volumes of magazines and
220: newspapers. A table in the centre was littered with English magazines
221: and newspapers, though none of them were of very recent date. The books
222: were of the most varied kind--history, geography, politics, political
223: economy, botany, geology, law--all relating to England and English life
224: and customs and manners. There were even such books of reference as the
225: London Directory, the “Red” and “Blue” books, Whitaker’s Almanac, the
226: Army and Navy Lists, and--it somehow gladdened my heart to see it--the
227: Law List.
228: 
229: Whilst I was looking at the books, the door opened, and the Count
230: entered. He saluted me in a hearty way, and hoped that I had had a good
231: night’s rest. Then he went on:--
232: 
233: “I am glad you found your way in here, for I am sure there is much that
234: will interest you. These companions”--and he laid his hand on some of
235: the books--“have been good friends to me, and for some years past, ever
236: since I had the idea of going to London, have given me many, many hours
237: of pleasure. Through them I have come to know your great England; and to
238: know her is to love her. I long to go through the crowded streets of
239: your mighty London, to be in the midst of the whirl and rush of
240: humanity, to share its life, its change, its death, and all that makes
241: it what it is. But alas! as yet I only know your tongue through books.
242: To you, my friend, I look that I know it to speak.”
243: 
244: “But, Count,” I said, “you know and speak English thoroughly!” He bowed
245: gravely.
246: 
247: “I thank you, my friend, for your all too-flattering estimate, but yet I
248: fear that I am but a little way on the road I would travel. True, I know
249: the grammar and the words, but yet I know not how to speak them.”
250: 
251: “Indeed,” I said, “you speak excellently.”
252: 
253: “Not so,” he answered. “Well, I know that, did I move and speak in your
254: London, none there are who would not know me for a stranger. That is not
255: enough for me. Here I am noble; I am _boyar_; the common people know me,
256: and I am master. But a stranger in a strange land, he is no one; men
257: know him not--and to know not is to care not for. I am content if I am
258: like the rest, so that no man stops if he see me, or pause in his
259: speaking if he hear my words, ‘Ha, ha! a stranger!’ I have been so long
260: master that I would be master still--or at least that none other should
261: be master of me. You come to me not alone as agent of my friend Peter
262: Hawkins, of Exeter, to tell me all about my new estate in London. You
263: shall, I trust, rest here with me awhile, so that by our talking I may
264: learn the English intonation; and I would that you tell me when I make
265: error, even of the smallest, in my speaking. I am sorry that I had to be
266: away so long to-day; but you will, I know, forgive one who has so many
267: important affairs in hand.”
268: 
269: Of course I said all I could about being willing, and asked if I might
270: come into that room when I chose. He answered: “Yes, certainly,” and
271: added:--
272: 
273: “You may go anywhere you wish in the castle, except where the doors are
274: locked, where of course you will not wish to go. There is reason that
275: all things are as they are, and did you see with my eyes and know with
276: my knowledge, you would perhaps better understand.” I said I was sure of
277: this, and then he went on:--
278: 
279: “We are in Transylvania; and Transylvania is not England. Our ways are
280: not your ways, and there shall be to you many strange things. Nay, from
281: what you have told me of your experiences already, you know something of
282: what strange things there may be.”
283: 
284: This led to much conversation; and as it was evident that he wanted to
285: talk, if only for talking’s sake, I asked him many questions regarding
286: things that had already happened to me or come within my notice.
287: Sometimes he sheered off the subject, or turned the conversation by
288: pretending not to understand; but generally he answered all I asked most
289: frankly. Then as time went on, and I had got somewhat bolder, I asked
290: him of some of the strange things of the preceding night, as, for
291: instance, why the coachman went to the places where he had seen the blue
292: flames. He then explained to me that it was commonly believed that on a
293: certain night of the year--last night, in fact, when all evil spirits
294: are supposed to have unchecked sway--a blue flame is seen over any place
295: where treasure has been concealed. “That treasure has been hidden,” he
296: went on, “in the region through which you came last night, there can be
297: but little doubt; for it was the ground fought over for centuries by the
298: Wallachian, the Saxon, and the Turk. Why, there is hardly a foot of soil
299: in all this region that has not been enriched by the blood of men,
300: patriots or invaders. In old days there were stirring times, when the
301: Austrian and the Hungarian came up in hordes, and the patriots went out
302: to meet them--men and women, the aged and the children too--and waited
303: their coming on the rocks above the passes, that they might sweep
304: destruction on them with their artificial avalanches. When the invader
305: was triumphant he found but little, for whatever there was had been
306: sheltered in the friendly soil.”
307: 
308: “But how,” said I, “can it have remained so long undiscovered, when
309: there is a sure index to it if men will but take the trouble to look?”
310: The Count smiled, and as his lips ran back over his gums, the long,
311: sharp, canine teeth showed out strangely; he answered:--
312: 
313: “Because your peasant is at heart a coward and a fool! Those flames only
314: appear on one night; and on that night no man of this land will, if he
315: can help it, stir without his doors. And, dear sir, even if he did he
316: would not know what to do. Why, even the peasant that you tell me of who
317: marked the place of the flame would not know where to look in daylight
318: even for his own work. Even you would not, I dare be sworn, be able to
319: find these places again?”
320: 
321: “There you are right,” I said. “I know no more than the dead where even
322: to look for them.” Then we drifted into other matters.
323: 
324: “Come,” he said at last, “tell me of London and of the house which you
325: have procured for me.” With an apology for my remissness, I went into my
326: own room to get the papers from my bag. Whilst I was placing them in
327: order I heard a rattling of china and silver in the next room, and as I
328: passed through, noticed that the table had been cleared and the lamp
329: lit, for it was by this time deep into the dark. The lamps were also lit
330: in the study or library, and I found the Count lying on the sofa,
331: reading, of all things in the world, an English Bradshaw’s Guide. When I
332: came in he cleared the books and papers from the table; and with him I
333: went into plans and deeds and figures of all sorts. He was interested in
334: everything, and asked me a myriad questions about the place and its
335: surroundings. He clearly had studied beforehand all he could get on the
336: subject of the neighbourhood, for he evidently at the end knew very much
337: more than I did. When I remarked this, he answered:--
338: 
339: “Well, but, my friend, is it not needful that I should? When I go there
340: I shall be all alone, and my friend Harker Jonathan--nay, pardon me, I
341: fall into my country’s habit of putting your patronymic first--my friend
342: Jonathan Harker will not be by my side to correct and aid me. He will be
343: in Exeter, miles away, probably working at papers of the law with my
344: other friend, Peter Hawkins. So!”
345: 
346: We went thoroughly into the business of the purchase of the estate at
347: Purfleet. When I had told him the facts and got his signature to the
348: necessary papers, and had written a letter with them ready to post to
349: Mr. Hawkins, he began to ask me how I had come across so suitable a
350: place. I read to him the notes which I had made at the time, and which I
351: inscribe here:--
352: 
353: “At Purfleet, on a by-road, I came across just such a place as seemed to
354: be required, and where was displayed a dilapidated notice that the place
355: was for sale. It is surrounded by a high wall, of ancient structure,
356: built of heavy stones, and has not been repaired for a large number of
357: years. The closed gates are of heavy old oak and iron, all eaten with
358: rust.
359: 
360: “The estate is called Carfax, no doubt a corruption of the old _Quatre
361: Face_, as the house is four-sided, agreeing with the cardinal points of
362: the compass. It contains in all some twenty acres, quite surrounded by
363: the solid stone wall above mentioned. There are many trees on it, which
364: make it in places gloomy, and there is a deep, dark-looking pond or
365: small lake, evidently fed by some springs, as the water is clear and
366: flows away in a fair-sized stream. The house is very large and of all
367: periods back, I should say, to mediæval times, for one part is of stone
368: immensely thick, with only a few windows high up and heavily barred with
369: iron. It looks like part of a keep, and is close to an old chapel or
370: church. I could not enter it, as I had not the key of the door leading
371: to it from the house, but I have taken with my kodak views of it from
372: various points. The house has been added to, but in a very straggling
373: way, and I can only guess at the amount of ground it covers, which must
374: be very great. There are but few houses close at hand, one being a very
375: large house only recently added to and formed into a private lunatic
376: asylum. It is not, however, visible from the grounds.”
377: 
378: When I had finished, he said:--
379: 
380: “I am glad that it is old and big. I myself am of an old family, and to
381: live in a new house would kill me. A house cannot be made habitable in a
382: day; and, after all, how few days go to make up a century. I rejoice
383: also that there is a chapel of old times. We Transylvanian nobles love
384: not to think that our bones may lie amongst the common dead. I seek not
385: gaiety nor mirth, not the bright voluptuousness of much sunshine and
386: sparkling waters which please the young and gay. I am no longer young;
387: and my heart, through weary years of mourning over the dead, is not
388: attuned to mirth. Moreover, the walls of my castle are broken; the
389: shadows are many, and the wind breathes cold through the broken
390: battlements and casements. I love the shade and the shadow, and would
391: be alone with my thoughts when I may.” Somehow his words and his look
392: did not seem to accord, or else it was that his cast of face made his
393: smile look malignant and saturnine.
394: 
395: Presently, with an excuse, he left me, asking me to put all my papers
396: together. He was some little time away, and I began to look at some of
397: the books around me. One was an atlas, which I found opened naturally at
398: England, as if that map had been much used. On looking at it I found in
399: certain places little rings marked, and on examining these I noticed
400: that one was near London on the east side, manifestly where his new
401: estate was situated; the other two were Exeter, and Whitby on the
402: Yorkshire coast.
403: 
404: It was the better part of an hour when the Count returned. “Aha!” he
405: said; “still at your books? Good! But you must not work always. Come; I
406: am informed that your supper is ready.” He took my arm, and we went into
407: the next room, where I found an excellent supper ready on the table. The
408: Count again excused himself, as he had dined out on his being away from
409: home. But he sat as on the previous night, and chatted whilst I ate.
410: After supper I smoked, as on the last evening, and the Count stayed with
411: me, chatting and asking questions on every conceivable subject, hour
412: after hour. I felt that it was getting very late indeed, but I did not
413: say anything, for I felt under obligation to meet my host’s wishes in
414: every way. I was not sleepy, as the long sleep yesterday had fortified
415: me; but I could not help experiencing that chill which comes over one at
416: the coming of the dawn, which is like, in its way, the turn of the tide.
417: They say that people who are near death die generally at the change to
418: the dawn or at the turn of the tide; any one who has when tired, and
419: tied as it were to his post, experienced this change in the atmosphere
420: can well believe it. All at once we heard the crow of a cock coming up
421: with preternatural shrillness through the clear morning air; Count
422: Dracula, jumping to his feet, said:--
423: 
424: “Why, there is the morning again! How remiss I am to let you stay up so
425: long. You must make your conversation regarding my dear new country of
426: England less interesting, so that I may not forget how time flies by
427: us,” and, with a courtly bow, he quickly left me.
428: 
429: I went into my own room and drew the curtains, but there was little to
430: notice; my window opened into the courtyard, all I could see was the
431: warm grey of quickening sky. So I pulled the curtains again, and have
432: written of this day.
433: 
434:        *       *       *       *       *
435: 
436: _8 May._--I began to fear as I wrote in this book that I was getting too
437: diffuse; but now I am glad that I went into detail from the first, for
438: there is something so strange about this place and all in it that I
439: cannot but feel uneasy. I wish I were safe out of it, or that I had
440: never come. It may be that this strange night-existence is telling on
441: me; but would that that were all! If there were any one to talk to I
442: could bear it, but there is no one. I have only the Count to speak with,
443: and he!--I fear I am myself the only living soul within the place. Let
444: me be prosaic so far as facts can be; it will help me to bear up, and
445: imagination must not run riot with me. If it does I am lost. Let me say
446: at once how I stand--or seem to.
447: 
448: I only slept a few hours when I went to bed, and feeling that I could
449: not sleep any more, got up. I had hung my shaving glass by the window,
450: and was just beginning to shave. Suddenly I felt a hand on my shoulder,
451: and heard the Count’s voice saying to me, “Good-morning.” I started, for
452: it amazed me that I had not seen him, since the reflection of the glass
453: covered the whole room behind me. In starting I had cut myself slightly,
454: but did not notice it at the moment. Having answered the Count’s
455: salutation, I turned to the glass again to see how I had been mistaken.
456: This time there could be no error, for the man was close to me, and I
457: could see him over my shoulder. But there was no reflection of him in
458: the mirror! The whole room behind me was displayed; but there was no
459: sign of a man in it, except myself. This was startling, and, coming on
460: the top of so many strange things, was beginning to increase that vague
461: feeling of uneasiness which I always have when the Count is near; but at
462: the instant I saw that the cut had bled a little, and the blood was
463: trickling over my chin. I laid down the razor, turning as I did so half
464: round to look for some sticking plaster. When the Count saw my face, his
465: eyes blazed with a sort of demoniac fury, and he suddenly made a grab at
466: my throat. I drew away, and his hand touched the string of beads which
467: held the crucifix. It made an instant change in him, for the fury passed
468: so quickly that I could hardly believe that it was ever there.

480: When I went into the dining-room, breakfast was prepared; but I could
481: not find the Count anywhere. So I breakfasted alone. It is strange that
482: as yet I have not seen the Count eat or drink. He must be a very
483: peculiar man! After breakfast I did a little exploring in the castle. I
484: went out on the stairs, and found a room looking towards the South. The
485: view was magnificent, and from where I stood there was every opportunity
486: of seeing it. The castle is on the very edge of a terrible precipice. A
487: stone falling from the window would fall a thousand feet without
488: touching anything! As far as the eye can reach is a sea of green tree
489: tops, with occasionally a deep rift where there is a chasm. Here and
490: there are silver threads where the rivers wind in deep gorges through
491: the forests.
492: 
493: But I am not in heart to describe beauty, for when I had seen the view I
494: explored further; doors, doors, doors everywhere, and all locked and
495: bolted. In no place save from the windows in the castle walls is there
496: an available exit.

498: The castle is a veritable prison, and I am a prisoner!
